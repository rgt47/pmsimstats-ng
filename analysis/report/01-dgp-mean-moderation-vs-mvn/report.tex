\section*{Abstract}\label{abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

\textbf{Background.} Validating a predictive biomarker requires an
interaction test, which needs about four times the sample of a
main-effect test (Brookes et al.~2004). Aggregated N-of-1 designs are
proposed to afford it,
and their power must be estimated by simulation. Hendrickson et al.
(2020) proposed a simulation method for this problem. The baseline
biomarker and three latent response components at every visit are
drawn from one multivariate normal distribution assembled from each
participant's exposure state. The predictive biomarker is a
correlation with the drug response that is present on drug and absent
off drug. We extend that data-generating process (DGP) for the Hybrid
design with carryover and judge each extension by five criteria.

\textbf{Methods.} We derive the closed-form moments and power of a
paired-difference interaction statistic under the original DGP and
the extensions. We derive a Schur-complement bound on the feasible
interaction strength. We verify them by Monte Carlo simulation,
including runs of the original code, and test fourteen analyses
against a prognostic biomarker.

\textbf{Results.} The original DGP holds the biomarker-response correlation
constant after first exposure. With any carryover the interaction is
then absent and power equals the test size. We extended the DGP so the
correlation decays at the carryover half-life. Power then returns to
its no-carryover value at half-lives of 0.1 to 0.2 week and declines
monotonically at longer ones. In the original code it raised power at
0.1 week from 0.13 to 0.64, against 0.67 without carryover. Under the
original compound-symmetry structure no joint distribution exists for
correlations above 0.256, so the original effect size of 0.6 relied
on repaired matrices. A separable AR(1) structure at serial
correlation 0.7 raises the bound to 0.474. A prognostic
biomarker with a pathway absent at baseline makes analyses that share
the biomarker effect with baseline anticonservative.

\textbf{Conclusions.} The correlation encoding of the interaction is
coherent. It needs a decaying correlation, a correlation structure
with room for realistic effects, and a feasibility check that stops
instead of repairing. We give the closed-form results, the code
changes, the extended DGP and planning sample sizes.

\textbf{Trial registration.} Not applicable.

\textbf{Keywords:} N-of-1 trials; predictive biomarker; simulation;
carryover; data-generating process; power; prazosin.

\section{Background}\label{background}

Validating a predictive biomarker requires a biomarker-by-treatment
interaction test. Such a test needs roughly four times the sample of a
main-effect test of the same size (\citeproc{ref-brookes2004}{1},\citeproc{ref-sargent2005}{2}).
Aggregated N-of-1 designs are proposed as the way to afford it. Their
power must be estimated by simulation. Hendrickson et al.
(\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{3}) proposed a simulation method for this problem. To
our knowledge it has no precedent in the N-of-1 literature. The
baseline biomarker and, at every visit, three latent response
components are drawn from one multivariate normal distribution. The
trial design assembles the correlation matrix from each participant's
exposure state. The predictive biomarker is a correlation with the
drug response that is present on drug and absent off drug. We call
this process the original DGP. This paper extends it for the Hybrid
design, which combines open-label titration, blinded discontinuation
and a brief crossover, with carryover between phases. The extensions
address two limits of the original DGP. Under any carryover the
original DGP has no interaction to detect. Its largest effect size has
no valid joint distribution. The paper adds a correlation that decays
after discontinuation and a feasibility check. It decides the elements
the original DGP left open. It ends with the extended DGP and the
sample sizes it implies.

\subsection{Aggregated N-of-1 trials and predictive biomarkers}\label{aggregated-n-of-1-trials-and-predictive-biomarkers}

In an aggregated N-of-1 trial each participant moves between drug and
placebo, or between drug and no drug, several times. The individual
series are analyzed together (\citeproc{ref-nikles2011}{4}--\citeproc{ref-punja2016}{6}).
Every participant contributes both an on-drug and an off-drug
response. The design can therefore estimate how the drug effect varies
between people. It can then test whether a baseline measurement
predicts who benefits (\citeproc{ref-senn2016mastering}{7},\citeproc{ref-schork2015}{8}). The motivating
example is prazosin for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Higher
pretreatment standing systolic blood pressure was associated with
greater symptom reduction on prazosin but not on placebo
(\citeproc{ref-raskind2016bp}{9}). Blood pressure is therefore a candidate predictive
biomarker (\citeproc{ref-raskind2003}{10}--\citeproc{ref-raskind2018}{12}).

Closed-form power calculations exist only for simplified versions of
these designs (\citeproc{ref-wang2019}{13},\citeproc{ref-senn2002}{14}). Power is therefore estimated by
simulating trials from a data-generating process (DGP) and analyzing
each simulated trial (\citeproc{ref-morris2019simulation}{15}). Several simulators of
N-of-1 and aggregated N-of-1 data exist. Percha et al. (\citeproc{ref-percha2019}{16})
simulate single series with exponential wash-in and wash-out to choose
block lengths and sampling rates. Chen and Chen (\citeproc{ref-chen2014}{17}) generate
cycles under compound symmetry or AR(1) with a carryover effect to
compare analyses. Blackston et al. (\citeproc{ref-blackston2019}{18}) and Gärtner et al.
(\citeproc{ref-gartner2023}{19}) simulate aggregated series with carryover to compare
designs and estimators. None of them represents a baseline biomarker
that modifies the drug response. The DGP determines what a simulation
can show: how the biomarker interaction is represented, how the drug
effect builds and wanes, and how carryover from one period reaches the
next.

\subsection{The original DGP}\label{the-original-dgp}

The original DGP has three parts. The first is a component
decomposition of the symptom score. The score is the baseline level
minus three responses. Each response follows a modified Gompertz
curve. The drug-response component (BR) is driven by time since the
current on-drug period began. The placebo-response component (PB) is
driven by time in the trial and scaled by the participant's
expectancy, the probability of receiving drug given the design. The
natural-history component (TV) is driven by time in the trial. The
second part is the design specification for open-label, crossover,
blinded-discontinuation and Hybrid designs. It tracks time on drug,
time since discontinuation and expectancy at each visit. The third
part is open software (the \texttt{pmsimstats} R package).

The original DGP also encodes the predictive biomarker in a
distinctive way. Simulation studies of N-of-1 and crossover designs
usually represent treatment-effect heterogeneity as random
participant-by-treatment variation
(\citeproc{ref-senn2016mastering}{7},\citeproc{ref-araujo2016}{20}--\citeproc{ref-zucker1997}{22}).
The original DGP instead places the biomarker in the same multivariate
normal distribution as the response components. The biomarker has a
nonzero correlation \(c_{bm}\) with the drug response at on-drug visits.
Without carryover it has zero correlation elsewhere. We call \(c_{bm}\)
the biomarker-response correlation. A predictive biomarker is then one
whose association with the outcome differs between on-drug and
off-drug visits. The parameterization keeps the marginal distribution
of the drug response fixed. It expresses the strength of the
interaction as a correlation. We call this the correlation encoding of
the interaction. The priority claim above rests on a PubMed search on
2026-10-27. The search was for N-of-1, single-patient or aggregated
N-of-1 trials with simulation, a biomarker or effect modifier, and
power or sample size. It returned one record, the original study.

\subsection{Why the joint distribution is built from the design}\label{why-the-joint-distribution-is-built-from-the-design}

The simplest DGP that could serve would generate one symptom score per
visit. Its mean would carry the design effects and an interaction term
\(\beta\, B_i D_{it}\). Its residual would be compound symmetric or
AR(1). Most simulators of crossover and N-of-1 data use that form
(\citeproc{ref-wang2019}{13},\citeproc{ref-chen2014}{17},\citeproc{ref-senn2019samplesize}{21}), and the analysis model
assumes it. It is adequate when the question is the main effect. It is
not adequate for the questions this series asks, for four reasons.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Carryover would be whatever the analyst specifies.} In the
  simple form carryover is a mean shift added at off-drug visits.
  Whether the design still identifies the interaction under carryover
  depends on how the biomarker's association with the response
  persists after discontinuation. The simple form has no such
  association, so the question cannot be studied.
\item
  \textbf{The interaction would be only a conditional mean effect.} With
  a mean term the residual variance of the drug response is the same
  at every biomarker value. A predictive biomarker that explains part
  of the between-person variation in drug response implies a
  responder class in the population. That implies a correlation
  between the biomarker and the drug response. The simple form has no
  parameter for it (Appendix A.6).
\item
  \textbf{A prognostic pathway could not be generated.} A biomarker may
  also be associated with the placebo response or with the natural
  history of the condition. Such a biomarker enters the observed
  outcome through a pathway the analysis cannot see. Whether an
  analysis keeps its nominal size under that feature can only be
  tested by a DGP that generates the pathways separately.
\item
  \textbf{Existence and identifiability would be invisible.} In the simple
  form every parameter combination yields a valid distribution. The
  design enters only through the regressor pattern. Whether the
  simulated effect sizes are jointly possible, and how much of the
  interaction the design can see, are questions about the joint
  distribution of the biomarker and the response components. They
  have closed-form answers only when that distribution is specified.
\end{enumerate}

The original DGP specifies it. The biomarker and the three response
components at every visit form one multivariate normal vector. The
design assembles its correlation matrix. The biomarker is correlated
with the drug response at on-drug visits, and at a value set by the
exposure state at off-drug visits. The components are serially
correlated in calendar weeks. The components are cross-correlated
through a common block. Three results follow. The largest feasible
interaction is a property of the design, not a number found by trial
and error. We call it the feasibility bound. The power of a
paired-difference statistic has a closed form. The design enters it
through the on/off correlation difference, the on-drug correlation
minus the mean off-drug correlation. The residual structure the
analysis model should assume is derived from the DGP rather than
asserted. Three requirements come with this form. The marginal
covariance of the outcome is implied rather than chosen. The joint
distribution must exist. Every entry must be calibrated or declared
uncalibrated. The Discussion shows that, conditional on the biomarker,
the correlation encoding reduces to the mean-term form with a smaller
residual variance. Nothing standard is lost.

The DGP is not new in its parts. Simulating repeated measures from a
multivariate normal with compound symmetry or AR(1) and a carryover
term is routine (\citeproc{ref-wang2019}{13},\citeproc{ref-chen2014}{17}). Decomposing a clinical
trajectory into disease progression, placebo response and drug action
is the pharmacometric convention (\citeproc{ref-holford2015}{23}). Separable (Kronecker)
covariances for several response variables measured repeatedly are an
established model (\citeproc{ref-galecki1994}{24}). The joint correlation structure of
an effect modifier and an outcome has been used to design
heterogeneity analyses in cluster randomized trials (\citeproc{ref-li2023}{25}). The
original DGP combined these parts. It placed a baseline variable in
the joint distribution of the latent response components, with a
correlation indexed by each visit's exposure state. This paper extends
that combination. We found no other instance of it in the searches
reported above.

\subsection{What a data-generating process for these trials must satisfy}\label{what-a-data-generating-process-for-these-trials-must-satisfy}

Reusing the original DGP for a series of simulation studies showed
where it could be extended. To judge an extension, a criterion for
``better'' is needed. We use five. Each stage of the Results ends with
the choice made and the criterion it rests on.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Existence.} The joint distribution must exist for the effect
  sizes a study simulates. The correlation matrix must be positive
  definite. We measure this by the largest feasible \(c_{bm}\) (the
  feasibility bound) and by the number of matrices a grid would need
  to repair.
\item
  \textbf{Identifiability under carryover.} The biomarker-by-treatment
  interaction must remain a contrast between on-drug and off-drug
  visits when carryover is present. We measure this by the on/off
  correlation difference. The closed form shows that this difference
  governs power.
\item
  \textbf{Fidelity to the pharmacology.} The time course of the drug
  effect, its decay after discontinuation and its behavior at
  re-exposure should be consistent with a short-half-life, titrated
  drug such as prazosin. This is a judgment. We state it as such
  where it decides a stage.
\item
  \textbf{Analyzability.} The residual structure the DGP generates should
  be one the analysis model can represent. The DGP should also be
  able to generate the features that decide which analyses are valid,
  such as a prognostic biomarker. We measure this by the agreement of
  the generated residual structure with the analysis model's, and by
  null rejection rates under those features.
\item
  \textbf{Calibration status.} Every parameter should be labeled as the
  original value, a choice made here, or uncalibrated. No parameter
  of the DGP has yet been estimated from prazosin trial data. This
  criterion is therefore reported, not met.
\end{enumerate}

A DGP meeting criteria 1 to 4, with criterion 5 reported, is what we
call the extended DGP. Its label in the repository's code and data is
\texttt{refined}.

\textbf{The reference drug.} Prazosin's plasma half-life is about 2.3 hours
(\citeproc{ref-hobbs1978}{26}), against weekly visits. The plasma concentration
therefore falls by orders of magnitude within a day of the last dose.
We assume a symptom-level half-life of 0.1 to 0.2 week (17 to 34
hours), or 7 to 15 plasma half-lives, as the pharmacological range.
This is an assumption, not a measurement. No symptom-response
half-life for prazosin in PTSD has been reported. The Results
therefore cover 0 to 2 weeks and label half-lives of 0.5 week and
above as behavioral scenarios. The drug is re-titrated after an
interruption, which bears on how the DGP treats re-exposure (Stage 3).

\subsection{Aims}\label{aims}

This paper extends the original DGP and judges each extension against
the five criteria. The Hybrid design is the primary case. The
open-label, blinded-discontinuation and crossover designs are
comparators. The paper shows in closed form why power equals the test
size under the original DGP when carryover is present, and why its
joint distribution often does not exist. It gives the changes to the
original code that add a decaying correlation and a feasibility check.
It chooses, with reasons, the elements the original DGP leaves open.
It collects the result as a reference specification with the stage
that justifies each element. It gives the sample sizes a trialist
needs to plan an aggregated N-of-1 trial under it.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

The Methods describe the original DGP and the tools used to judge each
extension: the estimands, a statistic with a closed-form distribution,
the feasibility bound and the simulation studies. Every choice about
the DGP is made in the Results.

\subsection{The original data-generating process}\label{the-original-data-generating-process}

We use the original DGP's notation, with a few additions. Participant
\(i\) is observed at a baseline visit \(t = 0\) and at post-baseline visits
\(t = 1, \ldots, T\), at calendar weeks \(w_t\) (4, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 16
and 20 in the Hybrid design). The symptom score is

\[
Y_{it} = \mathrm{BL}_i - \left(TV_{it} + PB_{it} + BR_{it}\right),
\]

so a larger response lowers the score. Each component has a mean given
by the modified Gompertz curve

\[
G(x;\, m, d, r) = m\,\frac{\exp(-d e^{-rx}) - e^{-d}}{1 - e^{-d}},
\]

which the code obtains by subtracting the offset \(m e^{-d}\) from
\(m\exp(-d e^{-rx})\) and rescaling, so that \(G(0) = 0\) and
\(G(\infty) = m\). It is evaluated at a component-specific clock:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The drug-response component (BR) at time on drug \(t_{od}\), the time
  since the current on-drug period began, with \(m = 10.99\), \(d = 5\),
  \(r = 0.42\) and SD 8. The curve reaches its inflection at 3.8 weeks,
  half its maximum at 4.7 weeks and 90\% at 9.2 weeks.
\item
  The placebo-response component (PB) at time in the trial, scaled by
  the expectancy \(\eta_t\) (1 in the open-label phase, 0.5 when
  blinded), with \(m = 6.51\), \(d = 5\), \(r = 0.35\) and SD \(10\eta_t\).
\item
  The natural-history component (TV) at time in the trial, with the
  same curve as PB and SD 10.
\end{itemize}

Off drug, the drug-response mean decays from its previous value with
half-life \(t_{1/2}\):
\(\mu_{BR,t} = \mu_{BR,t-1}\, 2^{-t_{sd,t}/t_{1/2}}\), where \(t_{sd}\) is
the time since discontinuation. We call this the original carryover
rule. When the drug is resumed, time on drug restarts at zero.

The vector (biomarker, baseline, \(TV_{i\cdot}\), \(PB_{i\cdot}\),
\(BR_{i\cdot}\)) is multivariate normal. Within each component the
correlation between any two visits is \(\rho_{cs} = 0.8\) (compound
symmetry). We write \(\rho_{cs}\) to keep \(\rho\) for the AR(1)
correlation per week. Between components the correlation is
\(c_1 = 0.2\) at the same visit and \(c_\times = 0.1\) otherwise. The
biomarker's correlation with \(BR_{it}\) is \(c_{bm}\,g_t\), where \(g_t\)
is the correlation profile, the fraction of \(c_{bm}\) present at visit
\(t\). The original rule sets \(g_t = 1\) wherever the drug-response mean
is nonzero and 0 elsewhere. The correlation is therefore constant
after first exposure. When the correlation matrix is not positive
definite, the original code replaces it with a nearby valid matrix
(\texttt{corpcor::make.positive.definite}, an approximation to the nearest
correlation matrix (\citeproc{ref-higham2002}{27})). No warning is issued.

The original analysis is the linear mixed model

\[
Y_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_B B_i + \beta_D D_{it} + \beta_T w_t
  + \beta_{bm:D} B_i D_{it} + u_i + \varepsilon_{it},
\]

with a random intercept \(u_i\), the binary drug indicator \(D_{it}\), and
baseline included as a row with \(D_{i0} = 0\). The interaction
\(\beta_{bm:D}\) is tested with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom
(\citeproc{ref-kuznetsova2017}{28}). Participants are assigned to the design's paths in
fixed numbers (18, 18, 17 and 17 in the Hybrid design at \(N = 70\)),
not at random.

\subsection{Estimands}\label{estimands}

The target is the on-drug biomarker moderation of the drug response.
This is the change in the drug effect per unit of biomarker while the
drug is fully present. Without carryover it equals
\(\beta_{bm:D} = -c_{bm}\sigma_{BR}/\sigma_{bm}\).

Analyses that contrast on-drug with off-drug visits through a binary
drug indicator estimate this quantity only when no moderation remains
off drug. Under carryover they estimate an attenuated on-minus-off
contrast. Analyses with an exposure-decayed indicator \(D_{bc,it}\) at
the true half-life recover the on-drug moderation. Table
\ref{tab:estimands} states which analysis in this paper targets which
quantity. The three sets of results are compared only where their
targets coincide (no carryover) or where the attenuation is the object
of study (Stage 2).

\subsection{The paired-difference statistic}\label{the-paired-difference-statistic}

To isolate how carryover acts, we use a summary statistic whose
distribution can be derived exactly (Appendix A). For each participant,

\[
\Delta_i = \bar Y_i^{\text{on}} - \bar Y_i^{\text{off}}
  = \sum_t a_t Y_{it},
\]

the difference between the mean on-drug and mean off-drug scores. It
is regressed on \(B_i\) by least squares. We call it E9 when it uses
post-baseline visits only. We call it E9b when baseline is counted as
one more off-drug visit, as in the original analysis. In the Hybrid
design, Monte Carlo simulation of the original mixed model shows that
E9b reproduces its mean estimates to within 0.004. It reproduces its
power closely (0.654 against 0.632 without carryover). In the
crossover design the closed form understates the mixed model's power
(0.645 against 0.708 without carryover, and 0.279 against 0.31 to 0.35
under the original rule). The mixed model's time term uses the
between-path contrast that the paired difference discards. The
derivation gives three results.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Proposition 1.} The variance of \(\Delta\) does not depend on the
  carryover half-life. The half-life enters only through the response
  means and the correlation profile. Neither appears in the variance.
\item
  \textbf{Proposition 2.} The population slope is
  \[
  \beta_{bm:D}^{\text{E9}}
    = -c_{bm}\frac{\sigma_{BR}}{\sigma_{bm}}\left(1 - \bar g_{\text{off}}\right),
  \qquad
  \beta_{bm:D}^{\text{E9b}}
    = -c_{bm}\frac{\sigma_{BR}}{\sigma_{bm}}
      \left(1 - \frac{n_{\text{off}}}{n_{\text{off}} + 1}\,
      \bar g_{\text{off}}\right),
  \]
  where \(\bar g_{\text{off}}\) is the mean of \(g_t\) over the
  \(n_{\text{off}}\) post-baseline off-drug visits. The slopes are
  specific to each path. In the Hybrid design \(n_{\text{off}}\) is 3 in
  two paths and 4 in the other two. The pooled slope is their
  allocation-weighted average.
\item
  \textbf{Power} is a noncentral-\(t\) probability governed by the correlation
  \(r_{\Delta B}\) between \(\Delta\) and \(B\). For E9 that correlation is
  proportional to the on/off correlation difference
  \(\mathcal G = 1 - \bar g_{\text{off}}\). For E9b it is proportional to
  \(1 - \frac{n_{\text{off}}}{n_{\text{off}}+1}\bar g_{\text{off}}\).
\end{itemize}

So carryover can affect power only through the on/off correlation
difference. The propositions reduce the question of why power falls
to the question of what the correlation rule does to
\(\bar g_{\text{off}}\).

\subsection{The feasibility bound}\label{the-feasibility-bound}

Write the response block of the correlation matrix as \(M\) and the
biomarker's correlations with the response components as
\(c_{bm}\tilde g\), with \(\tilde g\) the correlation profile padded with
zeros at the \(TV\) and \(PB\) entries. The full matrix is positive
definite if and only if \(M\) is, and

\[
c_{bm}^2\, \tilde g^\top M^{-1} \tilde g < 1 .
\]

The left side is the squared multiple correlation of the biomarker on
the whole response trajectory. The largest feasible \(c_{bm}\), the
feasibility bound, is therefore
\(c_{bm}^{\ast} = (\tilde g^\top M^{-1} \tilde g)^{-1/2}\) (Appendix A.4).
It depends on the correlation structure, the design and the
correlation profile. It is a bound on the DGP, not on any analysis.

\textbf{Number of replicates.} Replicate counts were set by the precision
each comparison needs. In study 3, 1,000 null replicates give a
two-MCSE band of 0.036 to 0.064 for a single cell. Pooling 11
non-prognostic null cells gives the band of 0.046 to 0.054 used in
Stage 6. In study 1, a cell is flagged when the simulated and
closed-form values differ by more than 2 MCSE. Chance alone produces
this in about 4.6\% of cells. The 278 cell-variants are not
independent, since each uses its own draws but the same designs.

\textbf{Random numbers.} Every cell, or chunk of a cell, sets its own seed
from a fixed rule: a function of the cell's parameters in study 1, a
shared seed per half-life (study 2a) or per design, sample size and
half-life (study 2b), and \(20261007 + 1000 \times \text{cell} +
\text{chunk}\) in study 3. Results therefore do not depend on the
number of cores or the order in which chunks run. Both versions of the
original code in study 2 use the same seeds. All analyses in study 3
are fitted to the same trials. The comparisons are therefore paired.
R's default Mersenne-Twister generator was used throughout.

an error was recorded as missing. Failures numbered 0 to 12 per
analysis over 28,000 trials, and at most 0.6\% in any cell. In study 2
the original pipeline returned an estimate for every replicate. No
correlation matrix was repaired in either version, because
\(c_{bm} = 0.25\) lies below the compound-symmetry feasibility bound of
0.256. Study 1 does not fit models for the paired-difference
statistic, so it has no failures.

calculations build the cross-component correlation as
\(c_1\rho^{\lvert w_t - w_s\rvert}\) (the separable form) where the
separable structure is named, and as the original constants where
compound symmetry is named. Study 2 is the original code and so the
original structure. Study 3's driver builds the separable form with
fixed path allocation (18, 18, 17, 17) and the original carryover rule
on the mean. The tables reported here are from that run. A rerun with
carryover decaying from the last on-drug mean and randomized
allocation changed no null rejection rate beyond Monte Carlo error and
no prognostic verdict (Appendix B.5).

\textbf{Software.} R 4.6.1, with lme4 2.0.1 and lmerTest 3.2.1 for the
original pipeline, nlme 3.1-169, mmrm 0.3.17, clubSandwich 0.7.0 and
MASS 7.3-65.

\textbf{Exploratory status.} The stress test is exploratory. Its cells and
analyses were written down as a working protocol in \texttt{docs/46} of the
repository. No analysis plan was fixed in advance, and the protocol
was revised as results came in. Two cells with a prognostic
association between the biomarker and baseline severity, and three
analyses, were added after the first results. Failed fits were first
counted as non-rejections and are now excluded from the denominator,
a change of at most 0.0025 in any rate. The study also compared the
analyses' power and selected one by a decision rule. That selection
was revised after results, is post hoc, and is reported in the
project's documentation (\texttt{docs/56}), not here. This paper uses the
stress test only for what it shows about the DGP: which analyses are
valid under a prognostic biomarker, and what the optional features do
to size and power.

\section{Results}\label{results}

The Results are organized as stages. Each stage states what the
original DGP does, what the extension adds, the evidence, and the
choice made with the criterion it rests on. Table \ref{tab:spec}, at
the end, collects the choices.

\subsection{Stage 1. Validity of the joint distribution}\label{stage-1.-validity-of-the-joint-distribution}

The original DGP accepts any \(c_{bm}\) and repairs a correlation matrix
that is not positive definite. Under its compound-symmetry structure
the matrix is positive definite only for \(c_{bm}\) below a bound.
Without carryover the bound is 0.256 in the Hybrid design (0.256 in
the crossover and 0.262 in the open-label-plus-blinded-discontinuation
design). These values follow from the feasibility bound with \(M\) the
original response block. The nonzero effect sizes in the original
study were 0.3 and 0.6. Counting one matrix per design path, effect
size and half-life, 37\% of the original grid's matrices at those
effect sizes (20 of 54) were replaced by nearby valid matrices (Table
\ref{tab:nonpd}). At \(c_{bm} = 0.3\) the repair changed no correlation
by more than 0.007. The original results at that effect size are
therefore essentially those of the stated DGP. At \(c_{bm} = 0.6\) the
repair lowered the on-drug correlation to 0.54 to 0.57 and moved other
correlations by up to 0.10. Those results describe a different DGP
from the one stated.

The bound is the condition that the biomarker's squared multiple
correlation on the whole response trajectory stays below one. Under
compound symmetry every visit's drug response shares the same
person-level factor. The biomarker therefore cannot be correlated with
the on-drug visits alone without being correlated, through that
factor, with the off-drug visits as well. The bound is low because the
correlation profile switches between visits that the structure ties
together.

We extended the DGP to check feasibility and stop. When the matrix is
not positive definite, the extended DGP reports the cell as infeasible
instead of changing it (Appendix B.2). The package function
\texttt{buildSigma()} now does so by default (Appendix B.5). The choice rests
on the existence criterion. One consequence is that the original
effect size of 0.6 cannot be simulated under any correlation encoding
in this paper. Table \ref{tab:nonpd} shows it infeasible in 75\% of
the extended grid under the extended DGP, and in every Hybrid cell.
The original DGP passes those cells at positive half-lives because its
rule keeps the correlation constant after first exposure. A constant
correlation places a small demand on the structure, and it carries no
interaction (Stage 2). The cells that need no repair under the
original rule are the cells in which the interaction is absent. What
the bound means in outcome units, and what to do above it, are taken
up in the planning section and the Discussion.

The case that fixed the default was a prognostic scenario in the
companion study (\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper02}{29}), not the original grid. Two runs
combined \(c_{bm} = 0.45\) with a prognostic correlation of 0.6. The
joint bound of Stage 6 shows that combination to be infeasible at any
\(c_{bm}\). The repair lowered the on-drug correlation, and the runs
reported the attenuated interaction as a loss of power to the
prognostic pathway. A repaired matrix does not report which
correlation it changed. A repair therefore turns an infeasible input
into a plausible but wrong finding. One degenerate case is
distinguished from a failure of existence. The placebo-response
component has standard deviation proportional to the expectancy. A
visit with expectancy zero has exactly zero placebo-response variance
and makes the covariance matrix singular by construction. The stop
path factors only the positive-variance sub-block and draws the
degenerate components at their means. No correlation is changed
(Appendix B.2).

\subsection{Stage 2. The biomarker-response correlation under carryover}\label{stage-2.-the-biomarker-response-correlation-under-carryover}

The original study reported that power to detect the interaction fell
sharply once carryover was introduced. In our 1,000-replicate rerun of
the original code in the Hybrid design at \(N = 70\) and \(c_{bm} = 0.3\),
power fell from 0.74 to 0.12 at a half-life of 0.1 week. At that
half-life only 0.1\% of the drug effect remains a week after stopping.
Figure 4 of the original study shows the same pattern. We did not
read values off it.

Under the original rule every off-drug visit in the Hybrid design
follows an exposure. With any positive half-life its drug-response
mean is positive, so \(g_t = 1\). Hence \(\bar g_{\text{off}} = 1\) and,
by Proposition 2,

\[
\beta_{bm:D}^{\text{E9}} = 0, \qquad
\beta_{bm:D}^{\text{E9b}} = -c_{bm}\frac{\sigma_{BR}}{\sigma_{bm}}\,
  \frac{1}{n_{\text{off}} + 1}
\qquad \text{for every } t_{1/2} > 0 .
\]

Power is a step function of the half-life (Table
\ref{tab:closedform}, Figure \ref{fig:powerfig}). It takes its
no-carryover value at \(t_{1/2} = 0\). It takes a constant value at
every half-life above about 0.007 week, whether 0.1 week or 10. Below
that, the off-drug mean underflows to zero in double precision and the
original rule sets \(g_t = 0\) at late off-drug visits (with the
original carryover rule, below about 0.012 week). For E9b the constant
is 0.081. This value comes from the baseline row, which alone keeps
\(g_t = 0\).

The original analysis behaves the same way. In our reconstruction of
the original DGP, the mean estimate falls from \(-0.126\) to between
\(-0.026\) and \(-0.032\) at every positive half-life. Power falls from
0.63 to between 0.08 and 0.11. In a separately seeded run of the
original code (Table \ref{tab:patch}), power falls from 0.668 to
0.126 at 0.1 week. The two implementations differ at 0.1 week by about
2.4 combined Monte Carlo SE, which is nominally significant. We did
not trace its source. It does not bear on the conclusion, since both
values are at or near the test size.

With a constant correlation, the biomarker moderates the on-drug and
off-drug responses equally. No interaction remains to detect. The loss
of power under small carryover is therefore a property of the
correlation rule, not of carryover or of the designs.

We extended the DGP so the correlation decays after discontinuation at
the carryover half-life: \(g_t = 1\) on drug, \(2^{-t_{sd,t}/t_{1/2}}\)
off drug after exposure, and 0 before any exposure. This is the
pharmacological reading of a predictive biomarker. The biomarker
predicts the size of the drug effect, so it cannot predict a drug
effect that is no longer present. Under the extended rule the mean of
\(g_t\) over the off-drug visits is

\[
\bar g_{\text{off}}(t_{1/2}) = \frac{1}{n_{\text{off}}}
  \sum_{t\,\text{off}} 2^{-t_{sd,t}/t_{1/2}},
\]

which is strictly increasing in \(t_{1/2}\), because every off-drug visit
has \(t_{sd} \ge 1\) week. Two consequences follow.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Power is strictly decreasing in the half-life} for the
  path-stratified paired-difference statistic. This holds on every
  path with at least one off-drug visit after an exposure (Proposition
  3, Appendix A.3). The placebo-first crossover path, whose off-drug
  visits all precede exposure, has constant power. For the pooled
  statistic power is non-increasing at every step of a 120-point grid
  from 0.03 to 4 weeks. For the original mixed model it was checked
  only at the simulated half-lives.
\item
  \textbf{The loss vanishes faster than any power of the half-life as
  \(t_{1/2} \to 0\).} At 0.1 week, \(\bar g_{\text{off}} \le 2^{-10}\)
  and power is unchanged to within 0.001.
\end{itemize}

The original rule is the limit of the extended rule as
\(t_{1/2} \to \infty\), applied at every positive half-life.

Power depends on the correlation profile only through the on/off
correlation difference \(\mathcal G = 1 - \bar g_{\text{off}}\) (Figure
\ref{fig:gapfig}). The difference is 1 without carryover. Under the
extended rule in the Hybrid design it is 0.9997 at 0.1 week, 0.991 at
0.2, 0.906 at 0.5, 0.754 at 1 and 0.551 at 2. Under the original rule
it is 0 at every positive half-life. The noncentrality is proportional
to \(r_{\Delta B}/\sqrt{1 - r_{\Delta B}^2}\), and \(r_{\Delta B}\) is
proportional to the difference. For small \(r_{\Delta B}\) the sample
size needed for a given power therefore scales approximately as
\(1/\mathcal G^2\). At 1 week this is about 1.8 times the no-carryover
sample. At 2 weeks it is about 3.3 times.

The extended DGP uses the decaying correlation. The choice rests on
identifiability under carryover and on fidelity to the pharmacology.
Two limitations of the choice are stated here. First, at the
pharmacological half-lives of 0.1 to 0.2 week the on/off correlation
difference is within 0.01 of 1. The extension therefore returns the
no-carryover result. The extended DGP at its default half-life is not
a test bed for carryover. The smooth decline is a statement about
half-lives of 0.5 week and above, which we label behavioral. Second,
the decaying correlation is asymmetric. Off drug the moderation fades
with the drug effect. On drug it is the full \(c_{bm}\sigma_{BR}b_i\) at
every visit, including the first weeks of titration when the Gompertz
mean is a small fraction of its maximum. A correlation proportional to
the current drug-response mean at every visit, or a correlation
half-life separate from the mean's, would remove the asymmetry.
Neither was tried. The sensitivity model of the Discussion is the form
that addresses it. The mixed-model power of the extended DGP at
half-lives of 0.5 to 2 weeks, with the half-life fixed in the analysis
or chosen from the data, is the subject of a companion paper
(\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper02}{29}).

\subsection{Stage 3. Carryover on the mean}\label{stage-3.-carryover-on-the-mean}

The original carryover rule decays the previous visit's mean by the
total time since discontinuation. At the second and later off-drug
visits that mean has already been decayed, so the decay compounds.
Consider two off-drug visits at weeks 11 and 12 following
discontinuation at week 10. The original code decays the week-10 value
by \(2^{-1/t_{1/2}}\) and then by \(2^{-2/t_{1/2}}\), a total exponent of
3 weeks for 2 weeks elapsed. Whether this was intended is not stated
in the original study, and we do not claim that it was not. We treat
the two forms as two specifications.

The extended DGP decays the mean from the last on-drug value,
\(\mu_{BR,\text{last}}\,2^{-t_{sd}/t_{1/2}}\). The mean at any off-drug
visit then depends only on the elapsed time (Appendix B.3).

The choice changes no slope, variance or feasibility bound. It changes
only the path means. It therefore changes the closed-form power of
study 1 by at most 0.002 in the Hybrid design. In the original
pipeline it changes power by at most 0.006. Figure \ref{fig:trajfig}
shows the drug-response mean by visit for the four Hybrid paths under
both forms. Two features are visible. At \(t_{1/2} = 1\) week the
original rule lies below the extended rule at the second off-drug
visit, as expected. At 0.2 week both are indistinguishable from zero
one week after discontinuation. At re-exposure (week 16 in paths 1 and
3) time on drug restarts at zero. The mean then rises along a fresh
Gompertz curve to 4.3 points at the week-16 visit, four weeks into the
restarted curve. The residual from the earlier exposure has decayed to
0.16 points by then even at a 1-week half-life. No downward step
therefore appears on the Hybrid schedule. A step would appear only if
a visit fell within about a week of re-exposure at a half-life of a
week or more.

The extended DGP decays carryover from the last on-drug mean. It keeps
the original restart of time on drug at re-exposure, which for
prazosin matches re-titration after an interruption. For drugs without
titration the response should instead rebuild from its residual level.
That is an option, not the default. The choice rests on fidelity to
the pharmacology, as a judgment. The numerical consequence is at most
0.006 in power either way.

\subsection{Stage 4. Serial correlation}\label{stage-4.-serial-correlation}

The original DGP uses compound symmetry. Visits one week apart and
sixteen weeks apart have the same correlation of 0.8. We have no PTSD
symptom series with which to decide whether that overstates long-lag
correlation, and we do not claim that it does. Both compound symmetry
and AR(1) are used in N-of-1 simulation (\citeproc{ref-chen2014}{17}). Serial
correlation has been discussed as a design consideration for
personalized studies (\citeproc{ref-schork2022}{30}). Two elements are open: the form of
the within-component correlation, and its value.

The extended DGP uses AR(1) in calendar weeks,
\(\rho^{\lvert w_t - w_s\rvert}\), with one \(\rho\) for all three
components. The value of \(\rho\) is a parameter. We take \(\rho = 0.7\)
as a convention, not an estimate. It is the original 0.8 lowered by
0.1, which makes the original effect size of 0.3 feasible under the
original structure (below). It should be read with the sensitivity
range in Table \ref{tab:rhostage}.

Table \ref{tab:rhostage} shows what \(\rho\) does under compound
symmetry and under the structure adopted in Stage 5, for
\(\rho \in \{0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8\}\). Under compound symmetry alone,
lowering \(\rho_{cs}\) from 0.8 to 0.7 raises the feasibility bound from
0.256 to 0.346. The original effect size of 0.3 then becomes feasible
by the change of \(\rho\) alone, with no change of structure. The same
change lowers closed-form power at \(c_{bm} = 0.25\) from 0.721 to
0.628. A lower serial correlation leaves more within-person noise in
the on-minus-off contrast. The two criteria pull in opposite
directions. The feasible range widens as \(\rho\) falls, and the power
at a matched \(c_{bm}\) falls. The paper's own default should be read in
that light.

\subsection{Stage 5. Cross-component structure}\label{stage-5.-cross-component-structure}

The original DGP has a same-visit cross-component correlation
\(c_1 = 0.2\) and an other-visit correlation \(c_\times = 0.1\) at every
lag. With AR(1) within components, the direct translation keeps \(c_1\)
at lag zero and lets the other-visit correlation decay,
\(c_\times\rho^{\lvert w_t - w_s\rvert}\). This two-constant form is what
the package's \texttt{buildSigma()} builds when its two constants differ
(Appendix B.5). It is not the only choice, and it has a validity
condition.

The extended DGP uses the separable form,
\(c_1\rho^{\lvert w_t - w_s\rvert}\) at every lag including zero. The
response block is then a Kronecker product \(M = K \otimes A\) of a
\(3 \times 3\) component correlation \(K\) (1 on the diagonal, \(c_1\) off
it) and one AR(1) kernel \(A_{ts} = \rho^{\lvert w_t - w_s\rvert}\). In
words, every component has the same first-order autoregressive
correlation, and the between-component correlation is constant at
every lag. It is the two-constant form with \(c_\times = c_1\). The
original same-visit value \(c_1 = 0.2\) is kept. Four properties follow
(Appendix A.5).

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Validity for every schedule.} The eigenvalues of \(M\) are products
  of those of \(K\) and \(A\), so \(M\) is positive definite for any visit
  times. The two-constant form adds a same-visit cross-component term
  that carries correlation without variance. It is positive definite
  only when the smallest eigenvalue of \(A\) exceeds
  \((c_1 - c_\times)/(1 - c_\times) = 0.111\). On the paper's schedules
  the condition holds (smallest eigenvalue 0.122 in the Hybrid and
  OL+BDC designs at \(\rho = 0.8\), 0.193 at 0.7; 0.28 and 0.43 in the
  crossover design). On 20 weekly visits at \(\rho = 0.8\) it holds by
  less than 0.001. On daily measurement over eight weeks it fails
  (0.016 at \(\rho = 0.8\), 0.026 at 0.7). The two-constant form
  therefore cannot be used for the dense schedules that wearable or
  diary outcomes produce.
\item
  \textbf{The original structure is already of this kind in pieces.} It is
  the sum of a person-level and an occasion-level separable term,
  each valid. This is why the original matrices fail only through the
  biomarker row, never in the response block.
\item
  \textbf{An exact, interpretable feasibility bound.} The bound factors
  into a component term and a schedule term,
  \[
  c_{bm}^{*} = \sqrt{1 - R^2}\;\bigl(g^\top A^{-1} g\bigr)^{-1/2},
  \qquad R^2 = \frac{2c_1^2}{1 + c_1},
  \]
  where \(R^2\) is the squared multiple correlation of the drug response
  on the other two components at one visit. The schedule term is a
  sum of per-step terms of the correlation profile. A profile that
  switches abruptly between closely spaced visits has a large
  per-step term. This is why designs with many on-off switches can
  carry only small effects.
\item
  \textbf{It matches the analysis model.} Under separability the summed
  response is an AR(1) process, up to the expectancy scaling of the
  placebo-response component. That is the residual structure
  \texttt{corCAR1} assumes. Under the two-constant form it is AR(1) plus a
  white-noise term the analysis model omits. This is derived. Its
  effect on test calibration has not been measured.
\end{enumerate}

Table \ref{tab:ceilings} gives the feasibility bounds. At the
original \(\rho = 0.8\) the two-constant form raises the bound only to
0.291. A single same-visit correlation repeated across visits that
share little demands more of the biomarker row. In the open-label
design, where every visit is on drug, it lowers the bound. The
separable form raises the bound to 0.440. At \(\rho = 0.7\) the two
forms are close, 0.452 against 0.474. At the recommended \(\rho\) the
separable form's gain in feasible range is therefore small, and its
reasons are properties 1 and 4. The bound of 0.474 is attained in the
OL+BDC design (0.480 in the Hybrid design). A correlation of 0.45 is
feasible in every design without carryover. Effects as large as the
original 0.6 exceed every feasibility bound in the Hybrid design.

The wider feasible range has a consequence that does not depend on
the form. At the same \(c_{bm}\), AR(1) data carry less information
about the interaction than compound-symmetric data. The closed-form
power of the paired-difference statistic in the Hybrid design at
\(c_{bm} = 0.25\) falls from 0.721 under the original structure to 0.335
under the separable form at \(\rho = 0.8\) and 0.290 at \(\rho = 0.7\). A
larger feasible correlation does not by itself mean more power. Effect
sizes under different structures should therefore be compared on the
scale of the implied slope, not of \(c_{bm}\).

\subsection{Stage 6. Features the extended DGP adds}\label{stage-6.-features-the-extended-dgp-adds}

The original DGP correlates the biomarker with the drug response only.
A biomarker that is also prognostic, through an association with the
natural history, the placebo response or baseline severity, cannot be
represented. A simulation built on the original DGP therefore cannot
show whether an analysis stays valid under such a biomarker.

The extended DGP adds optional correlations of the biomarker with the
natural-history component or the placebo-response component (constant
over visits, or growing along the natural-history curve) and with
baseline severity. It adds phase-specific measurement error, a Weibull
decay form and monotone dropout as further options.

\textbf{A prognostic biomarker.} We simulated a correlation of 0.3 between
the biomarker and the natural-history component (constant, or growing
along its curve), the placebo-response component, or baseline
severity, with no interaction. Analyses that keep baseline as an
outcome row use one biomarker main effect \(\beta_B\) for baseline and
for the off-drug visits. When the biomarker is prognostic through a
pathway that is absent at baseline, those two associations differ.
Part of the difference is attributed to the interaction (Appendix
A.4). Every analysis that shares the biomarker effect with baseline
exceeded the nominal level in at least one such cell (Table
\ref{tab:prognostic}).

design is within 0.03 of the Hybrid values. The crossover design has
looser bounds throughout. We verified the bounds by checking that the
smallest eigenvalue of the full matrix is positive at 0.999 times each
bound and negative at 1.001 times it.

\subsection{Using the extended DGP to plan a trial}\label{using-the-extended-dgp-to-plan-a-trial}

\textbf{What \(c_{bm}\) means.} Conditional on the biomarker, the correlation
encoding shifts the on-drug drug-response mean by \(c_{bm}\sigma_{BR}\)
per standard deviation of biomarker (Discussion). With the original
\(\sigma_{BR} = 8\), \(c_{bm} = 0.25\) is a slope of 2.0 symptom points
per SD of biomarker, 0.35 is 2.8 points, 0.45 is 3.6 points, and the
feasibility bound of 0.474 is 3.8 points. The biomarker's SD is 15.4
mm Hg in the original parameters, so 0.25 is 0.13 points per mm Hg.
The observed association for prazosin is 14 points on the
Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale per 10 mm Hg (\citeproc{ref-raskind2016bp}{9}), or
21.5 points per SD of biomarker. Representing that slope under the
correlation encoding would need \(\sigma_{BR} \ge 45\) at the bound.
That is larger than the SD of the symptom score itself (18.5 at
baseline in the original parameters). Under the original parameters
the bound is therefore below the prazosin association. The comparison
is approximate for two reasons. The observed slope is on the total
symptom change, not the drug-response component. And
\(\sigma_{BR} = 8\) is the original choice, not an estimate. A study
that needs a slope above the bound should use the sensitivity model of
the Discussion once it is implemented, or raise \(\sigma_{BR}\) with a
stated reason.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

\subsection{The correlation encoding of the interaction}\label{the-correlation-encoding-of-the-interaction}

\textbf{Conditional structure.} Under joint normality, conditioning on the
biomarker shows what the correlation encoding does to the drug
response. With \(b_i\) the standardized biomarker,

\[
E(BR_{it} \mid B_i) = \mu_{BR,t} + c_{bm}\, g_t\, \sigma_{BR}\, b_i,
\qquad
\mathrm{Cov}(BR_{i\cdot} \mid B_i) = \Sigma_{BR} - c_{bm}^2\sigma_{BR}^2\, g g^\top .
\]

The encoding therefore shifts the conditional drug-response mean in
proportion to the correlation profile \(g_t\). It reduces the residual
drug-response covariance by a rank-one term. Two consequences follow.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{The power loss under carryover is set by the profile.} The size
  of the loss depends on whether the interaction fades with the drug
  effect, which is the correlation rule.
\item
  \textbf{The feasibility bound is the condition} that the reduced
  residual covariance remains positive definite.
\end{itemize}

The contribution of the original DGP is therefore a parameterization
of the interaction as a correlation that keeps the marginal variance
of the drug response fixed. That parameterization was new in the
N-of-1 literature, to our knowledge. Both its strengths and its limits
follow from it.

The correlation encoding has four strengths.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{One interpretable parameter.} The interaction strength is a
  single correlation. Its square, \(c_{bm}^2\), is the share of on-drug
  drug-response variance the biomarker explains.
\item
  \textbf{A reduced form of responder heterogeneity.} Suppose participants
  fall into two latent responder classes, and both the biomarker and
  the drug response differ between classes. Then class membership
  carries the covariance between them. Matching second moments gives
  \(c_{bm}^2 = f_B f_{BR}\), where \(f_B\) and \(f_{BR}\) are the fractions
  of the biomarker's and the drug response's variance that lie between
  classes (Appendix A.6). The encoding therefore matches the second
  moments of a responder-class mechanism without requiring the mixture
  to be identified. Identifying the mixture typically needs larger
  samples than N-of-1 programs enroll (\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper03}{34}). The match
  is limited to second moments. The mixture's conditional mean is
  nonlinear in the biomarker, and its higher moments differ.
\item
  \textbf{The marginal variance of the drug response is fixed.} The
  biomarker explains part of the existing variation rather than
  adding to it. The effect size can therefore be stated as a share of
  response variance without changing the response distribution.
\item
  \textbf{It fits a biomarker that marks individual sensitivity to the
  drug} better than one that sets a fixed dose-response shift.
\end{itemize}

Its limits, all made explicit by this analysis, are structural.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{The joint distribution must exist,} which bounds the effect size.
  In outcome units the bound is \(c_{bm}^{*}\sigma_{BR}\) per SD of
  biomarker, 3.8 points under the extended DGP at the original
  \(\sigma_{BR}\). The planning section shows this is below the observed
  prazosin association. The bound depends on the correlation structure
  and the design.
\item
  \textbf{The correlation must be specified at off-drug visits.} A constant
  correlation after first exposure removes the interaction under
  carryover. The specification adopted here is itself asymmetric
  between titration and washout (Stage 2).
\item
  \textbf{The drug-response component keeps its full variance at off-drug
  visits,} with zero mean. This adds noise to every on-off contrast.
  It belongs to the component decomposition, not to the correlation
  encoding.
\item
  \textbf{A correlation is less intuitive than a slope for clinical
  readers.} The slope it implies, \(-c_{bm}\sigma_{BR}/\sigma_{bm}\),
  must be derived before it can be compared with an observed effect.
  The comparison depends on \(\sigma_{BR}\), which is not estimated.
\end{itemize}

A natural next step addresses these limits. Generate the drug
response from a participant-level sensitivity to exposure,

\[
BR_{it} = \omega_i f(t) + \varepsilon_{it}, \qquad
\omega_i = \bar\omega\,(1 + \zeta b_i) + u_i ,
\]

where \(f(t)\) is the exposure-response time course, \(\zeta\) the
biomarker's effect on sensitivity and \(u_i\) the sensitivity it does
not explain. This is a random-coefficient model with a
covariate-by-exposure interaction, the form used to describe
individual response in replicated crossover and N-of-1 trials
(\citeproc{ref-senn2016mastering}{7},\citeproc{ref-araujo2016}{20}).

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  It encodes the biomarker through individual sensitivity, so it has
  no feasibility bound.
\item
  Its moderation is proportional to the response at every visit, so it
  fades during titration as well as after discontinuation.
\item
  Its off-drug variance shrinks with the response.
\end{itemize}

It is not yet implemented in the software. Its response half-life for
prazosin in PTSD is not known. Until then, the correlation encoding's
range of use is effect sizes below the feasibility bound, which the
planning section translates into outcome units.

\subsection{What remains uncalibrated}\label{what-remains-uncalibrated}

The extended DGP meets the existence, identifiability and
analyzability criteria by construction and derivation. It meets the
fidelity criterion by judgment. It does not meet the calibration
criterion: every parameter is the original value or a choice made
here. The values that matter most are the serial correlation, the
drug-response SD and the symptom half-life. All three could be
estimated from an aggregated N-of-1 trial of prazosin with weekly
symptom measurement. Such a trial would also settle whether the
compound-symmetry or the AR(1) form fits such series.

\subsection{Recommendations}\label{recommendations}

For simulation studies that build on the original DGP, we recommend
the following.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Apply both extensions:} stop on non-positive-definite matrices,
  and let the biomarker-response correlation decay after
  discontinuation.
\item
  \textbf{State the choices:} carryover decaying from the last on-drug
  mean, AR(1) in weeks at a reported \(\rho\), and the separable
  cross-component form. Repeat the analysis at neighboring \(\rho\)
  where the conclusion could depend on it.
\item
  \textbf{Choose \(c_{bm}\) below the feasibility bound} for every design in
  the study. Report it on two scales: as \(c_{bm}\), and as the implied
  slope in outcome points per SD of biomarker.
\item
  \textbf{Keep the original DGP as a reference arm} whenever results are
  compared with the original ones. Attribute each difference to a
  named stage.
\item
  \textbf{Include a prognostic scenario} whenever the analysis shares the
  biomarker effect with the baseline visit. State the trade-off
  between the power of the plain model and the robustness of the
  expectancy-term model rather than resolving it for the trialist.
\item
  \textbf{Use the sensitivity model, once available, for effects above the
  feasibility bound.} Until then, state the bound in outcome units
  as a limitation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}\label{limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{The closed form covers one statistic.} It is exact for the
  paired-difference statistic, not for the original mixed model. The
  two agree closely in the Hybrid design. In the crossover design the
  closed form understates what a time-adjusted analysis achieves. The
  planning table inherits this.
\item
  \textbf{At pharmacological half-lives the extended DGP shows no carryover
  effect.} The on/off correlation difference is within 0.01 of 1 at
  0.2 week. The DGP therefore tests carryover only at half-lives of
  0.5 week and above, which we cannot ground pharmacologically.
\item
  \textbf{The correlation is asymmetric between titration and washout.}
  Full moderation during the first weeks on drug is a modeling choice
  inherited from the original DGP, not a consequence of the
  pharmacology.
\item
  \textbf{Parameter values are not estimated.} The Gompertz parameters,
  correlations, \(\sigma_{BR}\) and prognostic correlations are original
  values or chosen values, not estimates from prazosin trial data.
\item
  \textbf{The DGP is Gaussian with a single biomarker.}
\item
  \textbf{The pharmacological carryover range} for prazosin at weekly
  visits is inferred from its plasma half-life, not measured as a
  symptom-response half-life.
\item
  \textbf{The stress test is exploratory.} Cells and analyses were added
  after first results. Its prognostic scenarios are the ones that show
  the size inflation by construction.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

The original DGP encodes the predictive-biomarker interaction as a
correlation between the biomarker and the drug response. That encoding
remains a coherent choice for simulating aggregated N-of-1 trials. The
original DGP holds the correlation constant after first exposure, so
the interaction is absent whenever there is carryover. It repairs
correlation matrices that are not positive definite. Both features
have closed-form explanations, and both are changed by small
extensions. With a correlation that decays after discontinuation and
a feasibility check that stops instead of repairing, the extended DGP
returns the no-carryover power at pharmacological half-lives (the
on/off correlation difference is within 0.01 of 1 at 0.2 week). Power
declines smoothly at half-lives of 0.5 week and above, and
monotonically for the paired-difference statistic. A serial
correlation of 0.7 and a separable AR(1) structure allow a
biomarker-response correlation of 0.47, or 3.8 symptom points per SD
of biomarker at the original drug-response SD. The planning table
quantifies the power at matched strength. We propose the extended DGP,
with options for prognostic pathways and other features that change
which analysis is valid, as the reference for simulation studies of
these designs. Every parameter is labeled as original, chosen or
uncalibrated.

\section*{Glossary}\label{glossary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Glossary}

Terms are listed in the order in which the paper introduces them.
Labels in backticks are the identifiers used in the repository's
code, data files and table headings.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Original DGP} (\texttt{published}, ``Published'' and ``Step'' in tables):
  the data-generating process of Hendrickson et al.~(2020), as
  implemented in their code at commit 58b32a9.
\item
  \textbf{Extended DGP} (\texttt{refined}, ``Refined'' in tables): the
  data-generating process of Table \ref{tab:spec}, which adds the
  decaying correlation, the feasibility check, carryover decaying from
  the last on-drug mean, the separable AR(1) structure and the
  optional features to the original DGP.
\item
  \textbf{Correlation encoding of the interaction} (\texttt{mvn}): the predictive
  biomarker is a correlation between the biomarker and the drug
  response that is present on drug and absent off drug.
\item
  \textbf{Biomarker-response correlation} (\(c_{bm}\), \texttt{c.bm}): the
  correlation between the baseline biomarker and the drug-response
  component at an on-drug visit, which sets the strength of the
  interaction.
\item
  \textbf{Correlation profile} (\(g_t\)): the fraction of \(c_{bm}\) present at
  visit \(t\), equal to 1 on drug.
\item
  \textbf{Original rule} (\texttt{step}, ``Step'' in tables): the biomarker-response
  correlation is \(c_{bm}\) at every visit where the drug-response mean
  is nonzero, and so is constant after first exposure.
\item
  \textbf{Decaying correlation} (\texttt{graded}, ``Graded'' in tables): the
  extended rule, under which the correlation is \(c_{bm}\) on drug,
  \(c_{bm}2^{-t_{sd}/t_{1/2}}\) off drug after exposure, and 0 before
  exposure.
\item
  \textbf{On/off correlation difference} (\(\mathcal G\)): the on-drug value
  of \(g_t\) minus the mean of \(g_t\) over the post-baseline off-drug
  visits, \(1 - \bar g_{\text{off}}\), through which alone the
  correlation rule affects closed-form power.
\item
  \textbf{Feasibility bound} (\(c_{bm}^{*}\), ``Ceiling'' in tables): the
  largest \(c_{bm}\) for which the correlation matrix is positive
  definite under a given correlation structure, design and
  correlation profile.
\item
  \textbf{Carryover half-life} (\(t_{1/2}\), \texttt{carryover\_t1half}): the time
  after discontinuation for the drug-response mean, and under the
  extended rule the biomarker-response correlation, to fall by half.
\item
  \textbf{Pharmacological and behavioral half-lives}: half-lives of 0.1 to
  0.2 week are the assumed pharmacological range for prazosin, and
  half-lives of 0.5 week and above are labeled behavioral scenarios.
\item
  \textbf{Original carryover rule} (\texttt{recursive}): the drug-response mean at
  an off-drug visit is the previous visit's mean decayed by the time
  since discontinuation, so the decay compounds across visits.
\item
  \textbf{Extended carryover rule} (\texttt{anchored}): the drug-response mean at
  an off-drug visit is the last on-drug mean decayed by the elapsed
  time since discontinuation.
\item
  \textbf{Drug-response component} (BR): the latent response driven by time
  on drug, with mean on a modified Gompertz curve.
\item
  \textbf{Placebo-response component} (PB): the latent response driven by
  time in the trial and scaled by the expectancy.
\item
  \textbf{Natural-history component} (TV): the latent response driven by
  time in the trial that does not depend on treatment.
\item
  \textbf{Baseline severity} (BL): the participant's symptom level before
  any response, which enters every visit equally.
\item
  \textbf{Expectancy} (\(\eta_t\)): the participant's probability of being on
  drug at visit \(t\) given the design, 1 in the open-label phase and
  0.5 when blinded.
\item
  \textbf{Compound symmetry} (\(\rho_{cs}\)): the same correlation between
  any two visits of a component, whatever their separation.
\item
  \textbf{Separable AR(1) structure}: the same first-order autoregressive
  correlation \(\rho^{\lvert w_t - w_s\rvert}\) for every component,
  with a constant between-component correlation \(c_1\) at every lag,
  so that the response block is a Kronecker product.
\item
  \textbf{Two-constant form}: an AR(1) structure in which the same-visit
  cross-component correlation \(c_1\) and the other-visit correlation
  \(c_\times\rho^{\lvert w_t - w_s\rvert}\) use different constants.
\item
  \textbf{Hybrid design} (\texttt{Hybrid}): open-label titration, blinded
  discontinuation, then a brief crossover, with visits at weeks 4, 8,
  9, 10, 11, 12, 16 and 20.
\item
  \textbf{OL+BDC} (\texttt{OLBDC}): open label followed by blinded
  discontinuation.
\item
  \textbf{CO}: the crossover design, in which participants receive drug and
  placebo in one of two orders.
\item
  \textbf{OL}: the open-label design, in which every visit is on drug.
\item
  \textbf{Path}: one sequence of drug and placebo phases within a design,
  to which participants are allocated in fixed numbers.
\item
  \textbf{Paired-difference statistic} (E9, E9b): the per-participant
  difference between the mean on-drug and mean off-drug scores,
  regressed on the biomarker, using post-baseline visits only (E9) or
  counting baseline as one more off-drug visit (E9b).
\item
  \textbf{Original analysis} (``Published'' in tables): the linear mixed
  model of the original study, with a random intercept, a binary drug
  indicator and baseline included as an off-drug row.
\item
  \textbf{Exposure-decayed drug indicator} (\(D_{bc}\)): a drug indicator
  equal to 1 on drug, \(2^{-t_{sd}/t_{1/2}}\) off drug after exposure,
  and 0 before exposure.
\item
  \textbf{Prognostic correlation} (\(c_{PB}\), \(c_{TV}\), \(c_{BL}\);
  \texttt{prog-PB}, \texttt{prog-TV-const}, \texttt{prog-TV-grow}, \texttt{prog-BL} in data
  labels): a correlation of the biomarker with the placebo-response
  component, with the natural-history component (constant over visits,
  or growing along its curve), or with baseline severity.
\item
  \textbf{Expectancy-term model}: the random-intercept CAR(1) analysis that
  adds the expectancy and a biomarker-by-expectancy term to the mean
  model.
\item
  \textbf{Free-baseline model}: the random-intercept CAR(1) analysis that
  adds \texttt{bmc:bl0}, a separate biomarker slope for the baseline row.
\item
  \textbf{CAR(1)}: the continuous-time first-order autoregressive residual
  correlation \texttt{corCAR1} of the analysis model.
\item
  \textbf{Calibration ratio} (\(\kappa\)): the mean model standard error
  divided by the empirical standard deviation of the estimates.
\item
  \textbf{Stress test} (\texttt{11-strawman-stress-test.R}): study 3, the
  simulation that fits fourteen analyses to trials from the extended
  DGP with and without a prognostic biomarker.
\item
  \textbf{The five criteria}: existence (the joint distribution exists),
  identifiability under carryover (the interaction remains an
  on-minus-off contrast), fidelity to the pharmacology, analyzability
  (the analysis model can represent the generated structure and the
  DGP can generate the features that decide validity), and
  calibration status (each parameter is labeled original, chosen or
  uncalibrated).
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{A.1 Moments of the paired-difference statistic}\label{a.1-moments-of-the-paired-difference-statistic}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{A.1 Moments of the paired-difference statistic}

Let \(a_t = 1/n_{\text{on}}\) at on-drug visits and \(-1/n_{\text{off}}\) at
off-drug visits, so that \(\Delta_i = \sum_t a_t Y_{it}\). Because the
weights sum to zero, the baseline level cancels and
\(\Delta = -\sum_t a_t S_t\), with \(S_t = TV_t + PB_t + BR_t\). In general
\(\mathrm{Var}(\Delta) = a^\top \Sigma_S a\), where \(\Sigma_S\) is the
covariance of the summed response, whatever the structure.

With \(m_1 = \sum a_t\), \(m_2 = \sum a_t^2\), \(m_\eta = \sum a_t \eta_t\),
\(m_{\eta\eta} = \sum a_t^2 \eta_t^2\) and \(m_{2\eta} = \sum a_t^2 \eta_t\),
with \(\eta_t\) the design expectancy, the variance under compound
symmetry with common correlation \(\rho_{cs}\) is

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Var}(\Delta) = {} & (\sigma_{TV}^2 + \sigma_{BR}^2)
  \big[\rho_{cs} m_1^2 + (1 - \rho_{cs}) m_2\big]
+ \sigma_{PB}^2 \big[\rho_{cs} m_\eta^2 + (1 - \rho_{cs}) m_{\eta\eta}\big] \\
& + 2\sigma_{TV}\sigma_{BR}\big[c_\times m_1^2 + (c_1 - c_\times) m_2\big]
+ 2(\sigma_{TV} + \sigma_{BR})\sigma_{PB}
  \big[c_\times m_1 m_\eta + (c_1 - c_\times) m_{2\eta}\big],
\end{aligned}
\]

where \(c_1\) and \(c_\times\) are the same-visit and other-visit
cross-component correlations. Under the separable form of Stage 5,
\(\mathrm{Cov}(S_t, S_s) = (s_t^\top K s_s) A_{ts}\) (A.5), and
\(\mathrm{Var}(\Delta) = \sum_{t,s} a_t a_s (s_t^\top K s_s) A_{ts}\), with
\(s_t\) the vector of component standard deviations at visit \(t\).

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Proposition 1.} No term depends on \(t_{1/2}\) under either
  structure, so \(\mathrm{Var}(\Delta)\) is free of the half-life. The
  argument applies to any correlation matrix in which the half-life
  does not enter the response block. It therefore holds for unrepaired
  matrices only. The repair changes the response block, and its
  changes depend on the half-life through the correlation profile. For
  E9 the weights sum to zero, so \(m_1 = 0\). For E9b the post-baseline
  weights sum to \(1/(n_{\text{off}} + 1)\), and the baseline row, with
  \(S_0 = 0\), carries the remaining weight.
\item
  \textbf{Proposition 2.} Only \(BR_t\) is correlated with \(B\), with covariance
  \(c_{bm} g_t\sigma_{bm}\sigma_{BR}\), so
  \(\beta_{bm:D} = -c_{bm}(\sigma_{BR}/\sigma_{bm})\sum_t a_t g_t\). With
  \(g_t = 1\) on drug, this gives the expressions in the Methods.
\item
  \textbf{Residual variance.} Under the correlation encoding,
  \(\sigma_\Delta^2 = \mathrm{Var}(\Delta) - \beta_{bm:D}^2\sigma_{bm}^2\).
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{A.2 Power}\label{a.2-power}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{A.2 Power}

Within a path of \(n\) participants, the least-squares slope is exactly
normal given the biomarker values. Its \(t\)-statistic is exactly
noncentral \(t\) with \(n - 2\) degrees of freedom and noncentrality
\(\beta_{bm:D}\sqrt{\mathrm{SS}_B}/\sigma_\Delta\), where
\(\mathrm{SS}_B \sim \sigma_{bm}^2\chi^2_{n-1}\).

At \(\mathrm{SS}_B = \sigma_{bm}^2(n - 1)\) the noncentrality is
\(\Lambda = \sqrt{n - 1}\,r_{\Delta B}/\sqrt{1 - r_{\Delta B}^2}\),
where \(r_{\Delta B} = \beta_{bm:D}\sigma_{bm}/\mathrm{SD}(\Delta)\) is
proportional to the on/off correlation difference. For small
\(r_{\Delta B}\) the noncentrality is approximately
\(\sqrt{n - 1}\,r_{\Delta B}\). The sample size for a fixed
noncentrality therefore scales as \(1/r_{\Delta B}^2\) and hence
approximately as \(1/\mathcal G^2\). The exact scaling uses
\(r/\sqrt{1 - r^2}\).

Pooling paths with a common intercept adds the between-path variation
in mean contrast to the residual variance. In the Hybrid design it is
under 0.7\% of the total. Power is the noncentral-\(t\) tail probability
averaged over the \(\chi^2\) law of \(\mathrm{SS}_B\).

\subsection*{A.3 Monotonicity under the decaying correlation}\label{a.3-monotonicity-under-the-decaying-correlation}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{A.3 Monotonicity under the decaying correlation}

\[
\frac{d\bar g_{\text{off}}}{dt_{1/2}}
  = \frac{\ln 2}{n_{\text{off}}\,t_{1/2}^2}
    \sum_{t\,\text{off}} t_{sd,t}\,2^{-t_{sd,t}/t_{1/2}} > 0
\]

on any path with at least one off-drug visit after an exposure.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Proposition 3.} On such a path, by Proposition 2 the slope's
  magnitude is strictly decreasing in \(t_{1/2}\). By A.1,
  \(\sigma_\Delta^2\) is strictly increasing, since \(\mathrm{Var}(\Delta)\)
  is fixed. So the noncentrality and the power of the path-stratified
  statistic are strictly decreasing. On a path whose off-drug visits
  all precede first exposure (the placebo-first crossover path),
  \(\bar g_{\text{off}} = 0\) at every half-life and the power is constant.
  The pooled statistic's residual variance also contains the
  between-path variation of mean contrasts, which depends on the
  half-life through the carryover means. Its monotonicity was checked
  on a grid (Stage 2), not proved.
\item
  \textbf{Small half-lives.} \(\bar g_{\text{off}} \le 2^{-1/t_{1/2}}\), which
  gives the behavior near zero stated in the Results.
\item
  \textbf{Large half-lives.} \(\bar g_{\text{off}} \to 1\) as
  \(t_{1/2} \to \infty\), which recovers the original rule.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{A.4 The feasibility bound and the shared biomarker effect}\label{a.4-the-feasibility-bound-and-the-shared-biomarker-effect}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{A.4 The feasibility bound and the shared biomarker effect}

\textbf{The feasibility bound.} Order the variables as biomarker, then
responses, and partition the correlation matrix as
\(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & c_{bm}\tilde g^\top \\ c_{bm}\tilde g & M\end{pmatrix}\).
By the Schur complement, the matrix is positive definite if and only if
\(M\) is positive definite and \(1 - c_{bm}^2 \tilde g^\top M^{-1}\tilde g > 0\).
Hence the bound is \(c_{bm}^* = (\tilde g^\top M^{-1}\tilde g)^{-1/2}\).

\textbf{With prognostic correlations.} Let \(M_0\) be the correlation matrix
of everything but the biomarker, including baseline severity, which is
uncorrelated with the response components. The biomarker row is a sum
of fixed patterns scaled by the correlations,
\(r = c_{bm}\tilde g + c_{PB}\, p + c_{TV}\, q + c_{BL}\, b\), where \(p\)
and \(q\) are one on every visit of the placebo-response and
natural-history components and \(b\) is one on baseline severity. The
same Schur argument gives the condition \(r^\top M_0^{-1} r < 1\), that
is \(c^\top A c < 1\) with \(c = (c_{bm}, c_{PB}, c_{TV}, c_{BL})\) and
\(A_{jk} = x_j^\top M_0^{-1} x_k\) for \(x \in \{\tilde g, p, q, b\}\). The
feasible region is the interior of an ellipsoid. The bound for a
single correlation is the case \(A_{\tilde g\tilde g}^{-1/2}\). Fixing
all correlations but one makes \(c^\top A c = 1\) a quadratic in that
correlation, whose positive root is its bound. A negative discriminant
means the fixed correlations are already infeasible. Because \(b\) is an
eigenvector of \(M_0\) with unit eigenvalue, \(c_{BL}\) enters only
through its square. Its bound given \(c_{bm}\) is
\(\sqrt{1 - c_{bm}^2 A_{\tilde g\tilde g}}\). The \(p\) and \(q\) patterns
are structurally identical, so \(c_{PB}\) and \(c_{TV}\) have the same
bounds. A design's bound is the minimum over its paths. The
off-diagonal entries of \(A\) are negative at the extended DGP's values,
so the correlations are mildly complementary. A biomarker correlated
with the drug response already implies a small correlation with the
other components. An explicit correlation of the same sign therefore
uses less of the feasible region than it would if the components were
independent.

\textbf{The shared biomarker effect.} Let \(s_0\), \(s_{\text{off}}\) and
\(s_{\text{on}}\) be the true slopes of \(Y\) on \(B\) at baseline, at
post-baseline off-drug visits and at on-drug visits. A model with
baseline as an off-drug row estimates one \(\beta_B\) for \(s_0\) and
\(s_{\text{off}}\).

The following account is heuristic, not a derivation. Consider a
prognostic biomarker with no interaction, whose post-baseline slope is
constant: \(s_0 = 0 \ne s_{\text{off}} = s_{\text{on}} = s_{\text{prog}}\).

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{The bias.} The fitted \(\hat\beta_B\) lies between the baseline and
  off-drug slopes, \(\hat\beta_B \approx f\,s_{\text{prog}}\) for some
  \(0 < f < 1\) that depends on the weight the baseline row receives.
  The interaction absorbs the remainder,
  \(\hat\beta_{bm:D} \approx (1 - f)\,s_{\text{prog}} \ne 0\).
\item
  \textbf{The correction.} Adding \(B_i\cdot\mathbf 1\{t = 0\}\) frees \(s_0\)
  and removes this source of bias. That the baseline row imposes such
  a constancy constraint is known from the literature on baseline as a
  covariate or a response (\citeproc{ref-liu2009baseline}{35}).
\end{itemize}

Two further mechanisms operate in the stress-test scenarios.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{The slope grows over time.} When the post-baseline slope grows,
  the bias depends on how drug status is confounded with time.
  Freeing \(s_0\) is then not sufficient in general. The residual 0.063
  for that analysis is consistent with this.
\item
  \textbf{Expectancy.} When the biomarker is associated with the placebo
  response, the slope also scales with the expectancy \(\eta_t\), which
  is higher at the open-label (on-drug) visits. The expectancy term
  \(B_i \cdot \eta_t\), with \(\eta_0 = 0\), addresses both this mechanism
  and the baseline constraint. Freeing \(s_0\) alone does not. An
  expectancy-scaled slope still enters the interaction through the
  open-label visits.
\end{itemize}

Neither correction is exact in the Hybrid design. The expectancy term
removes a constant post-baseline slope from the interaction only when
the blinded on-drug and off-drug visits carry equal total weight. In
the Hybrid design they do not, so a small bias remains. The
corresponding derivations are in the project's compendium of
mathematical results (\texttt{analysis/report/compendium-mathematics.tex}).
The stress-test null rejection rates across the four prognostic cells
(0.040 to 0.047 for the expectancy-term model, 0.049 to 0.063 for the
free-baseline model) show that these residual biases are small at the
prognostic correlations simulated.

A scenario in which the biomarker is associated with baseline severity
alone was simulated in the stress test. The original analysis rejected
0.075 there, and both corrections stayed within the band.

\subsection*{A.5 The separable structure}\label{a.5-the-separable-structure}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{A.5 The separable structure}

Let \(A_{ts} = \rho^{\lvert w_t - w_s\rvert}\) and
\(K = (1 - c_1)I_3 + c_1 J_3\), and set \(M = K \otimes A\), so that the
correlation between component \(c\) at visit \(t\) and component \(c'\) at
visit \(s\) is \(K_{cc'}A_{ts}\).

\textbf{Validity.} The eigenvalues of \(M\) are the products of those of \(K\)
and \(A\).

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(K\) has eigenvalues \(1 + 2c_1\) and \(1 - c_1\) (twice), so it is
  positive definite for \(-\tfrac12 < c_1 < 1\).
\item
  \(A\) is the correlation matrix of a stationary Ornstein-Uhlenbeck
  process at the visit times, and is positive definite for any
  distinct times.
\end{itemize}

Hence \(M\) is positive definite for every schedule. Generatively,
\(\mathbf X = L_A\mathbf Z L_K^\top\) with \(L_AL_A^\top = A\) and
\(L_KL_K^\top = K\): each column of \(L_A\mathbf Z\) is an independent AR(1)
path, and the components are fixed mixtures of them.

\textbf{The feasibility bound.} With the biomarker correlated with the
drug-response component only, its correlation vector is
\(c_{bm}\tilde g\), with \(\tilde g = e_3 \otimes g\) and \(g\) the
correlation profile. Since \(M^{-1} = K^{-1} \otimes A^{-1}\),

\[
\tilde g^\top M^{-1} \tilde g = [K^{-1}]_{33}\, g^\top A^{-1} g,
\qquad
[K^{-1}]_{33} = \frac{1 + c_1}{(1 - c_1)(1 + 2c_1)} = \frac{1}{1 - R^2}.
\]

\textbf{The schedule term.} The sampled process is Markov, with
step correlation \(\rho_t = \rho^{w_t - w_{t-1}}\), so

\[
g^\top A^{-1} g = g_1^2 + \sum_{t=2}^{n}
  \frac{(g_t - \rho_t g_{t-1})^2}{1 - \rho_t^2}.
\]

For a binary correlation profile, each step from visit \(t-1\) to visit
\(t\) contributes the following term:

{\def\LTcaptype{none} % do not increment counter
\begin{longtable}[]{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule\noalign{}
Step & Term \\
\midrule\noalign{}
\endhead
\bottomrule\noalign{}
\endlastfoot
on to on & \((1 - \rho_t)/(1 + \rho_t)\) \\
on to off & \(\rho_t^2/(1 - \rho_t^2)\) \\
off to on & \(1/(1 - \rho_t^2)\) \\
off to off & 0 \\
\end{longtable}
}

Under the decaying correlation, the off-drug correlation decays
instead of switching, which lowers the on-to-off term. This is why the
feasibility bound under the decaying correlation rises with the
half-life. Under compound symmetry it rises from 0.256 without
carryover to 0.307 at 1 week and 0.369 at 2 weeks, which makes
\(c_{bm} = 0.3\) feasible from 1 week (Figure \ref{fig:fig4b}).

\textbf{The two-constant form.} With a common \(\rho\), setting the
same-visit cross-component correlation to \(c_1\) and the different-visit
correlation to \(c_\times A_{ts}\) gives

\[
M = K_a \otimes A + K_b \otimes I_n, \qquad
K_a = (1 - c_\times)I_3 + c_\times J_3, \qquad
K_b = (c_1 - c_\times)(J_3 - I_3).
\]

\(K_b\) has zero diagonal and so is indefinite. The eigenvalues of \(M\)
are \(\omega_j(A)(1 + 2c_\times) + 2(c_1 - c_\times)\) and
\(\omega_j(A)(1 - c_\times) - (c_1 - c_\times)\), where \(\omega_j(A)\) are
the eigenvalues of \(A\). Hence \(M\) is positive definite if and only if
\(\omega_{\min}(A) > (c_1 - c_\times)/(1 - c_\times)\). For visits at a
common spacing of \(s\) weeks, \(\omega_{\min}(A)\) decreases toward
\((1 - \rho^s)/(1 + \rho^s)\) as visits accumulate, which is 0.111 at
weekly spacing and \(\rho = 0.8\) (the threshold itself) and 0.016 at
daily spacing. The values for the paper's schedules are in Stage 5.

\textbf{The original structure.} The original compound-symmetry form is
\(M = K_o \otimes I_n + K_p \otimes J_n\), with a person-level factor
\(K_p = \rho_{cs} I_3 + c_\times(J_3 - I_3)\) and an occasion-level factor
\(K_o = (1 - \rho_{cs})I_3 + (c_1 - c_\times)(J_3 - I_3)\). It is positive
definite if and only if \((1 - \rho_{cs}) - (c_1 - c_\times) > 0\), whatever
the schedule (0.10 at the original values, which is also the smallest
eigenvalue of the original eight-visit response block).

\textbf{The summed response.} With component standard deviations \(s_t\) and
\(S_t = \sum_c s_{c,t}X_{c,t}\), separability gives

\[
\mathrm{Cov}(S_t, S_s) = (s_t^\top K s_s)\,A_{ts},
\]

proportional to the AR(1) kernel when the standard deviations are
constant. The two-constant form adds
\((s_t^\top K_b s_t)\,\mathbf 1[t = s] > 0\), a white-noise term.

These results are derived in full, and checked numerically, in the
pmsimstats-ng documentation (\texttt{docs/35-separable-response-covariance.md}).

\subsection*{\texorpdfstring{A.6 The responder-class reading of \(c_{bm}\)}{A.6 The responder-class reading of c\_\{bm\}}}\label{a.6-the-responder-class-reading-of-c_bm}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{A.6 The responder-class reading of \(c_{bm}\)}

Let \(Z_i \in \{0, 1\}\) be a latent responder class with
\(\Pr(Z_i = 1) = p\), and let \(B_i = \mu_B(Z_i) + e_{B,i}\) and
\(BR_i = \mu_{BR}(Z_i) + e_{BR,i}\) for the on-drug drug response, with
\(e_B\) and \(e_{BR}\) independent of each other and of \(Z\). Then
\(\mathrm{Cov}(B, BR) = \mathrm{Cov}(\mu_B(Z), \mu_{BR}(Z))\). Both
\(\mu_B(Z)\) and \(\mu_{BR}(Z)\) are affine in the binary \(Z\), so their
correlation is \(\pm 1\) and
\(\lvert\mathrm{Cov}(B, BR)\rvert = \mathrm{SD}(\mu_B(Z))\,\mathrm{SD}(\mu_{BR}(Z))\).
Dividing by \(\sigma_B\sigma_{BR}\),

\[
c_{bm}^2 = \frac{\mathrm{Var}(\mu_B(Z))}{\sigma_B^2}\cdot
  \frac{\mathrm{Var}(\mu_{BR}(Z))}{\sigma_{BR}^2} = f_B\, f_{BR},
\]

the product of the between-class variance fractions. With more than
two classes the correlation of \(\mu_B(Z)\) and \(\mu_{BR}(Z)\) is at most
1 in magnitude, so \(c_{bm}^2 \le f_B f_{BR}\).

\subsection*{B.5 The extended DGP in pmsimstats-ng}\label{b.5-the-extended-dgp-in-pmsimstats-ng}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{B.5 The extended DGP in pmsimstats-ng}

The package function \texttt{buildSigma()} (\texttt{R/generateData.R}) implements
the decaying correlation, with \texttt{c.bm} on drug,
\texttt{c.bm\ *\ exp(-lambda\_cor\ *\ tsd)} off drug after exposure and zero
before exposure. It implements AR(1) within each component in
calendar weeks. It decays carryover on the drug-response mean from the
last on-drug value (not the original rule of B.3). It implements the
prognostic correlations of Stage 6: with the placebo-response
component (\texttt{c.bm.pb}), with the natural-history component as a
constant correlation (\texttt{c.bm.tv}) or as a mean shift growing along the
natural-history curve (\texttt{b.bm.tv}), and with baseline severity
(\texttt{c.bm.bl}). The first three enter the biomarker row of the
correlation matrix and share the joint bound of Table
\ref{tab:jointceiling}. The mean shift does not enter the matrix and
has no bound.

\textbf{The cross-component block.} \texttt{buildSigma()} builds the
cross-component correlation from two constants: \texttt{c.cf1t} at the same
visit and \texttt{c.cfct\ *\ rho\^{}tg} at a lag of \texttt{tg} weeks. With
\texttt{c.cf1t\ =\ c.cfct\ =\ c\_1} this is exactly the separable form of Table
\ref{tab:spec}, \(c_1\rho^{\lvert w_t - w_s\rvert}\) at every lag, since
\(\rho^0 = 1\). With the two constants unequal it is the two-constant
form of Stage 5. The extended DGP is therefore the package with both
constants set to 0.2, which is how the project's carryover driver
calls it (\texttt{analysis/scripts/carryover-sensitivity/simulation-core.R}).
Study 3's driver builds the separable block directly
(\texttt{C1\ *\ A} in \texttt{11-strawman-stress-test.R}).

\textbf{Allocation and carryover in study 3.} The driver takes
\texttt{-\/-allocation\ fixed\textbar{}random} and \texttt{-\/-carryover\ anchored\textbar{}recursive}. The
tables in Stage 6 are from the first run, with fixed allocation
(18, 18, 17, 17) and the original carryover rule (\texttt{recursive}). The
rerun used carryover decaying from the last on-drug mean (\texttt{anchored})
and randomized allocation. It changed no null rejection rate beyond
Monte Carlo error and no prognostic verdict. Its power values pooled
with the first run's are quoted once, in the paragraph on power and
robustness. The extended DGP as defined uses fixed allocation. The
rerun's randomized allocation is a driver option that is not part of
the definition.

\textbf{Stop instead of repair.} \texttt{buildSigma()}, \texttt{generateData()} and
\texttt{generateSimulatedResults()} accept a \texttt{stopOnNonPD} argument, \texttt{TRUE}
by default. A non-positive-definite covariance matrix then raises an
error, exactly as in B.2, both at the explicit positive-definiteness
check and in the Cholesky-factorization fallback that previously
repaired regardless of the \texttt{makePositiveDefinite} setting. The
original DGP's repair is reproduced only by passing
\texttt{stopOnNonPD\ =\ FALSE} explicitly, as the scripts that study the repair
itself do. The argument was first added with a \texttt{FALSE} default for
backward compatibility. The default was changed after two runs of the
companion carryover study repaired infeasible cells and reported the
attenuated interaction as a power loss.

\textbf{Not in the package.} The measurement-error, Weibull-decay and
