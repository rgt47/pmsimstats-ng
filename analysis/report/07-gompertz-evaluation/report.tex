\section*{Abstract}\label{abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

\textbf{Background.} Simulation-based power analyses for aggregated N-of-1
trials need a parametric template for the within-participant
response trajectory. The pmsimstats framework uses a modified Gompertz
curve for each latent response component. The template is fixed before
any analysis is run, and every reported power figure is conditional on
it. No published N-of-1 simulation study has compared response-curve
families.

\textbf{Methods.} We compared the modified Gompertz curve with three
alternatives: the symmetric logistic, the hyperbolic tangent and a
piecewise-linear breakpoint model. Each was matched to the Gompertz
curve at the asymptote and at a week-5 anchor. We simulated the
two-path open-label plus blinded-discontinuation design with
\(N = 70\) participants, no carryover and the prazosin reference
parameters. The interaction was encoded by mean moderation and,
separately, by covariance moderation. The four families used common
random numbers. We report paired differences in the rejection rate of
the biomarker-by-treatment interaction test with paired Monte Carlo
standard errors. We report Type I error from 2,000
null replicates per family and bias against the closed-form value
\(-c_{bm}\sigma_{BR}/\sigma_{bm} = -0.2344\).

\textbf{Results.} At the sampled visits the calibrated logistic curve departs from the Gompertz curve by at most 0.12 points. The two families returned the same decision in all 1,000 seeds under both encodings. The piecewise-linear family differed from Gompertz in 1 seed under mean moderation and 1 under covariance moderation. The hyperbolic tangent is the logistic curve at twice the rate. It had lower power by 0.010 (paired MCSE 0.003) under mean moderation and by 0.008 (paired MCSE 0.003) under covariance moderation. The covariance encoding was nearly but not exactly inert: the families disagreed on the decision in 8 of 1,000 seeds. Type I error was 0.034 (MCSE 0.002), 68 percent of the nominal 0.05. Bias against the closed-form value was within 0.0014 points per biomarker unit in every cell.

\textbf{Conclusions.} Families that coincide at the sampled visits give the
same decisions. The only detectable family effect is about one
percentage point of power, confined to the hyperbolic tangent, and it
does not depend on the interaction encoding. The result is conditional
on a visit schedule that samples the discriminating region at two
visits. The modified Gompertz default can be retained at this design.
Comparisons between data-generating processes should be paired by
common random numbers. An earlier unpaired version of this study
reported a 0.039 spread that was Monte Carlo noise.

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

\subsection{The question}\label{the-question}

The pmsimstats simulation framework supports power analyses for
aggregated N-of-1 trials of biomarker-treatment interactions. It
operationalizes the design family of Hendrickson et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{1}}. The latent drug-response trajectory in that
framework follows a modified Gompertz curve. The Gompertz curve is
established in tumor growth dynamics\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-laird1964dynamics}{2},\citeproc{ref-norton1988gompertzian}{3}}, microbial growth modeling\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-zwietering1990modeling}{4}} and the unified-Richards family of growth
curves\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-richards1959flexible}{5}--\citeproc{ref-tjorve2017}{7}}. Its
three-parameter form represents asymmetric saturation, which linear,
exponential and symmetric logistic curves cannot.

This paper asks whether the choice of family changes the inference
that the framework reports. We compare the Gompertz curve with three
alternatives in a simulation study. The comparators are the symmetric
logistic, the hyperbolic tangent and a piecewise-linear breakpoint
model. We report power, Type I error and bias of the
biomarker-by-treatment interaction estimate under the standard
pmsimstats linear mixed-effects analysis.

The authors of the present paper overlap with the authors of the
evaluated framework\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{1}}. The present paper evaluates a
modeling choice in that framework; it does not re-evaluate the
framework's published results.

\subsection{What the trajectory template is for}\label{what-the-trajectory-template-is-for}

A power simulation for an aggregated N-of-1 trial turns a design into
a distribution. The design is a visit schedule with a drug state and
an expectancy at each visit. The schedule carries no magnitudes. The
trajectory template maps elapsed time onto an expected symptom
reduction. It is the only place in the data-generating process (DGP)
where the size and time course of the treatment response are set.

The template is applied once per latent component, and each
application reads a different clock. The drug-response component BR
is evaluated at cumulative time on drug, \(t_{od}\). The
placebo-response component PB is evaluated at accumulated time under
nonzero expectancy and is scaled by the visit expectancy \(\eta_t\).
The natural-history component TV is evaluated at calendar time. The
observed score is their sum plus a correlated error. A
blinded-discontinuation block advances calendar time and belief time
while freezing time on drug, so the three components are separable by
design.

The template is not estimated. The analysis model is linear in
calendar time and in the continuous drug indicator \(D_{bc,it}\). It
has no parameter for response shape. The template is a fixed nuisance
structure that enters every cell of every parameter grid. No
diagnostic inside a standard run would reveal a poor choice.

Figure \ref{fig:fig-families} shows the four families at the
calibration this paper uses. The piecewise-linear curve rises
linearly and saturates at week
9.3. The hyperbolic
tangent is the most delayed early and the steepest through its
inflection. The Gompertz and logistic curves lie between the two and
are nearly indistinguishable. All four are pinned to the same
asymptote and the same week-5 value, so they cross there, and the room
left for them to differ is small. Across the six on-drug durations the
design samples, the largest departure of any family from the Gompertz
reference is 1.32
points at the first two visits, against a BR residual standard
deviation of 8 points. From week 12 onward the largest
departure is 0.35
points. The response has saturated by the third on-drug visit, and the
blinded-discontinuation block adds visits that are nearly identical in
time on drug. The family question at this design is a question about
the first eight weeks on drug. Any answer we report is conditional on
a visit schedule that samples the discriminating region twice.

\subsection{How the framework treats the template}\label{how-the-framework-treats-the-template}

The framework treats the trajectory template as a fixed calibration
input\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{1}}. The package ships one response-parameter set
as data, the \texttt{extracted\_rp} object, with one \((m, d, r, \sigma)\)
quadruple per component. Its documentation describes the set as a
`tabula rasa' chosen so that the three components together could
plausibly reproduce the authors' prazosin-PTSD randomized trial\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-raskind2013}{8}}. The drug-response component has
\(m_{BR} = 10.99\) CAPS-IV points,
\(d_{BR} = 5\) and \(r_{BR} = 0.42\) per week. The
natural-history and placebo-response components share one parameter
set, because the documentation declines to assume a difference between
them. Published sensitivity analyses sweep the parameters of the
Gompertz family. The family itself is held fixed. The sweeps explore
the interior of the family and never test its boundary.

The estimand is the biomarker-by-drug interaction, not the treatment
effect. The analysis routine returns the coefficient of
\texttt{bm:Dbc}. The drug main effect is carried as an adjustment and is not
reported. Power here means power to detect that the treatment effect
varies with the biomarker. The treatment main effect is the subject of
the companion manuscript at
\texttt{analysis/report/04-treatment-main-effect/}.

The template governs the treatment effect: it sets the BR mean curve,
which is the \(D_{bc}\) channel. Whether the template can reach the
interaction depends on how the biomarker moderation is attached to the
trajectory. That attachment is the architecture choice, not a property
of the template. Under the covariance-moderation architecture of
Hendrickson et al.~the moderation is a biomarker-response correlation
in the covariance matrix, and the template populates only the mean
vector. Under the mean-moderation architecture the moderation
multiplies the trajectory. We simulate both.

\subsection{Why the question matters}\label{why-the-question-matters}

First, the family is fixed before any analysis-side question is asked.
A simulation that draws every trajectory from a Gompertz curve cannot
detect the cost of that choice. Only a comparative simulation that
varies the DGP family while holding the analysis fixed can quantify
it. This is the logic of analysis-model misspecification studies\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morris2019simulation}{9}}, applied one level down in the simulation
hierarchy. Sigmoidal mixed models fitted directly to longitudinal data
show that a logistic-family curve recovers mean levels even under
misspecification\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-capuano2016sigmoidal}{10}}. The corresponding question
for a simulation DGP has not been asked.

Second, the Gompertz family has known identifiability problems in
short noisy trajectories. Joint estimation of \((m, d, r)\) is poorly
conditioned: the asymptote and rate correlate strongly, and the
displacement is weakly identified when few visits fall in the
inflection region.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-tjorve2010richards}{6}} and\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-tjorve2017}{7}} propose
unified-Richards reparameterizations with interpretable and less
correlated parameters.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-jagadeesan2023sloppiness}{11}} formalize the
`sloppiness' diagnostic.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-benzekry2014classical}{12}} fitted eight
tumor-growth models to two in vivo systems and ranked them on fit,
identifiability and prediction. That comparison has not been made for
short N-of-1 trajectories. We do not make it here; see Section
\ref{contrib}.

Third, the default reflects historical borrowing. The Gompertz form
entered the framework from the growth-curve tradition\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-laird1964dynamics}{2},\citeproc{ref-zwietering1990modeling}{4}}. The pharmacology
literature defaults to the Hill and sigmoid-Emax family\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hill1910}{13},\citeproc{ref-goutelle2008hill}{14}} and to indirect-response models\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-dayneka1993indirect}{15}}. The inheritance is not evidence, and the
question merits a direct answer.

\subsection{What the N-of-1 simulation literature does}\label{what-the-n-of-1-simulation-literature-does}

Several N-of-1 simulators specify an explicit response form. Percha et
al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-percha2019robust}{16}} built a stochastic time-series model with an
exponential approach to a treatment-driven target, separate wash-in
and wash-out time constants, and released the software. Gärtner et
al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-gartner2023comparison}{17}} simulate aggregated N-of-1 trials with
carryover from a causal graph. Liao et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-liao2023bayesian}{18}}
simulate several lag shapes for a distributed-lag analysis. Wang and
Schork\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-wang2019power}{19}} derive power under an AR(1) error structure
with carryover. The Hendrickson framework\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{1}}
introduces the Gompertz template. Each of these fixes one response
form. None compares forms. The CONSORT extension for N-of-1 trials\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-vohraCONSORTExtensionReporting2015}{20}} and the programmatic statements\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-lillie2011}{21},\citeproc{ref-schork2023precision}{22}} do not address trajectory shape.

In adjacent literatures the comparison is routine. Oncology modelers
fit several growth families and compare them on identifiability and
prediction\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-benzekry2014classical}{12}}. Pharmacometrics has sensitivity
studies for sigmoid-Emax and indirect-response models\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ebling1996feasibility}{23},\citeproc{ref-triggs1996levodopa}{24}}. Latent-class
growth-trajectory models treat response-curve heterogeneity by
mixtures\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hockenberry2017symptom}{25}}. The present paper applies a
four-family comparison to N-of-1 simulation with the Gompertz form as
the reference, reported in the ADEMP framework of Morris, White and
Crowther\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morris2019simulation}{9}}.

\subsection{What this paper contributes}\label{contrib}

Section 2 characterizes the modified Gompertz family as implemented in
pmsimstats. Section 3 presents the three alternatives at matched
asymptote and anchor, and shows what the calibration leaves at the
sampled visits. Sections 4 and 5 report a Monte Carlo study under
common random numbers. Section 6 interprets the result and states its
limits.

The sloppiness diagnostic of\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-jagadeesan2023sloppiness}{11}} motivates the
identifiability framing above but is not computed here. It is Study 2
of the working protocol (\texttt{00-ademp-protocol.md}) and is future work.
The full mathematical and historical context is in the companion
document \texttt{docs/legacy/25-gompertz-model-evaluation.md}.

\section{The modified Gompertz function in pmsimstats}\label{the-modified-gompertz-function-in-pmsimstats}

The Gompertz curve originates in Gompertz's law of mortality\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-gompertz1825}{26}} and was first used as a growth curve by\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-winsor1932gompertz}{27}}. The framework uses the monotone-saturating form.
Each latent component (BR, PB, TV) has a mean

\begin{equation}
G(t; m, d, r) = \frac{m\exp\!\left(-d\exp(-r t)\right) - m\exp(-d)}
                     {1 - \exp(-d)},
\label{eq:gompertz}
\end{equation}

where \(m\) is the asymptote (points), \(d\) the displacement (none) and
\(r\) the rate (per week). The raw curve \(m\exp(-d\exp(-rt))\) takes the
value \(m\exp(-d)\) at \(t = 0\). The implementation subtracts that
offset and rescales so that \(G(0) = 0\) and \(G(\infty) = m\). The code
names are \texttt{maxr}, \texttt{disp} and \texttt{rate}.

The curve is asymmetric about its inflection at
\(t^\ast = \ln(d)/r\). At the reference parameters \(t^\ast =
3.83\) weeks, where the curve has reached
36 percent of its asymptote. It
then needs 11.0 more
weeks to reach 99 percent. The Gompertz asymmetry is a short
acceleration phase followed by a long deceleration tail.

The prazosin reference cell uses the \texttt{extracted\_rp} set:
\(m_{BR} = 10.99\) CAPS-IV points,
\(d_{BR} = 5\), \(r_{BR} = 0.42\) per week, and a
within-visit residual standard deviation of 8 points. The
placebo-response and natural-history components share
\(m = 6.51\),
\(d = 5\) and \(r = 0.35\). The full set is documented with the
\texttt{extracted\_rp} data object.

\section{Alternative response-curve families}\label{alternative-response-curve-families}

Each alternative is parameterized so that its asymptote equals \(m_{BR}\)
and its value at the anchor \(t = 5\) weeks equals the Gompertz value
\(G(5) = 5.92\) points. The logistic and
hyperbolic-tangent rates are fixed at 0.5 per week and the inflection
time is solved by \texttt{uniroot}. The breakpoint is solved in closed form.
The families therefore differ in smoothness and symmetry while sharing
the same effect size at the anchor.

\subsection{Symmetric logistic}\label{symmetric-logistic}

The logistic curve\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-verhulst1838}{28}} is

\begin{equation}
L(t; m, r, t_0) = \frac{m}{1 - \ell_0}\left[\frac{1}{1 + \exp(-r(t - t_0))} - \ell_0\right],
\qquad \ell_0 = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(r t_0)},
\label{eq:logistic}
\end{equation}

with the offset at \(t = 0\) subtracted and the asymptote restored by the
rescaling, as in Equation \eqref{eq:gompertz}. It is symmetric about
its inflection at \(t_0 = 4.30\)
weeks at the calibration. At the calibrated parameters the logistic
curve lies above the Gompertz curve on almost the whole range and
reaches 50, 90 and 99 percent of the asymptote at
4.7,
8.9 and
13.7 weeks, against
4.7,
9.2 and
14.8 weeks for Gompertz.

\subsection{Hyperbolic tangent}\label{hyperbolic-tangent}

The hyperbolic-tangent curve is

\begin{equation}
H(t; m, r, t_0) = \frac{m}{2}\left(1 + \tanh(r(t - t_0))\right),
\label{eq:tanh}
\end{equation}

offset and rescaled as above. Because \(\tfrac{1}{2}(1 + \tanh x) =
1/(1 + e^{-2x})\), this is the logistic curve at rate \(2r\). The
implementation confirms the identity: the largest absolute difference
between \(H\) at rate 0.5 and \(L\) at rate 1.0 with the same \(t_0\) on a
grid over 0 to 20 weeks is
\(1.8e-15\). The family is
not a distinct shape. Its role in this study is a logistic curve with
twice the calibrated rate, which gives the steepest rise of the three
smooth curves. We found no source for a hyperbolic-tangent time-course
model in pharmacokinetics and make no such claim.

\subsection{Piecewise-linear breakpoint}\label{piecewise-linear-breakpoint}

The piecewise-linear breakpoint curve, formalized in the broken-line
regression literature\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-muggeo2003}{29}}, is

\begin{equation}
P(t; m, t_{bp}) = m\min(1, t/t_{bp}),
\label{eq:plb}
\end{equation}

a linear ramp to the breakpoint \(t_{bp}\) and constant afterward. The
post-breakpoint slope is zero throughout. At the calibration
\(t_{bp} = 9.28\)
weeks. The breakpoint is the only non-differentiable feature across
the four families.

the Gompertz curve, which the logistic and piecewise-linear curves

can find and is the reason for the result in Section 5.

\section{Simulation design}\label{simulation-design}

\textbf{Estimand and performance measures.} The estimand is the
biomarker-by-treatment interaction coefficient \(\beta_{bm:D}\) from the
linear mixed-effects model fitted by \texttt{nlme::lme} with the formula
\texttt{Sx\ \textasciitilde{}\ bm\ +\ t\ +\ Dbc\ +\ bm:Dbc}, a random intercept (\texttt{random\ =\ \textasciitilde{}1\textbar{}ptID})
and a continuous-time AR(1) residual correlation
(\texttt{corCAR1(form\ =\ \textasciitilde{}t\textbar{}ptID)}). The \(p\)-value is the Wald \(t\) test from
the \texttt{nlme} summary, which uses containment degrees of freedom with no
small-sample correction. The primary outputs are power for
\(H_0: \beta_{bm:D} = 0\) at \(\alpha = 0.05\) and Type I error at
\(\beta_{bm:D} = 0\). Secondary outputs are the mean and empirical
standard deviation of \(\hat\beta_{bm:D}\), the mean model-based
standard error, and bias against the closed-form reference below.
Proportions carry binomial MCSEs; means carry the standard error of the
replicate mean.

\textbf{Sign convention.} The components are non-negative reductions in
symptom severity, so an increase in any component lowers \(Y\). The
interaction coefficient is therefore negative, and the moderation
parameter is positive.

\textbf{Reference value.} With the analysis on the raw biomarker and
\(D_{bc,it}\) binary at \(t_{1/2} = 0\), the population slope of the
on-drug drug response on the biomarker is
\(-c_{bm}\,\sigma_{BR}/\sigma_{bm} = -0.45 \times 8 /
15.36 = -0.2344\) points per
biomarker unit. This is the conditional mean of \(BR\) given \(B_i\) under
covariance moderation and the shift under constant-scaled mean
moderation. Under trajectory-scaled mean moderation the per-visit
multiplier is normalized so that its on-drug mean is \(\sigma_{BR}\), so
the same value is the visit-averaged target; the LME's weighting across
visits is the only reason it is approximate. We report bias against
this value in every alternative cell.

\textbf{Design and parameters.} The trial design is the two-path open-label
plus blinded-discontinuation (OL+BDC) design: visits at weeks 4, 8, 12,
16, 17, 18, 19 and 20; open label at the first four visits; blinded
discontinuation at week 18 (path A) or week 19 (path B); no crossover.
This is not the four-path Hybrid design of the companion manuscripts.
\(N = 70\) is the total across the two paths, 35 per path. Carryover is
absent (\(t_{1/2} = 0\)) to isolate trajectory shape. The within-component
AR(1) correlation is 0.7 and the cross-component correlations are 0.2
at the same visit and 0.1 across visits. The response and biomarker
parameters are the prazosin reference sets \texttt{extracted\_rp} and
\texttt{extracted\_bp}.

\textbf{Factors.} Four families crossed with two architectures at
\(c_{bm} = 0.45\), and four families at \(c_{bm} = 0\). At \(c_{bm} = 0\) the
two architectures build the same covariance matrix and apply no mean
shift, so the null DGP is the same under both. We run it once per
family. The driver confirms on 20 seeds that the two architectures
return identical fits at the null.

\textbf{Notation for the moderation parameter.} \(c_{bm}\) is used as a
common label for the moderation strength at a matched numeric value.
Strictly, it is the biomarker-response correlation under covariance
moderation and the multiplier \(\beta_{bm}\) under mean moderation. The
two are on a common scale because the mean shift is
\(\beta_{bm}\,b_i\,\sigma_{BR}\) on the standardized biomarker \(b_i\).

\textbf{Family encoding.} The family sets the BR mean curve inside the
covariance construction via the \texttt{br\_family} argument of \texttt{buildSigma}.
Under mean moderation the interaction is the multiplicative form of
the companion manuscript at
\texttt{analysis/report/01-dgp-mean-moderation-vs-mvn/},
\(Y_{it} = \mu_0 + \beta_1 D_{it}(1 + \beta_{bm} b_i) + \varepsilon_{it}\),
so the biomarker-dependent part of the treatment effect follows the
response trajectory (\texttt{moderation\_scaling\ =\ \textquotesingle{}trajectory\textquotesingle{}}). The
per-visit moderation is normalized so its on-drug mean equals
\(\sigma_{BR}\). Under covariance moderation the interaction is the
biomarker-response correlation in the BM-BR block, and the family
enters only through the mean curve.

\textbf{Common random numbers.} Every family and both architectures use
the same seed for the same replicate index. The seed is set immediately
before the multivariate-normal draw, and the covariance matrix has the
same dimension in every cell, so the standard-normal matrix \(Z\) is
identical across families. Family contrasts are therefore paired. For
rejection decisions the paired standard error of a difference in
proportions is \(\sqrt{b + c}/n\), where \(b\) and \(c\) are the numbers of
seeds on which the two families disagree in each direction. For
estimates it is the standard error of the mean paired difference.
Morris, White and Crowther\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morris2019simulation}{9}} recommend this
design for comparing methods on the same data sets. The same logic
applies to comparing DGPs that differ only in a nuisance input.

\textbf{Replicates.} 1,000
seeds per family and architecture at the alternative, and
2,000
seeds per family at the null. Fits are run by \texttt{parallel::mclapply} on
8 workers.

The working protocol at
\texttt{analysis/scripts/gompertz-evaluation/00-ademp-protocol.md} describes a
144-cell grid and two further sub-studies. The executed study is the
focused factorial above. Departures from the protocol are recorded in
\texttt{02-deviations-log.md}. The study is exploratory, and its findings
should be confirmed in an independent study.

present design runs each null DGP once on twice as many seeds.

model, a random intercept plus a CAR(1) residual, and the DGP, which
has three AR(1) components with a placebo-response variance that
scales with expectancy. We have not tested this conjecture. The companion manuscript at
\texttt{analysis/report/10-interaction-test-calibration/} studies the
calibration of this test directly.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

\subsection{The supported result}\label{the-supported-result}

Families that coincide at the sampled visits give the same decisions.
The calibrated logistic curve equals the Gompertz curve to within
0.12 points at every
visit the design samples, and the two families returned the same
decision in every seed under both architectures. The piecewise-linear
curve is within
0.46
points and differed in one seed. The hyperbolic tangent, which is the
logistic curve at twice the rate, differs by about one point at weeks
4 and 8 and lowers power by about 0.01 under both architectures. That
is the whole family effect at this design. It does not depend on the
interaction encoding, and its direction is the same under both.

The earlier version of this manuscript reported a 0.039 spread under
mean moderation, with Gompertz the lowest-power family, and an exactly
inert covariance architecture. Both claims were wrong. The spread was
the noise of independent seeding at 1,000 replicates per cell. The
covariance architecture is nearly but not exactly inert (Section
\ref{inert}). The method that exposed the error, common random
numbers with paired contrasts, is the one that between-DGP comparisons
should use.

\subsection{Why the covariance architecture is nearly inert}\label{inert}

Under covariance moderation the interaction is the biomarker-response
correlation in the BM-BR block. The family enters only the BR mean
curve. If the mean curve were in the column space of the fixed
effects, the interaction test would be exactly invariant to it. It is
not. The fixed effects are an intercept, the biomarker, linear time,
\(D_{bc}\) and their product. The family-specific mean curve is a
function of visit and path that these columns do not span. The part
they do not span enters the residuals. It therefore enters the REML
estimates of the random-intercept variance, the residual variance and
the working CAR(1) parameter, and through them the generalized
least-squares weights and the test statistic. The effect is small
because the unspanned part is small: at most
1.32 points
against a residual standard deviation of 8.
It is not zero: the families disagreed on the decision in 8 of 1,000 seeds under covariance moderation, and the largest change in the \(p\)-value against Gompertz was 0.056.

Under mean moderation the family enters twice: through the BR mean
curve, as above, and through the per-visit multiplier on the
biomarker-dependent shift. The second channel is where a family effect
was expected. It is small for the same reason: the multipliers are
normalized to a common on-drug mean, and the families differ in their
visit profile by amounts that the Wald test, which averages over
visits, mostly removes.

\subsection{Design conditionality}\label{design-conditionality}

The result is a property of a visit schedule with three on-drug visits
at or beyond saturation. The design samples on-drug durations of 4, 8,
12, 16, 17 and 18 weeks. The Gompertz curve has reached
39,
84 and
97 percent of
its asymptote at the first three. The families differ only at the
first two. A design with weekly visits in weeks 1 to 8 would sample the
rising phase where the curves differ and could give a different
answer. So could a calibration that matched the asymptote and the time
to half-maximum but let the early slope differ. We have not run either.

\subsection{Conditions that would change the answer}\label{conditions-that-would-change-the-answer}

Four conditions, none exercised here, could enlarge the family effect.
First, smaller \(N\): the Wald test would integrate over fewer
participants, but the visit schedule would be the same, so we expect
little change. Second, a different \(c_{bm}\): at a power near 0.5 the
test is most sensitive to small shifts in the noncentrality parameter,
so the hyperbolic-tangent difference could be larger in absolute terms.
We have no cell at another \(c_{bm}\) and do not estimate the size.
Third, carryover: with \(t_{1/2} > 0\) the off-drug mean decays from the
last on-drug value, which differs by family at week 18 by at most
0.03 points, so we expect
little change at this design. Fourth, non-monotone shapes: a family
that peaks and declines within the trial window would produce a
different on-minus-off contrast and would move the test. The four
families here are all monotone-saturating. A matched-seed run at
\(t_{1/2} = 0.5\) weeks and at \(N = 35\) would cost about 15 minutes and
would answer the first and third conditions directly; we have not run
it.

\subsection{The prazosin reference cell}\label{the-prazosin-reference-cell}

For the prazosin-PTSD application that motivates the framework, the
present result implies that sensitivity to the Gompertz assumption is
small relative to sensitivity to the carryover specification (the
subject of \texttt{analysis/report/02-carryover-sensitivity/}) and to the
interaction encoding (the subject of
\texttt{analysis/report/01-dgp-mean-moderation-vs-mvn/}). At the reference
parameters and this design, trajectory family changes power by about
0.01 at most. Sensitivity-analysis effort should go to the carryover
and encoding axes.

\section{Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

First, at the two-path OL+BDC design with \(N = 70\) and the prazosin
reference parameters, the biomarker-by-treatment interaction test is
insensitive to the response-curve family to within one percentage
point of power. Families that coincide at the sampled visits give
identical decisions. The hyperbolic tangent, which is the logistic
curve at twice the calibrated rate, lowers power by about 0.01 under
both interaction encodings. The modified Gompertz default can be
retained.

Second, the covariance-moderation architecture is nearly but not
exactly inert to the family. The family-specific mean curve is not
spanned by the fixed effects, so it reaches the REML variance
components and the test statistic. At this design the effect is a
handful of discordant decisions in 1,000.

Third, the test runs below its nominal size at this design, at
0.034 against
0.05. The cause is not the degrees-of-freedom rule, which makes no
small-sample correction. The model-based standard error exceeds the
empirical standard deviation of the estimate; we conjecture a
working-model mismatch as the cause and have not tested it.

Fourth, comparisons between data-generating processes that differ in
a nuisance input must be paired by common random numbers. An unpaired
comparison at 1,000 replicates per cell has a standard error of about
0.019 on each contrast and produced a spurious 0.039 family effect in
the earlier version of this study.

Fifth, the result is conditional on a visit schedule that samples the
rising phase of the response at two visits. A design with denser early
visits, or a calibration that leaves the early slope free, is the
natural follow-up.

\section*{Glossary}\label{glossary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Glossary}

Terms are listed in the order in which the paper introduces them.
Labels in backticks are the identifiers used in the repository's
code, data files and table headings.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Trajectory template} (\texttt{br\_family}): the parametric curve that
  maps elapsed time onto the expected symptom reduction of a latent
  response component.
\item
  \textbf{Modified Gompertz curve} (\(G(t; m, d, r)\), \texttt{gompertz}): Equation
  \eqref{eq:gompertz}, with asymptote \(m\), displacement \(d\) and rate
  \(r\); the framework's default.
\item
  \textbf{Mean-moderation architecture} (\texttt{mean\_moderation}, ``Mean'' in
  tables): the biomarker scales the treatment effect in the
  conditional mean.
\item
  \textbf{Covariance-moderation architecture} (\texttt{mvn}, ``Covariance'' in
  tables): the biomarker-response correlation is present on drug and
  absent off drug; the interaction is in the covariance matrix.
\item
  \textbf{Moderation strength} (\(c_{bm}\), \texttt{c.bm}): under covariance
  moderation, the on-drug correlation between the biomarker and the
  drug response; under mean moderation, the multiplier \(\beta_{bm}\) on
  the standardized biomarker; the two are on a common scale.
\item
  \textbf{Anchor}: the week-5 value of the Gompertz curve to which every
  alternative family is matched.
\item
  \textbf{Trajectory-scaled moderation} (\texttt{moderation\_scaling\ =\ \textquotesingle{}trajectory\textquotesingle{}}): under mean moderation, the per-visit shift follows
  the drug-response mean curve, normalized so its on-drug mean is
  \(\sigma_{BR}\).
\item
  \textbf{Two-path OL+BDC design}: open-label titration at weeks 4 to 16,
  then blinded discontinuation at week 18 or week 19; two
  randomization paths; no crossover.
\item
  \textbf{Interaction coefficient} (\(\beta_{bm:D}\), \texttt{bm:Dbc}): the
  biomarker-by-treatment interaction in the analysis model; the
  estimand.
\item
  \textbf{Continuous drug indicator} (\(D_{bc,it}\), \texttt{Dbc}): 1 on drug,
  \(e^{-\lambda t_{sd}}\) off drug after exposure, 0 before exposure;
  binary here because \(t_{1/2} = 0\).
\item
  \textbf{Reference value}: the closed-form interaction slope
  \(-c_{bm}\sigma_{BR}/\sigma_{bm}\) against which bias is reported.
\item
  \textbf{Common random numbers}: the same seed for the same replicate
  index in every family and architecture, so that contrasts are paired.
\item
  \textbf{Discordant seeds}: seeds on which two families reach different
  rejection decisions; the paired MCSE of a power difference is
  \(\sqrt{b + c}/n\) with \(b\) and \(c\) the discordant counts in each
  direction.
\end{itemize}

It sets the seed to \texttt{810001\ +\ i\ -\ 1} for alternative replicate \(i\) and
\texttt{820001\ +\ i\ -\ 1} for null replicate \(i\), immediately before each
multivariate-normal draw, in every family and architecture. The family
is routed through the \texttt{br\_family} argument of \texttt{buildSigma}, and the
mean-moderation shift is trajectory-scaled by the
\texttt{moderation\_scaling\ =\ \textquotesingle{}trajectory\textquotesingle{}} argument of \texttt{generateData}; both
default to the original behavior, so other pmsimstats runs are
unchanged. Outputs are
\texttt{analysis/data/quick-sim/07-gompertz-crn-replicates.rds},
\texttt{07-gompertz-crn-paired.rds}, \texttt{07-gompertz-crn-meta.rds} and
\texttt{07-gompertz-crn-summary.txt}. A run on another platform is
reproducible in its seeds but not draw for draw, because the BLAS and
LAPACK differ; contrasts that must be paired have to be run on one
platform. The earlier unpaired driver and its outputs
(\texttt{02-faithful-trajectory-sweep.R}, \texttt{07-gompertz-faithful-*}) are kept
for the comparison in Section 5.1.

The ADEMP working protocol is at
\texttt{analysis/scripts/gompertz-evaluation/00-ademp-protocol.md}, and
departures from it are recorded in \texttt{02-deviations-log.md}. The
manuscript source, bibliography (\texttt{references.bib}) and CSL file are at
\texttt{analysis/report/07-gompertz-evaluation/}.
