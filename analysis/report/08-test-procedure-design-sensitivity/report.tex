\section{Abstract}\label{abstract}

\textbf{Background.} Inference about the biomarker-treatment interaction
in aggregated N-of-1 trials depends on the test procedure. The
classical repeated-measures ANOVA (RM-ANOVA) requires a dichotomized
biomarker. Mixed-effects models and generalized estimating equations
(GEE) do not. We separate the cost of dichotomization from the cost
of the test procedure.

\textbf{Methods.} We derive the closed-form strict RM-ANOVA \(F\)-statistic
for the biomarker-stratified treatment interaction in a balanced
split-plot design. We show that it is a two-sample \(t\)-test on
within-participant phase differences. We then ran a Monte Carlo
study reported under the ADEMP framework\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morris2019simulation}{1}}.
Six procedures were compared. Strict RM-ANOVA used a median-split
biomarker. A within-participant contrast kept the biomarker
continuous and otherwise matched RM-ANOVA. A linear mixed model
(LME) used continuous-time AR(1) residuals, once with model-based
inference and once with CR2 cluster-robust inference. Two GEE
analyses used the Liang-Zeger and the Mancl-DeRouen sandwich. The
design was a two-path open-label plus blinded discontinuation
schedule with \(N \in \{25, 35, 70, 100\}\) and an interaction
coefficient \(\beta_{bm} \in \{0, 0.45\}\). The data-generating
parameters were those published by Hendrickson et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{2}}. Each power
cell had 1000 replicates and each null cell
2000.

\textbf{Results.} Dichotomization cost 0.12 to 0.22 of absolute power.
Among procedures with nominal or conservative size, the test
procedure moved power by at most 0.09 relative to the
continuous-biomarker contrast. The observed loss agreed with Cohen's
\(2/\pi\) efficiency factor for a median split to within
1.9 MCSE. Power was modest at this effect
size: no procedure reached 0.80 at \(N \le 35\). The Liang-Zeger GEE
rejected at 6.8 to 11.3 percent under the null. The
Mancl-DeRouen sandwich with a \(t\) reference on \(K - 5\) degrees of
freedom rejected at 5.1 to 6.0
percent. Its power was
11 to 14 points
below the uncorrected GEE at \(N \le 35\). The LME with model-based
inference rejected at 2.8 to 3.6 percent. Its
standard error exceeded the empirical standard error by
7 to 11
percent. The same fit with CR2 inference rejected at
4.5 to 5.8 percent. Its power was
2.7 points below the
model-based fit at \(N = 25\) and
0.1 to 1.9 points above it
at larger \(N\).

\textbf{Conclusions.} Keeping the biomarker continuous is the most
consequential choice. The LME's conservatism is a working-covariance
calibration defect. Degrees-of-freedom corrections cannot repair it.
The cluster-robust CR2 standard error used by the companion papers
restores nominal size here. Uncorrected GEE should not be used at
these sample sizes.

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

\subsection{The question}\label{the-question}

An analyst testing a biomarker-treatment interaction in an
aggregated N-of-1 trial chooses a test procedure. The classical
choice is a repeated-measures ANOVA (RM-ANOVA) on a biomarker split
into high and low strata. The modern choices are a linear
mixed-effects model (LME) or generalized estimating equations (GEE)
with the biomarker kept continuous. The two routes differ in two
ways at once. The classical route dichotomizes the biomarker. The
modern route models the within-participant covariance. A direct
comparison of RM-ANOVA against a mixed model confounds the two.

This paper separates them. We add a fourth procedure, a
within-participant contrast regressed on the continuous biomarker.
It differs from strict RM-ANOVA only in keeping the biomarker
continuous. It differs from the mixed model only in collapsing each
phase to its mean. The step from RM-ANOVA to the contrast measures
the cost of dichotomization. The step from the contrast to the
mixed model measures the cost of the test procedure. We report both
steps across four sample sizes.

We also report the small-sample calibration of the four
continuous-biomarker procedures. GEE with the Liang-Zeger sandwich
is known to over-reject with few clusters. Bias-corrected sandwiches
exist, and the choice of reference distribution matters as much as
the correction. The LME with model-based inference can be
conservative when its working covariance is misspecified. We
tabulate size, power, coverage, and the calibration ratio \(\kappa\)
(mean model standard error over the empirical standard deviation of
the estimates) for each arm and cell.

\subsection{Why the interaction and the N-of-1 setting}\label{why-the-interaction-and-the-n-of-1-setting}

The biomarker-treatment interaction is harder to detect than the
average treatment effect. The PATH framework\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-kent2020path}{3},\citeproc{ref-kent2020pathee}{4}} distinguishes effect modeling from risk modeling.
The interaction is estimated from within-participant treatment
contrasts, and its precision depends on the number and placement of
off-drug visits. A test procedure that loses information on the
interaction will miss predictive-biomarker signal at sample sizes
that are adequate for the main effect.

Within-participant residuals in chronic-condition trials are
serially correlated on a timescale set by pharmacokinetics,
natural-history drift, and measurement reactivity. A continuous-time
AR(1) residual correlation represents this. It is not spherical in
the sense required by a classical analysis that keeps every visit
as a repeated level. Haverkamp and Beauducel\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-haverkamp2017sphericity}{5}} report through
simulation that uncorrected RM-ANOVA inflates Type I error under
sphericity violations. They also report that the
Greenhouse-Geisser-corrected RM-ANOVA and the multilevel model
perform comparably, and that the multilevel model is not uniformly
safer. Blanca et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-blanca2023sphericity}{6}} extend this with finer epsilon
resolution. The classical procedure we evaluate pre-averages each
phase to one value, so it has a two-level within-participant factor
and sphericity holds by construction. The serial correlation
affects it only through the variance of the phase difference.

\subsection{What the literature does and does not do}\label{what-the-literature-does-and-does-not-do}

Most published N-of-1 analyses ignore serial correlation.
Natesan Batley et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-natesanbatley2023}{7}} report that 83.8 percent of the studies they
catalog do so. Stunnenberg et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-stunnenberg2022}{8}} and Shaffer et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-shaffer2018}{9}} reach similar
conclusions in neurology and behavioral medicine, and
Defelippe et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-defelippe2023}{10}} in rare-disease applications. The CENT
reporting standards\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-vohraCONSORTExtensionReporting2015}{11},\citeproc{ref-shamseer2015cent}{12}} do not address the analytic-model decision, and
Kwasnicka et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-kwasnicka2019}{13}} rank it as the field's highest-priority
methodological problem.

The cost of a median split is a classical result.
Cohen\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-cohen1983dichotomization}{14}} gave the efficiency loss of dichotomizing
a normal predictor at the median as about 36 percent, the factor
\(2/\pi\) on the squared correlation. MacCallum et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-maccallum2002dichotomization}{15}}
documented the practice and its consequences at length.
Royston et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-royston2006dichotomizing}{16}} showed the power loss and residual
confounding in regression. The present paper observes that loss
once more, in the N-of-1 interaction setting, and compares it with
the \(2/\pi\) prediction.

Test-procedure comparisons for longitudinal data are not new.
Ma et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ma2012beyond}{17}} report that RM-ANOVA is less powerful than GEE and
mixed models for continuous longitudinal endpoints. The MMRM
literature\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-mallinckrodt2003}{18},\citeproc{ref-mallinckrodt2008recommendations}{19}}
addresses parallel-group trials with monotone missingness. The GEE
of Liang and Zeger\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-liang1986}{20}} over-rejects with few clusters, and several
bias-corrected sandwiches address this\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-mancl2001covariance}{21}--\citeproc{ref-wang2016geesmv}{24}}.
Pan and Wall\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-pan2002sandwich}{25}} proposed \(t\) and \(F\) references that account for
the variability of the sandwich. Li and Redden\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-li2015sandwich}{26}} compared the
corrections and concluded that the Wald \(z\) test should be avoided
with few clusters even with a bias-corrected sandwich, and that a
\(t\) reference is needed. Tong et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-tong2024stepwedge}{27}} report that in
stepped-wedge trials with fewer than six units only 6.6 percent of
analyses use a small-sample correction. None of these papers
addresses the biomarker-interaction estimand in an N-of-1 design.

Design optimization has been studied separately from
test-procedure choice. Hedges et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hedges2023power}{28}} give power computations for
\((AB)^k\) single-case designs. Bouwmeester and Jongerling\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-bouwmeester2020}{29}} document the effect
of serial correlation on randomization-test power.
Senn\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-senn1994crossover}{30}} and Senn et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-senn2011individual}{31}} show that replicate
crossover designs identify the patient-by-treatment interaction
variance. Hendrickson et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{2}} compare design families for the
biomarker-interaction question with one test procedure.
Blackston et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-blackstonComparisonAggregatedNof12019}{32}} compare aggregated N-of-1
trials against parallel and crossover designs with one mixed
model. Schork et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-schork2023precision}{33}} and Zucker et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-zucker2010}{34}} review the design
literature. Run-in selection is a separate controversy:
Berger et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-berger2003runin}{35}} show that response-based exclusion during a
placebo run-in biases validity, and the open-label titration used
here avoids it.

\subsection{What this paper contributes}\label{what-this-paper-contributes}

The paper makes three contributions.

First, we derive the closed-form strict RM-ANOVA \(F\)-statistic for
the biomarker-stratified treatment interaction in a balanced
split-plot design (Section \ref{sec-closed-form}). We show that it
is a two-sample \(t\)-test on within-participant phase differences and
that the reduction is exact for any within-participant covariance
once each phase is averaged. We relate it to the LME Wald test.

Second, we run a Monte Carlo study that separates the cost of
dichotomization from the cost of the test procedure (Sections
\ref{sec-design} and \ref{sec-results}). The continuous-biomarker
contrast is the instrument. The study uses the pmsimstats package
data-generating process under the mean-shift encoding of the
interaction, at a two-path open-label plus blinded discontinuation
design with no carryover.

Third, we tabulate the small-sample calibration of the four
continuous-biomarker procedures and relate it to the inference the
companion papers recommend (Sections \ref{sec-results} and
\ref{sec-recommendation}). The LME arm evaluated here is the
series' historical analysis with model-based inference. Its
conservatism is a calibration defect of the working covariance,
and the companion papers\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper02}{36},\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper10}{37}}
remedy it with a cluster-robust standard error. We add that arm and
report it beside the model-based LME.

\textbf{Notation for the interaction parameter.} The data-generating
process shifts the drug-response mean by the centered biomarker.
We write the interaction parameter as \(\beta_{bm}\), a dimensionless
multiplier on the standardized biomarker. The companion
architecture manuscript uses \(c_{bm}\) for the correlation encoding.
The two are calibrated so that \(0.45\) denotes a matched interaction
strength, but the symbols are not interchangeable. The biomarker is
generated on its published scale and standardized by its population
mean and SD before every analysis, so \(\beta_{bm:D}\) is the
interaction per biomarker SD.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
\ref{sec-closed-form} gives the derivation, adapted from
\texttt{docs/legacy/26-rm-anova-interaction-test.md}. Section
\ref{sec-design} gives the simulation design. Section
\ref{sec-results} reports the results. Section
\ref{sec-recommendation} gives the recommendation. Section
\ref{sec-discussion} discusses limitations and future work.

\section{Closed-form strict RM-ANOVA test of the biomarker-treatment interaction}\label{sec-closed-form}

This section derives the strict RM-ANOVA \(F\)-statistic for the
biomarker-treatment interaction in a balanced split-plot design and
relates it to the Wald \(t\)-statistic on the corresponding LME
coefficient. The derivation specializes the classical split-plot
analysis\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-maxwelldelaney2018}{38}} and the crossover treatment of
within-subject contrasts\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-senn2002}{39}} to the simplest design that
supports the test: two biomarker strata, two treatment phases, and \(T\)
within-phase timepoints. Three facts anchor the rest of the paper.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  Under balance, the strict RM-ANOVA test of the interaction is a
  two-sample \(t\)-test on the within-participant phase differences
  with \(2(n-1)\) degrees of freedom. The reduction is exact for any
  within-participant covariance once each phase is averaged.
\item
  The closed form gives a non-central \(F\) power formula.
\item
  The closed form assumes a categorical biomarker. The present
  study exercises that assumption and no other.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Notation and design}\label{notation-and-design}

We consider the following elements.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Two biomarker strata \(i \in \{1, 2\}\), formed by dichotomizing the
  continuous biomarker at a cut-point. Strict RM-ANOVA requires
  categorical predictors.
\item
  \(n\) participants per stratum, indexed \(l \in \{1, \ldots, n\}\).
\item
  Two treatment phases \(j \in \{1, 2\}\), drug and placebo. Every
  participant passes through both.
\item
  \(T\) within-phase timepoints, indexed \(k \in \{1, \ldots, T\}\). The
  analyst may pre-average them to one value per participant per
  phase.
\end{itemize}

The biomarker stratum is between participants. The phase and the
timepoints are within participants. This is the split-plot
repeated-measures design\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-maxwelldelaney2018}{38}}.

We write \(Y_{ijlk}\) for the outcome at stratum \(i\), phase \(j\),
participant \(l\), timepoint \(k\). The classical split-plot model is

\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
Y_{ijlk} = {} & \mu + \alpha_i + \beta_j + (\alpha\beta)_{ij}
+ \pi_{l(i)} \\
& + \tau_k + (\alpha\tau)_{ik} + (\beta\tau)_{jk}
+ (\alpha\beta\tau)_{ijk} + e_{ijlk},
\end{split}
\end{equation*}

with sum-to-zero constraints, a random participant effect
\(\pi_{l(i)} \sim N(0, \sigma_\pi^2)\) nested in stratum, and error
\(e_{ijlk} \sim N(0, \sigma_e^2)\). The model implies compound
symmetry of the within-participant covariance. The \(F\)-tests of
within-participant effects require only sphericity, the equality of
the variances of all pairwise differences among the levels of a
within-participant factor. Compound symmetry implies sphericity. A
within-participant factor with two levels is always spherical.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Sums of squares and the interaction \(F\)-statistic}{Sums of squares and the interaction F-statistic}}\label{sums-of-squares-and-the-interaction-f-statistic}

The total sum of squares splits into between- and within-participant
components:

\begin{equation}
SS_{\text{tot}} = SS_{\text{between}} + SS_{\text{within}} .
\label{eq:rmanova-ss-partition}
\end{equation}

The between component decomposes into \(SS_A\) (stratum) and
\(SS_{S(A)}\) (participant within stratum):

\begin{equation}
SS_A = bnT \sum_{i=1}^a (\bar Y_{i\cdots} - \bar Y_{\cdots})^2,
\quad
SS_{S(A)} = bT \sum_{i=1}^a \sum_{l=1}^n
(\bar Y_{i\cdot l\cdot} - \bar Y_{i\cdots})^2 .
\label{eq:rmanova-between}
\end{equation}

The within component decomposes into \(SS_B\) (phase), \(SS_{AB}\) (the
interaction), and the within error \(SS_{B \times S(A)}\):

\begin{equation}
SS_{AB} = nT \sum_{i,j} (\bar Y_{ij\cdot\cdot}
- \bar Y_{i\cdots} - \bar Y_{\cdot j\cdot\cdot}
+ \bar Y_{\cdots})^2 ,
\label{eq:rmanova-ss-ab}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
SS_{B \times S(A)} = T \sum_{i,j,l} (\bar Y_{ijl\cdot}
- \bar Y_{i\cdot l\cdot} - \bar Y_{ij\cdot\cdot}
+ \bar Y_{i\cdots})^2 .
\label{eq:rmanova-ss-error}
\end{equation}

The degrees of freedom are \(df_A = a - 1\), \(df_{S(A)} = a(n-1)\),
\(df_B = b - 1\), \(df_{AB} = (a-1)(b-1)\), and \(df_{B \times S(A)} =
a(n-1)(b-1)\). The interaction test is

\begin{equation}
F_{AB} = \frac{MS_{AB}}{MS_{B \times S(A)}}
= \frac{SS_{AB} / df_{AB}}
       {SS_{B \times S(A)} / df_{B \times S(A)}} .
\label{eq:rmanova-f}
\end{equation}

Under \(H_0: (\alpha\beta)_{ij} \equiv 0\) and sphericity,
\(F_{AB} \sim F(df_{AB}, df_{B \times S(A)})\).

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{The \(2 \times 2\) split-plot case}{The 2 \textbackslash times 2 split-plot case}}\label{the-2-times-2-split-plot-case}

We specialize to \(a = b = 2\) and \(T = 1\), so that the analyst
pre-averages within-phase observations to one value per participant
per phase. The sample interaction contrast is

\begin{equation}
\hat L = (\bar Y_{11\cdot} - \bar Y_{12\cdot})
- (\bar Y_{21\cdot} - \bar Y_{22\cdot}) .
\label{eq:rmanova-Lhat}
\end{equation}

\(\hat L\) is the difference of within-participant treatment
differences across strata. Under the sum-to-zero parameterization,
\(\widehat{(\alpha\beta)}_{11} = \hat L / 4\), so \(SS_{AB} =
nT\,\hat L^2 / 4\). With \(df_{AB} = 1\) this is the interaction mean
square. Under sphericity and balance, \(MS_{B \times S(A)} =
\hat\sigma_e^2\). Substituting into equation \eqref{eq:rmanova-f},

\begin{equation}
F_{AB} = \frac{nT\,\hat L^2}{4\,\hat\sigma_e^2},
\qquad df_1 = 1, \quad df_2 = 2(n-1) .
\label{eq:rmanova-f-22}
\end{equation}

We call equation \eqref{eq:rmanova-f-22} the strict RM-ANOVA \(F\)
throughout.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Equivalence to a within-participant \(t\)-test}{Equivalence to a within-participant t-test}}\label{equivalence-to-a-within-participant-t-test}

Define \(\Delta_{il} = \bar Y_{i1l\cdot} - \bar Y_{i2l\cdot}\), the
within-participant phase difference for participant \(l\) in stratum
\(i\). Then

\begin{equation}
\hat L = \bar\Delta_1 - \bar\Delta_2,
\qquad
\bar\Delta_i = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=1}^n \Delta_{il}.
\label{eq:rmanova-Delta}
\end{equation}

Under the model, the \(\Delta_{il}\) are i.i.d. within stratum with
\(\mathrm{Var}(\Delta_{il}) = 2\sigma_e^2 / T\). The participant
effect cancels in the difference. For any within-participant
covariance shared by all participants, AR(1) included, the
\(\Delta_{il}\) remain i.i.d. normal within stratum with a common
variance. Only the value of that variance changes. The variance of
\(\hat L\) is

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Var}(\hat L) = \frac{2 \cdot 2\sigma_e^2}{nT}
= \frac{4\sigma_e^2}{nT} ,
\label{eq:rmanova-varL}
\end{equation}

and the \(t\)-statistic is

\begin{equation}
t_{AB} = \frac{\hat L}{\sqrt{4\hat\sigma_e^2 / (nT)}}
= \sqrt{\frac{nT}{4}} \cdot \frac{\hat L}{\hat\sigma_e} .
\label{eq:rmanova-t}
\end{equation}

Then \(t_{AB}^2 = F_{AB}\), which recovers equation
\eqref{eq:rmanova-f-22}. The strict RM-ANOVA \(F\)-test for the
interaction is a two-sample \(t\)-test on the within-participant
phase differences with \(2(n-1)\) degrees of freedom. The identity is
standard in the split-plot literature\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-maxwelldelaney2018}{38}}.

The pooled \(t\)-test needs only that the \(\Delta_{il}\) be i.i.d.
normal with a common variance in the two strata. The identity and
the exactness at \(2(n-1)\) degrees of freedom therefore hold for any
within-participant covariance once each phase is averaged. Two
qualifications apply to the executed study. First, the two
randomization paths have different numbers of off-drug visits (3
and 4, counting baseline), so the contrast variance differs by
path and the pooled \(t\) is approximate. Second, with \(N\) odd the
median split gives unequal strata (13 and 12 at \(N = 25\)), so the
\(2(n-1)\) statement assumes balance. The observed null rejection
rates of the arm (Section \ref{sec-results}) show that neither
approximation matters here.

For \(a\) strata, \(b\) phases, \(n\) participants per stratum, and \(T\)
timepoints, equation \eqref{eq:rmanova-f} generalizes with
\(df_1 = (a-1)(b-1)\) and \(df_2 = a(n-1)(b-1)\). With the time factor
retained, the test of whether the biomarker modifies the treatment
effect across time is the three-way interaction, with
\(F_{AB\tau} = MS_{AB\tau} / MS_{B\tau \times S(A)}\),
\(df_1 = (a-1)(b-1)(T-1)\) and \(df_2 = a(n-1)(b-1)(T-1)\).

\subsection{Relation to the linear-mixed Wald test}\label{sec-lme-relation}

The strict RM-ANOVA \(F\) of equation \eqref{eq:rmanova-f-22} and the
\texttt{nlme::lme} Wald \(t\)-statistic on the \texttt{bm:Dbc} coefficient are
asymptotically equivalent under three conditions: (i) the biomarker
is dichotomized at the cut-point that defines the strata, (ii) the
within-participant covariance is compound-symmetric, and (iii)
\texttt{Dbc} is the binary on-drug indicator. When all three hold, the two
tests reach the same coefficient and the same standard error up to
small-sample factors. When any one fails, the mixed model uses
information the strict RM-ANOVA discards. The continuous biomarker
contributes within-stratum variation. The AR(1) covariance is
modeled. The exposure-decay predictor uses the off-drug trajectory.

The sphericity condition binds for the classical analysis that
keeps the \(T\) within-phase timepoints as repeated levels. The
pre-averaged \(2 \times 2\) form has a two-level within-participant
factor and is spherical for any covariance. When sphericity fails,
the nominal \(F\)-distribution is no longer exact under \(H_0\) and the
test over-rejects. Mauchly's test\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-mauchly1940}{40}} tests sphericity
but is uninformative about the direction of departure. Box's
\(\epsilon\) measures the departure, with \(\epsilon = 1\) under
sphericity and \(\epsilon \geq 1/(T-1)\) at maximum departure. The
Greenhouse-Geisser correction\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-greenhouse1959}{41}} estimates
\(\hat\epsilon\) and scales both degrees of freedom by it. The
Huynh-Feldt correction\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-huynh1976}{42}} is less conservative and is
recommended when \(\hat\epsilon > 0.75\)\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-haverkamp2017sphericity}{5},\citeproc{ref-blanca2023sphericity}{6}}.

The pmsimstats DGP imposes AR(1) correlation within each component.
A classical analysis that keeps the full visit series as
within-participant levels therefore over-rejects without a
correction. The project's audit
(\texttt{docs/legacy/06-ar1-residual-correlation.tex}) fitted a mixed model
with no residual-correlation structure to AR(1) data at eight
post-baseline visits and found Type I error of 9 to 17 percent
across the crossover and open-label designs at \(N = 35\) and \(70\)
(crossover 0.17 and 0.13, open-label 0.13 and 0.09). Switching to
\texttt{corCAR1} returned it to nominal. That figure is for the
unstructured fit, not for the strict RM-ANOVA arm evaluated here.
The arm evaluated here pre-averages each phase, so its Type I error
is at nominal (Section \ref{sec-results}) and its operative cost is
dichotomization.

Where a reviewer requests RM-ANOVA results, three responses are
defensible: (a) point to the LME as a continuous-time member of the
RM-ANOVA family, (b) report the strict RM-ANOVA \(F\) of equation
\eqref{eq:rmanova-f-22} as a sensitivity comparator, or (c) report
both with the power-loss decomposition. The simulation study
operationalizes (c).

\subsubsection{Power calculation under the closed form}\label{power-calculation-under-the-closed-form}

Under the alternative with \(\mathbb{E}[\hat L] = \Delta_0\), the
statistic \(F_{AB}\) follows a non-central \(F\) distribution with
non-centrality parameter

\begin{equation}
\lambda = \frac{nT\,\Delta_0^2}{4\sigma_e^2} ,
\label{eq:rmanova-ncp}
\end{equation}

where \(\Delta_0\) is the true interaction contrast on the outcome
scale. Power at level \(\alpha\) is

\begin{equation}
1 - \beta = \Pr\!\left[\, F_{AB} > F_{1, 2(n-1), 1-\alpha}
\,\big|\, \lambda \,\right] .
\label{eq:rmanova-power}
\end{equation}

This is computable with \texttt{stats::pf} and its \texttt{ncp} argument. For
sample-size calculation, \(n = 4\sigma_e^2 \lambda / (T
\Delta_0^2)\).

\subsubsection{Assumptions of the closed form and which the study exercises}\label{assumptions-of-the-closed-form-and-which-the-study-exercises}

The closed form assumes (i) a categorical biomarker at the
dichotomized cut-point with equal strata, (ii) sphericity of any
within-participant factor with more than two levels, (iii) no
carryover, (iv) a balanced design with no missing data, and (v)
equal time spacing if time is kept as a factor. We state which of
these the present study exercises.

Only assumption (i) is exercised. The executed RM-ANOVA arm
pre-averages each phase to one value, so (ii) holds by
construction. The DGP has no carryover, so (iii) holds. There is no
dropout, so (iv) holds. Time is not a factor in the pre-averaged
arm, so (v) does not arise. The comparison between strict RM-ANOVA
and the continuous-biomarker contrast therefore isolates the
dichotomization cost. The MMRM literature's handling of missing data\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-mallinckrodt2008recommendations}{19}} and the carryover specifications
of the companion paper\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper02}{36}} address assumptions
(iii) and (iv) in other studies. They are not tested here.

\section{Simulation design}\label{sec-design}

The study follows the ADEMP structure of Morris et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morris2019simulation}{1}}. It is
exploratory. Its findings should be confirmed in an independent
study.

\subsection{Aims}\label{aims}

The aims are (a) to separate the power cost of dichotomizing the
biomarker from the power cost of the test procedure, and (b) to
tabulate the size, power, coverage, and calibration ratio of the
continuous-biomarker procedures at \(N \in \{25, 35, 70, 100\}\).

\subsection{Trial design}\label{sec-trial-design}

The design is a two-path open-label plus blinded discontinuation
(OL+BDC) schedule. Participants take the drug open-label for 16
weeks with visits at weeks 4, 8, 12, and 16. A blinded phase follows
with weekly visits at weeks 17 to 20. Each participant is allocated
to one of two paths that differ in the week of blinded
discontinuation. Table \ref{tab:visits} gives the schedule. The
baseline visit at week 0 is off drug with expectancy 0. Expectancy
is 1 at the open-label visits and 0.5 at the blinded visits.

\texttt{generateData()} with \texttt{dgp\_architecture\ =\ \textquotesingle{}mean\_moderation\textquotesingle{}}. The

Under the mean-shift encoding, the drug-response mean at on-drug
visits is shifted by \(\beta_{bm}\, b_i\, \sigma_{BR}\), where \(b_i\) is
the standardized biomarker and \(\sigma_{BR} = 8\). The
biomarker is drawn with mean 124.33 and SD 15.36
and is standardized by these values before every analysis. Without
the standardization, a biomarker centered near 124 would make the
\texttt{bm:Dbc} column nearly collinear with \texttt{Dbc}. The interaction on the
outcome scale is therefore \(\beta_{bm} \sigma_{BR}\) per biomarker
SD, with an analytic magnitude of 3.6 at
\(\beta_{bm} = 0.45\). A Monte Carlo reference fit of the model-based
LME, averaged over 6 data sets of \(N = 2000\),
gave \(-3.51\) in the sign convention of the fitted
coefficient. That value is the reference for bias and coverage.

The correlation structure is neither that of the original DGP of
Hendrickson et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{2}} (compound symmetry within components,
\(\rho_{cs} = 0.8\)) nor that of the extended DGP of the series'
notation, which sets the cross-component correlation \(c_1 = 0.2\).
The carryover half-life is zero: the drug-response mean drops to
zero at discontinuation. There is no dropout. Under the mean-shift
encoding \(\beta_{bm}\) does not enter the covariance matrix. The
correlation matrices of both paths are positive definite, and
positive component SDs do not change that. The run refuses a
non-positive-definite matrix rather than repairing it, and every
replicate data set was generated.

\subsection{Estimands}\label{estimands}

The primary estimand is the interaction coefficient \(\beta_{bm:D}\)
on the outcome scale for the four continuous-biomarker arms. The
strict RM-ANOVA arm targets a dichotomized-stratum contrast on a
different scale and is excluded from the coefficient-based measures.

\subsection{Methods}\label{sec-methods}

Six procedures were run. Each procedure is applied to the same
replicate data set.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Strict RM-ANOVA (dichotomized).} The biomarker is split at
  the sample median. Each participant's outcome is averaged within
  the on-drug and off-drug phases, baseline counted as off drug. A
  split-plot ANOVA (\texttt{aov} with \texttt{Error(ptID/Db)}) tests the
  stratum-by-phase interaction. This is the \(2 \times 2\) form of
  equation \eqref{eq:rmanova-f-22}.
\item
  \textbf{Continuous-biomarker contrast.} Each participant's on-drug
  mean minus off-drug mean, \(\Delta_l\), is regressed on the
  continuous biomarker by ordinary least squares. The slope is the
  interaction estimate and its \(t\)-test on \(N - 2\) degrees of
  freedom is the test. This is the paired-difference statistic of
  the companion papers (their E9b, baseline counted as off drug).
\item
  \textbf{LME, model-based inference.} The package function
  \texttt{lme\_analysis()} fits \texttt{Sx\ \textasciitilde{}\ bm\ +\ t\ +\ Dbc\ +\ bm:Dbc\ +\ De} by
  \texttt{nlme::lme} with a participant random intercept and
  \texttt{corCAR1(form\ =\ \textasciitilde{}t\ \textbar{}\ ptID)} residual correlation on calendar
  weeks. \texttt{t} is calendar week. \texttt{De} is the design expectancy.
  \texttt{Dbc} is the exposure predictor at an analysis half-life of
  zero, which is the binary on-drug indicator (0 at baseline). The
  test is the \texttt{nlme} Wald \(t\) on \texttt{bm:Dbc} with the model-based
  standard error and \texttt{nlme}'s within-participant degrees of
  freedom (556 at \(N = 70\)). Apart from the \texttt{De} term, this is
  specification G1 of the companion paper\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper02}{36}},
  its Table of analysis codes, fitted at the true half-life.
\item
  \textbf{LME plus CR2.} The same fit as
  procedure 3. The standard error of \texttt{bm:Dbc} is replaced by the
  CR2 bias-reduced cluster-robust sandwich with Satterthwaite
  degrees of freedom\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-pustejovsky2018clubsandwich}{43},\citeproc{ref-satterthwaite1946}{44}}, computed by \texttt{clubSandwich::coef\_test} with
  participant as the cluster. This is the inference of G6 in the
  companion paper and of the calibration manuscript\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper10}{37}}. The point estimate is unchanged.
\item
  \textbf{GEE, Liang-Zeger sandwich.} \texttt{geepack::geeglm} fits
  \texttt{Sx\ \textasciitilde{}\ bm\ +\ t\ +\ Dbc\ +\ bm:Dbc} with a Gaussian family and
  \texttt{corstr\ =\ \textquotesingle{}ar1\textquotesingle{}}. The working correlation is a discrete-lag AR(1)
  on observation order within participant. The visits are at weeks
  0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 17, 18, 19, and 20, so the working correlation
  treats unequally spaced occasions as equally spaced. The LME arms
  use the continuous-time form instead. The test is the Wald \(z\)
  with the robust sandwich of Liang and Zeger\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-liang1986}{20}}.
\item
  \textbf{GEE, Mancl-DeRouen sandwich.} The same fit as procedure 5.
  The sandwich meat is bias-corrected by inflating each cluster's
  residual as \((I - H_{ii})^{-1} r_i\)\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-mancl2001covariance}{21}},
  computed inline. The Wald statistic is referred to a \(t\)
  distribution on \(K - p\) degrees of freedom, with \(K = N\) clusters
  and \(p = 5\) fixed effects, the pairing Li and Redden\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-li2015sandwich}{26}} recommend.
  The driver also stores the normal-reference test for comparison.
  A separate script (\texttt{02-mancl-t-reference.R}) recomputes both
  references from the per-replicate estimates and standard errors
  without refitting and reproduces the driver's rejection rates.
  The inline sandwich was benchmarked against \texttt{geesmv::GEE.var.md}
  on 25 probe replicates at \(N = 35\),
  \(\beta_{bm} = 0.45\): the inline-to-\texttt{geesmv} ratio of the
  interaction standard error had mean 1.043,
  SD 0.033, and range
  1.003 to 1.147.
\end{enumerate}

The arms differ in their mean model as well as in their covariance
treatment. The contrast arm uses no time trend and no expectancy
term. The GEE arms include \texttt{t}. The LME arms include \texttt{t} and \texttt{De}.
The step from the contrast to the LME therefore changes the mean
model as well as the covariance treatment, and we do not attribute
it to the covariance treatment alone.

\subsection{Performance measures}\label{performance-measures}

Power is \(\Pr(p < 0.05)\) at \(\beta_{bm} = 0.45\). Type I error is the
same rate at \(\beta_{bm} = 0\). Both carry the binomial MCSE
\(\sqrt{\hat\pi(1 - \hat\pi)/n_{\text{reps}}}\). For the
coefficient-bearing arms we report bias relative to the Monte Carlo
reference value, the empirical SE (the SD of the estimates), the
mean model-based SE, the calibration ratio \(\kappa\) (mean model SE
over empirical SE; \(\kappa > 1\) indicates a conservative test), and
the coverage of the nominal 95 percent Wald interval. Every null
cell is tested against 0.05 by an exact binomial test.

\subsection{The cost of dichotomization}\label{the-cost-of-dichotomization}

The power gap between strict RM-ANOVA and the LME is mostly the
cost of dichotomizing the biomarker. At \(N = 25\) strict RM-ANOVA
had power 0.253. The contrast, which differs
only in keeping the biomarker continuous, had power
0.376. Dichotomization therefore cost
0.123. The LME had power 0.413, so the
test procedure added 0.037. The decomposition holds
across \(N\). The dichotomization cost was 0.175 at
\(N = 35\) (0.309 to
0.484), 0.223 at \(N = 70\)
(0.563 to 0.786),
and 0.166 at \(N = 100\) (0.724 to
0.890). It was largest at \(N = 70\),
where power was near the middle of its range, and smaller at the
ends. The contrast-to-LME increment was 0.037 to 0.066 with
model-based inference. With CR2 inference it was
0.010 to 0.085, the largest at
\(N = 70\). In every cell the dichotomization
cost exceeded the largest test-procedure increment among the
procedures with nominal or conservative size (0.085).

The observed cost agrees with the classical prediction. A median
split of a normal predictor retains a fraction \(2/\pi \approx 0.64\)
of the squared correlation\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-cohen1983dichotomization}{14}}, so the
non-centrality of the contrast test is multiplied by
\(\sqrt{2/\pi} \approx 0.80\). Solving the observed contrast power
for its non-centrality at \(N - 2\) degrees of freedom and scaling by
0.80 predicts dichotomized power of 0.259, 0.335, 0.593, and 0.720 at
\(N = 25, 35, 70, 100\). The observed values were 0.253, 0.309, 0.563, and 0.724.
The signed differences, observed minus predicted, are
-0.4, -1.7, -1.9, and 0.3 MCSE, each within
1.9 MCSE. The observed loss is slightly
larger than predicted at \(N = 35\) and \(70\), but no cell departs by
more than 2 MCSE, and the prediction itself carries the Monte Carlo
error of the contrast's power. The dichotomization result is
therefore Cohen's loss observed in the N-of-1 interaction setting,
not a new finding.

\subsection{GEE calibration}\label{gee-calibration}

The Liang-Zeger GEE over-rejected at every \(N\). Its null rate was
0.113, 0.086, 0.068, and 0.075 at \(N = 25, 35, 70, 100\) (exact binomial
\(p \le 0.0004\) in every cell). The rate was
highest at \(N = 25\) and did not return to nominal at \(N = 100\). The
inflation persists at trial-relevant sizes.

The Mancl-DeRouen correction with the normal reference did not
restore nominal size. Its null rates were
0.0645, 0.0645, 0.0565, and 0.0640. Three of the four cells
differ from 0.05 by the exact binomial test
(\(p = 0.004\),
0.004 and
0.006 at \(N = 25, 35, 100\)); the
\(N = 70\) cell does not (\(p = 0.18\)).
This is the residual over-rejection that Li and Redden\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-li2015sandwich}{26}} report for
a bias-corrected sandwich with a Wald \(z\) test at small cluster
counts.

The same correction with a \(t\) reference on \(K - 5\) degrees of
freedom (20, 30, 65, and 95) gave null rates of
0.0530, 0.0545, 0.0510, and 0.0595. No cell differs from 0.05 by
the binomial test (smallest
\(p = 0.057\), at
\(N = 100\)). Its coverage of the
reference interaction at \(\beta_{bm} = 0.45\) was
95.6, 94.2, 94.8, and 94.7 percent. The power cost
relative to the Liang-Zeger GEE was
14 points at \(N = 25\)
(0.463 to 0.326),
11 points at \(N = 35\)
(0.537 to 0.431),
5 points at \(N = 70\),
and 2 points at
\(N = 100\). The Liang-Zeger GEE had more power than the model-based
LME only at \(N = 25\) (0.463 against
0.413). That advantage is the residue of its
over-rejection, not an efficiency gain. At larger \(N\) the LME had
equal or greater power despite its conservative size. With the \(t\)
reference the corrected GEE sits
0.06 to 0.11 below the model-based LME
and 0.00 to 0.05 below the
contrast. It is the least powerful of the continuous-biomarker
procedures with nominal size at every \(N\).

\subsection{The conservatism of the model-based LME}\label{the-conservatism-of-the-model-based-lme}

The LME's null rejection rate was 0.0340, 0.0300, 0.0280, and 0.0360. Every cell
is below 0.05 by 2.9 to 4.5 binomial
standard errors at the nominal rate (exact binomial
\(p \le 0.003\) in each). Its coverage is
96.1 to 97.6 percent at
\(\beta_{bm} = 0.45\) and its \(\kappa\) is
1.07 to 1.11. The
model-based standard error exceeds the empirical standard error by
7 to 11
percent in every cell.

This is a working-covariance calibration defect, not a
degrees-of-freedom effect. The fitted covariance (random intercept
plus continuous-time AR(1)) is applied to data generated from three
AR(1) components with distinct curves, a separate baseline level,
and cross-component correlations. The \texttt{nlme} Wald test assigns the
within-participant term \texttt{bm:Dbc} the inner-level degrees of
freedom, 556 at \(N = 70\), so no choice of reference distribution
can produce a size this far below 5 percent. A Kenward-Roger\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-kenward1997small}{45}} or Satterthwaite adjustment of the model-based
test can only reduce the rejection rate further. Both inflate the
estimated variance or reduce the denominator degrees of freedom.
They are remedies for anti-conservative tests, not conservative
ones.

The companion papers diagnose and remedy this defect. The
calibration manuscript\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper10}{37}} traces the
over-estimated standard error to the working covariance. The
carryover manuscript\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper02}{36}} replaces the model-based
standard error by the CR2 cluster-robust sandwich with Satterthwaite
degrees of freedom (its rung G6), which moves the null rejection
rate from the conservative G1 value to near nominal. Procedure 4
(Section \ref{sec-methods}) applies that inference to the fit
evaluated here.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec-discussion}

\subsection{What the study settles}\label{what-the-study-settles}

The study settles four points at its resolution. First, the power
gap between strict RM-ANOVA and the LME is the cost of
dichotomization. The continuous-biomarker contrast recovers
0.123 of the 0.160
gap at \(N = 25\), and the pattern holds across the grid. The loss
matches Cohen's \(2/\pi\) factor within 2 MCSE. Second, the
Liang-Zeger GEE over-rejects at every \(N\) examined, and the
Mancl-DeRouen correction restores size only when paired with a \(t\)
reference on \(K - p\) degrees of freedom. That pairing costs power:
it is the least powerful of the continuous-biomarker arms with
nominal size. Third, the bias of the four continuous-biomarker arms
is small against their standard errors, so their differences are
variance-estimation effects that the \(\kappa\) table explains.
Fourth, the model-based LME is conservative because its working
covariance over-estimates the standard error by
7 to 11
percent. A degrees-of-freedom correction would move it further from
nominal. The CR2 cluster-robust standard error is the remedy for
size. Here it brings the null rejection rate to
4.5 to 5.8 percent. It raises power at
\(N \ge 35\) but lowers it at \(N = 25\).

At \(\beta_{bm} = 0.45\) under the published parameters, power was
modest. No procedure reached 0.80 at \(N \le 35\), and the arms with
nominal size first reached it at \(N = 70\) (CR2) or \(N = 100\) (the
contrast and the corrected GEE). The procedure ranking is
informative at these sample sizes; the absolute power values depend
on the effect size and the variance components and do not transfer
to other settings.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{limitations}

The study has one design, one effect size, no carryover, and no
dropout. The GEE working correlation is a discrete-lag AR(1) on
unequally spaced visits, which mismatches the DGP and the LME's
continuous-time form; a closer working correlation might change the
GEE power by a few points. The LME and GEE arms include a time
trend and the LME an expectancy term, while the contrast includes
neither, so the contrast-to-LME step is not a pure covariance
comparison. The Monte Carlo reference value for the interaction
rests on 6 large-sample fits, so the reported bias and
coverage carry its error as well as their own. The study is
exploratory.

\subsection{Future work}\label{future-work}

Two extensions are recorded. A carryover cell at \(t_{1/2} = 0.5\)
week, the series' standard grid value, would exercise the
no-carryover assumption rather than assert it. A cycle-by-period
design grid (cycle count, on- and off-drug period lengths, on/off
symmetry, run-in) was planned as Studies 2 and 3 of the working
protocol and is not delivered here. The recommendation in Section
\ref{sec-recommendation} holds at the fixed design only.

\section{Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

Keep the biomarker continuous. Dichotomization cost 0.12 to 0.22 of
absolute power across \(N \in \{25, 35, 70, 100\}\), which is the
classical \(2/\pi\) loss, while the test procedure moved power by at
most 0.09 among procedures with nominal or conservative
size. The model-based LME was conservative, with size
0.028 to 0.036 and a model standard error
7 to 11
percent above the empirical one. That defect is in the working
covariance, not in the degrees of freedom. The series' CR2
cluster-robust inference repairs it: the same fit rejected at
0.045 to 0.058 under the null and had the highest power
of the continuous-biomarker arms with nominal size at every \(N\).
It was slightly less powerful than the conservative model-based fit
at \(N = 25\). The Liang-Zeger GEE over-rejected at every \(N\). The
Mancl-DeRouen sandwich with a \(t_{K-p}\) reference held size at
0.051 to 0.060 with
94 to 96 percent coverage, at a power
cost of 11 to 14
points relative to the uncorrected GEE at \(N \le 35\). Power at the
reference effect size was modest: no procedure reached 0.80 below
\(N = 70\). The
strict RM-ANOVA \(F\)-test is a two-sample \(t\)-test on
within-participant phase differences, exact for any
within-participant covariance once each phase is averaged, and its
only cost here is the dichotomization.

\section{Glossary}\label{glossary}

\textbf{Aggregated N-of-1 trial.} Many single-participant on/off series
pooled into one model.

\textbf{OL+BDC.} Open-label titration followed by blinded discontinuation.
The two-path version used here is defined in Table
\ref{tab:visits}.

\textbf{Hybrid design.} The Hendrickson et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{2}} design: open-label titration,
blinded discontinuation, then a brief crossover. Not used here.

\textbf{Mean-shift encoding.} The interaction is a shift of the
drug-response mean by the centered biomarker. The data value is
\texttt{mean\_moderation}.

\textbf{Correlation encoding.} The interaction is a correlation between
the biomarker and the drug response that is present on drug and
absent off drug. The companion papers' \(c_{bm}\). Not used here.

\textbf{\(\beta_{bm}\).} The interaction multiplier on the standardized
biomarker under the mean-shift encoding. The outcome-scale
interaction is \(\beta_{bm} \sigma_{BR}\).

\textbf{\(\beta_{bm:D}\).} The interaction coefficient on the outcome scale,
the coefficient of \texttt{bm:Dbc} in the LME and GEE fits.

\textbf{\texttt{Dbc}.} The exposure predictor. At an analysis half-life of zero
it is the binary on-drug indicator, 0 at baseline.

\textbf{\texttt{De}.} The design expectancy at each visit (Table
\ref{tab:visits}).

\textbf{\texttt{corCAR1}.} The continuous-time AR(1) residual correlation of
\texttt{nlme}, \(\varphi^{|w_t - w_s|}\) on calendar weeks.

\textbf{G1, G6, G9, F1.} Analysis codes of the companion paper\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper02}{36}}. G1 is the binary-indicator LME with
model-based inference. G6 is G1 with CR2 inference. G9 is the
exposure-decay predictor with the half-life chosen by AIC and CR2
inference. F1 adds expectancy terms at a fixed half-life with CR2.

\textbf{E9b.} The companion papers' label for the paired-difference
statistic with baseline counted as an off-drug visit; the contrast
arm here.

\textbf{CR2.} The bias-reduced cluster-robust variance estimator of
Pustejovsky and Tipton\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-pustejovsky2018clubsandwich}{43}} with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom.

\textbf{Liang-Zeger sandwich.} The robust variance estimator of
Liang and Zeger\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-liang1986}{20}}. Some GEE literature calls the model-based variance
``naive''; this paper does not use that word.

\textbf{Mancl-DeRouen sandwich.} The bias-corrected sandwich of
Mancl and DeRouen\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-mancl2001covariance}{21}}, with residuals inflated by \((I -
H_{ii})^{-1}\).

\textbf{\(\kappa\).} The calibration ratio: mean model-based standard error
divided by the empirical standard deviation of the estimates.
\(\kappa > 1\) indicates a conservative test.

\textbf{Extended DGP, original DGP.} The series' names for the
recommended data-generating process (\(c_1 = 0.2\), decaying
correlation, carryover from the last on-drug mean) and for the
Hendrickson et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{2}} process. The configuration used here is neither.

\textbf{MCSE.} Monte Carlo standard error.

20260507, then runs the
cell-by-replicate job list through \texttt{parallel::mclapply} with
\texttt{mc.set.seed\ =\ TRUE} and \texttt{mc.preschedule\ =\ TRUE}. Each forked worker
receives one L'Ecuyer stream and processes a prescheduled block of
jobs from it sequentially. Reproduction therefore requires the same
worker count (6 for the stored run), the same job
list, and the same platform. The reference run that pins the
estimand (6 draws at \(N = 2000\)) precedes the
job list and advances the generator, so it must also be run
unchanged. Results from the Docker container and from a local
session are not draw-for-draw identical (different BLAS).

\textbf{Data and code.} Per-replicate outputs are at
\texttt{analysis/data/quick-sim/08-test-published/08-test-replicates.rds};
the run's parameter set and settings at \texttt{08-test-meta.rds}; the
cell-level
summary at \texttt{08-test-summary.txt}; the Monte Carlo reference estimand
at \texttt{08-test-betatrue.txt}; the \texttt{geesmv} benchmark at
\texttt{08-test-mancl-benchmark.txt}; the recomputed Mancl-DeRouen
\(t\)-reference summary, the binomial tests of the null cells, and the
\(\kappa\) table at \texttt{08-test-mancl-tref.txt},
\texttt{08-test-null-binomial.txt} and \texttt{08-test-kappa.txt}. The driver
is
\texttt{analysis/scripts/test-procedure-design-sensitivity/01-study1-test-procedure.R}
and the recomputation script \texttt{02-mancl-t-reference.R} in the same
directory. The working protocol is \texttt{00-ademp-protocol.md} there. The
manuscript source, bibliography (\texttt{references.bib}) and CSL file are
at \texttt{analysis/report/08-test-procedure-design-sensitivity/}.
