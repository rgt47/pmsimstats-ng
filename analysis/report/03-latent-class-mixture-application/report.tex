\section*{Abstract}\label{abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

\textbf{Background.} Aggregated N-of-1 trials can test whether a baseline
biomarker predicts the response to a drug. Simulation studies of that
test encode the biomarker interaction as a continuous effect. The
interaction can instead be a latent-class structure, in which the
biomarker predicts membership in a responder class. We ask whether a
class-aware analysis has any advantage over a linear mixed model when
the class structure is real.

\textbf{Methods.} We define the continuous and latent-class encodings and
give the marginal correlation induced by a two-class mixture. We
report a 240-replicate pilot and two 2,000-replicate null cells at
\(N = 35\) and \(N = 70\), all on an open-label design. We describe a
corrected study on the Hybrid design at \(N \in \{35, 70, 140\}\) that
compares a linear mixed model with class-aware tests under identical
fixed effects. The class-aware tests include a single-resample
parametric-bootstrap likelihood-ratio test of two classes against
one. The study is exploratory.

\textbf{Results.} The pilot's open-label design had no off-drug visit. Its
linear mixed model therefore fitted a biomarker-by-time term, not the
biomarker-by-drug interaction. That test rejected 0 of 240 null
replicates and 5 of 2,000 and
1 of 2,000 in the production null cells.
Its model standard error exceeded the empirical standard deviation by
a factor of 1.67. The
unconditional Wald test on the gating slope of a two-class mixed model
rejected 0.197 of null replicates in the
pilot (MCSE 0.027).
It rejected 0.166 and
0.240 in the production cells. The
gating slope exists only when two classes exist, so the Wald statistic
has no chi-square limit under one class. BIC selected two classes in
0.4 to
5.1 percent of pilot fits. The
corrected study's results will be inserted when the run completes.

\textbf{Conclusions.} The pilot and the production null cells do not answer
the question. They show that an unconditional gating Wald test is
anti-conservative at every sample size examined, and that a BIC gate
turns it into a near-never-reject rule. The corrected study tests
calibrated procedures on a design with an off-drug contrast.

\textbf{Trial registration.} Not applicable.

\textbf{Keywords:} N-of-1 trials; predictive biomarker; latent class;
finite mixture; growth mixture model; simulation; Type I error.

\section{Background}\label{background}

\subsection{Aggregated N-of-1 trials and predictive biomarkers}\label{aggregated-n-of-1-trials-and-predictive-biomarkers}

In an aggregated N-of-1 trial each participant moves between drug and
placebo, or between drug and no drug, several times. The individual
series are analyzed together (\citeproc{ref-guyatt1986}{1}--\citeproc{ref-vohra2015cent}{5}). Every participant contributes an on-drug
and an off-drug response, so the design can test whether a baseline
measurement predicts who benefits (\citeproc{ref-schork2015}{6},\citeproc{ref-kent2020path}{7}). The
statistical target is the biomarker-by-treatment interaction
\(\beta_{bm:D}\) in a within-participant outcome model. Its power
depends on the strength of the interaction, on the precision of the
within-participant contrast, on the design, and on carryover between
phases. Power is estimated by simulation (\citeproc{ref-morris2019simulation}{8},\citeproc{ref-percha2019}{9}). Hendrickson et al. (\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{10}) proposed a
simulation method for the Hybrid design, which combines open-label
titration, blinded discontinuation and a brief crossover. We call
their process the original DGP.

\subsection{Two encodings of a continuous interaction}\label{two-encodings-of-a-continuous-interaction}

The original DGP encodes the interaction as a correlation. The
baseline biomarker and the drug-response component at every visit are
drawn from one multivariate normal distribution. The predictive
biomarker is a correlation \(c_{bm}\) between the biomarker and the drug
response that is present on drug and absent off drug. We call this
the correlation encoding. The alternative in the trial literature
shifts the drug-response mean by the centered biomarker, so the
interaction is a coefficient \(\beta_{bm}\) in the conditional mean. We
call this the mean-shift encoding. The companion paper
(\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper01}{11}) compares the two. At a matched total
\(N = 70\) in the Hybrid design, carryover with a half-life of one week
reduced power by 15.4 percent under the correlation encoding and by
6.9 percent under the mean-shift encoding. Conditional on the
biomarker, the correlation encoding is a mean shift with a smaller
residual variance, so the two differ in the exposure profile of the
interaction, not in kind.

\subsection{The latent-class encoding}\label{the-latent-class-encoding}

Both encodings are continuous. Every participant has some degree of
drug response, and the response is a smooth function of the
biomarker. A third encoding is discrete. Participants belong to one of
a small number of unobserved classes, most often a responder and a
non-responder class. The classes have distinct drug-response curves.
The biomarker predicts class membership without determining it. The
marginal interaction then arises from the mixture, not from a
conditional-mean function. The discrete encoding corresponds to a
different biological hypothesis. A metabolizing or non-metabolizing
allele, or a present or absent target receptor, produces classes. A
receptor density or a baseline severity produces a continuous
gradient. The two hypotheses can give the same biomarker-outcome
association at a single visit and differ over a longitudinal series,
because the class label is fixed within a participant while the
within-class drug contrast decays under carryover.

\subsection{Related work}\label{related-work}

The latent-class encoding has a direct precedent. Muthén et al.
(\citeproc{ref-muthen2002}{12}) fitted a growth mixture model to a randomized
longitudinal trial and estimated the treatment effect within latent
trajectory classes. Our encoding is that model with the biomarker as a
class-membership covariate. The N-of-1 and crossover literature has
also examined the responder dichotomy. Senn (\citeproc{ref-senn2004individual}{13},\citeproc{ref-senn2016mastering}{14}) showed that the patient-by-treatment interaction
variance is identifiable only with repeated within-patient crossover,
and that apparent responder classes are often artifacts of
within-patient variation. Araujo, Julious and Senn (\citeproc{ref-araujo2016}{15})
extended this by simulation. These papers address treatment-effect
heterogeneity. They do not encode a biomarker-predicted class
structure in a simulation DGP, which is the gap this paper addresses.

The finite-mixture machinery comes from psychometrics and
econometrics. Latent structure analysis (\citeproc{ref-lazarsfeldhenry1968}{16},\citeproc{ref-goodman1974}{17}) and the finite-mixture monograph of McLachlan and Peel
(\citeproc{ref-mclachlanpeel2000}{18}) supply the discrete model. Covariate-dependent
gating of class membership was developed in marketing science
(\citeproc{ref-desarbocron1988}{19},\citeproc{ref-wedeldesarbo1995}{20}) and in machine learning as
mixtures of experts (\citeproc{ref-jacobs1991}{21}). The growth mixture model
(\citeproc{ref-muthenshedden1999}{22},\citeproc{ref-nagin1999}{23}) and the heterogeneous random-effects
model (\citeproc{ref-verbekelesaffre1996}{24}) place the mixture on the random effects
of a longitudinal mixed model. Lubke and Neale (\citeproc{ref-lubkeneale2006}{25})
simulated the continuous-versus-discrete question directly, and
Bauer and Curran (\citeproc{ref-bauercurran2004}{26}) set out its conceptual problems.
Growth and factor mixture models fitted to single-component
non-normal data can extract spurious classes (\citeproc{ref-bauercurran2003}{27},\citeproc{ref-bauer2007}{28}), and class enumeration at small \(N\) has limited power
(\citeproc{ref-nylund2007}{29}--\citeproc{ref-lubkemuthen2007}{31}). The \texttt{lcmm} package
(\citeproc{ref-proustlima2017}{32}) and the joint latent class literature
(\citeproc{ref-proustlima2014}{33}) implement these models in R. Reporting standards
for mixture studies exist (\citeproc{ref-vandeschoot2017}{34},\citeproc{ref-nylundgibson2018}{35}).

\subsection{Aims}\label{aims}

This paper has three aims. The first is to state the continuous and
latent-class encodings of the biomarker interaction in one notation
and to give the marginal correlation induced by a two-class mixture.
The second is to report, for the record, a pilot and two production
null cells on an open-label design, and to state what they do and do
not show. The third is to describe and receive a corrected simulation
on the Hybrid design that compares a linear mixed model with
class-aware tests under identical fixed effects and with calibrated
tests of class count. The paper does not derive identifiability
conditions. It examines by simulation whether the gating slope of a
two-class model is testable at \(N \in \{35, 70, 140\}\). The study is
exploratory.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{Notation}\label{notation}

\(B_i\) is the pre-treatment biomarker of participant \(i\) and
\(b_i = (B_i - \bar B)/s_B\) its standardized value. \(BR_{it}\) is the
drug-response component of the symptom score \(Y_{it}\) at visit \(t\).
The original DGP adds a natural-history component \(TV_{it}\) and a
placebo-response component \(PB_{it}\). \(D_{it}\) is the binary drug
state. \(D_{bc,it}\) is the exposure-decayed drug indicator of the
analysis model: 1 on drug, \(\exp(-\lambda t_{sd,it})\) off drug after
the first exposure, and 0 at off-drug visits before the first
exposure. Here \(t_{sd,it}\) is the time since discontinuation and
\(\lambda = \ln 2 / t_{1/2}\) with \(t_{1/2}\) the carryover half-life.
\(D_{e,t}\), written \texttt{De} in code, is the design expectancy \(\eta_t\): 1
in the open-label phase, 0.5 when blinded and 0 at baseline. \(Z_i\) is
the unobserved class label. \(\omega\) is a mixing proportion and
\(\omega(B_i) = \Pr(Z_i = 1 \mid B_i)\) the class-membership
probability. \(\psi_g\) is the gating slope, the steepness of
\(\omega(B_i)\) in \(b_i\); the code calls it \texttt{gs}. \(\mathbb E[\cdot]\) is
the expectation. \(\kappa\) is the mean model standard error divided by
the empirical standard deviation of the estimates. MCSE is the Monte
Carlo standard error \(\sqrt{\hat\pi(1-\hat\pi)/n}\) of a rejection
rate \(\hat\pi\) over \(n\) replicates. The symbol \(\psi_g\) is unrelated
to the \(\gamma\) of the companion component-decomposition paper
(\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper06}{36}).

\subsection{The two-class mixture DGP}\label{the-two-class-mixture-dgp}

If the biomarker indexes an unobserved responder dichotomy, the
generative form is a two-component mixture,

\[
Z_i \mid B_i \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}\bigl(\omega(B_i)\bigr), \qquad
BR_{it} \mid Z_i = z \sim f_z(t, D_{bc,it}),
\]

with a logistic gate in the standardized biomarker,

\[
\omega(B_i) = \mathrm{expit}(\psi_0 + \psi_g \, b_i).
\]

In every simulation below \(\psi_0 = 0\), so \(\omega(\bar B) = 0.5\).
The class-conditional distributions \(f_0\) and \(f_1\) differ in their
drug-response parameters. Two consequences follow. The class label is
fixed across all visits of a participant. The class-conditional mean
still depends on \(D_{bc,it}\), so the two class means converge when
\(D_{bc,it} \to 0\) after a long off-drug period. An analysis with
access to \(Z_i\) could use the fixed label where the instantaneous
mean separation has decayed. The correlation encoding has no such
label. Its correlation is the whole signal, and carryover erodes it
directly.

\subsection{The MVN approximation}\label{the-mvn-approximation}

A two-component mixture with class-dependent means and a common
within-class covariance has a marginal distribution of
\((B_i, BR_{it})\) whose first two moments are reproduced by one
multivariate normal with \(\mathrm{Cor}(B, BR) = c_{bm}\). Suppose \(B_i\)
and \(BR_{it}\) are independent given \(Z_i\). Then
\(\mathrm{Cov}(B, BR) = \omega(1-\omega)\Delta_B\Delta_{BR}\) with
\(\Delta_B = \mu_B^1 - \mu_B^0\) and \(\Delta_{BR} = \mu_{BR}^1 -
\mu_{BR}^0\), and

\[
c_{bm}^2 \;=\;
\frac{\omega(1-\omega)\Delta_B^2}{\mathrm{Var}(B)}
\;\cdot\;
\frac{\omega(1-\omega)\Delta_{BR}^2}{\mathrm{Var}(BR)}.
\]

The squared marginal correlation is the product of the between-class
variance fractions of \(B\) and of \(BR\). The marginal correlation
therefore equals the geometric mean of the two between-class variance
fractions. If either class separation is small, the marginal \(c_{bm}\)
is small even when the classes are well defined. The formula
conditions on \(Z_i\). It requires \(B_i \perp BR_{it} \mid Z_i\), which
is a restriction beyond the mixture DGP above, where \(B_i\) enters only
through the gate. Within that restriction, and with \(\omega\) bounded
away from 0 and 1, a single multivariate normal with a correlation
that is present on drug and absent off drug reproduces the mixture's
second moments. A power calculation for a Wald test of
\(\beta_{bm:D}\) in a linear mixed model then rests on those moments.

\subsection{Where the approximation may fail}\label{where-the-approximation-may-fail}

The approximation can mislead in three regimes. Under well separated
classes the marginal distribution of \(BR\) is bimodal and no
multivariate normal matches its tails. A mixture analysis might then
exceed the power of a linear mixed model. We do not assert that it
does. The pilot below shows the opposite at \(N = 70\) on an open-label
design, and the corrected study examines it on the Hybrid design.
Under a steep gate the biomarker behaves as a class label and a
thresholded mean-shift specification is the better approximation.
Under class-varying covariance, if the residual variance or the
serial correlation differs between classes, no single-component
multivariate normal represents the generative structure. The
class-scaling DGP below is in this regime.

\subsection{The class-scaling DGP and the random-slopes DGP}\label{the-class-scaling-dgp-and-the-random-slopes-dgp}

The pilot used the DGP of
\texttt{02-heterogeneous-slopes-dgp.R}, which we call the class-scaling
DGP. It draws one trial from the original DGP at \(c_{bm} = 0\),
assigns each participant a class by the logistic gate, and
multiplies the drug-response component at on-drug visits by 1 in
class 1 and by 0 in class 0. The non-responder class therefore loses
the drug-response mean and the drug-response variance alike. The
class-conditional variances differ: the drug-response variance
\(\sigma_{BR}^2\) is present in class 1 and absent in class 0 at every
on-drug visit. The class-scaling DGP contains no random slope. The
pilot's earlier label, ``heterogeneous-random-slopes DGP'', was wrong.

The random-slopes DGP of \texttt{07-true-heterogeneous-slopes-dgp.R} is the
Study 3 DGP of the working protocol. Each participant's drug-effect
slope is drawn from a class-conditional normal distribution,
\(\beta_R \sim \mathcal N(\mu_R, \sigma_R^2)\) in the responder class
and \(\beta_{NR} \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma_{NR}^2)\) in the
non-responder class, with class membership from the same gate. This
is the Verbeke-Lesaffre heterogeneous random-effects form
(\citeproc{ref-verbekelesaffre1996}{24}). The corrected study runs both DGPs as
distinct cells at the same gating slopes. In the working protocol the
gating-slope cells belong to Study 3 and the single-component null
cells to Study 5. The pilot's gating-slope cells were labeled Study 5
in the earlier draft; that label was wrong.

\subsection{Testing for a second class}\label{testing-for-a-second-class}

The pilot tested the gating slope with an unconditional Wald
statistic from a two-class \texttt{hlme} fit. Under one class the gating
slope is a parameter that exists only under the alternative. The
information matrix is singular at the null, and the Wald statistic
has no \(\chi^2_1\) limit at any sample size (\citeproc{ref-davies1977}{37}--\citeproc{ref-hansen1996}{39}). The same problem affects the naive
likelihood-ratio test of two classes against one, whose null
distribution is not \(\chi^2\) on the parameter-count difference
(\citeproc{ref-mclachlanpeel2000}{18}). The calibrated tests are the parametric
bootstrap likelihood-ratio test (\citeproc{ref-nylund2007}{29},\citeproc{ref-mclachlan1987}{40}), the
Lo-Mendell-Rubin test (\citeproc{ref-lo2001}{41}), and the modified likelihood-ratio
test of Chen, Chen and Kalbfleisch (\citeproc{ref-chen2001modified}{42}). Dziak, Lanza
and Tan (\citeproc{ref-dziak2014}{30}) give the power of the bootstrap test as a
function of \(N\) and class separation at sample sizes that include the
N-of-1 range. Local solutions are common when the multi-start budget
is small (\citeproc{ref-hippbauer2006}{43}). One-step estimation of a covariate in the
class-membership model, as the \texttt{classmb\ =\ \textasciitilde{}bm} specification does, is
the standard approach, and its alternatives are the three-step
methods of Vermunt (\citeproc{ref-vermunt2010}{44}) and Asparouhov and Muthén
(\citeproc{ref-asparouhov2014}{45}).

The earlier draft attributed the inflation of the gating Wald test to
the overextraction mechanism of Bauer and Curran (\citeproc{ref-bauercurran2003}{27}).
That mechanism concerns spurious classes extracted from a single
non-normal population. The null DGP here is a single multivariate
normal, so the pilot does not test that mechanism. The inflation is
the non-regular testing problem above.

The corrected study separates the two questions. It tests two classes
against one with a single-resample parametric-bootstrap
likelihood-ratio test. Under the null, one bootstrap sample from the
one-class fit is drawn per replicate and the two fits are repeated on
it. Across replicates the bootstrap statistics estimate the null
distribution of the likelihood ratio, and each replicate's observed
statistic is compared with that distribution. This is the
single-resample form of the bootstrap test. Conditional on two
classes, the test of whether the biomarker predicts class membership
is a regular problem and the Wald test on \(\psi_g\) applies. The
BLRT-gated gating test rejects when both the bootstrap test and the
gating Wald reject.

\subsection{Analyses and test forms}\label{analyses-and-test-forms}

The linear mixed model is \(Y_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_B B_i +
\beta_T w_t + \beta_D D_{bc,it} + \beta_{bm:D} B_i D_{bc,it} +
u_i + \varepsilon_{it}\), with a random intercept and a
continuous-time AR(1) residual correlation, fitted by \texttt{nlme::lme}.
The test of \(\beta_{bm:D}\) is the Wald test on \texttt{bm:Dbc}, with
model-based standard errors and, in the corrected study, also with
CR2 cluster-robust standard errors and Satterthwaite degrees of
freedom. The class-aware model is \texttt{lcmm::hlme} with the same fixed
effects, a random intercept, class-specific coefficients on
\(D_{bc,it}\) (\texttt{mixture\ =\ \textasciitilde{}Dbc}), and the biomarker in the
class-membership model (\texttt{classmb\ =\ \textasciitilde{}bm}). The one-class fit anchors
the starting values of a \texttt{gridsearch} with five starts and 30
iterations for the two-class fit, followed by a final fit with 100
iterations. The class-aware test forms are the unconditional Wald on
\(\psi_g\), the within-class Wald on \texttt{bm:Dbc} from the two-class fit,
the BIC-gated gating test, which rejects when BIC prefers two classes
and the gating Wald rejects, and, in the corrected study, the
BLRT-gated gating test. BIC selection of two classes is reported as
an estimand in its own right.

On the open-label design of the pilot and of the production null
cells, every post-baseline visit is on drug, so \(D_{bc,it}\) equals 1
at every post-baseline visit and 0 at baseline. \texttt{lme\_analysis()}
detects that no participant has within-person variation in drug
state and fits \texttt{Sx\ \textasciitilde{}\ bm\ +\ t\ +\ bm:t}. The reported coefficient is
\texttt{bm:t}, a biomarker-by-linear-time term. The \texttt{hlme} wrapper fits
\texttt{Sx\ \textasciitilde{}\ bm\ +\ t\ +\ Dbc\ +\ bm:Dbc}, in which \texttt{Dbc} is a post-baseline
indicator. The two arms of the pilot therefore fitted different fixed
effects, and neither tested \(\beta_{bm:D}\) as defined above. The
carryover half-life of 1.0 week set in the pilot had no effect,
because \(t_{sd,it} = 0\) at every visit.

\subsection{Performance measures}\label{performance-measures}

For each cell we report the number of replicates, the fraction of
two-class fits that converged (\texttt{conv\ =\ 1}), the rejection rate at
\(\alpha = 0.05\) with its MCSE for each test form over converged fits,
the fraction of fits in which BIC preferred two classes, \(\kappa\) for
the linear mixed model and for the gating coefficient, and the
dispersion of the gating estimate as the median \(|\hat\psi_g|\) and
the fraction with \(|\hat\psi_g| > 10\). Non-convergence is reported as
an outcome. A test whose realized size is far below \(\alpha\) is
reported as conservative, not as controlled, because its power is not
comparable with the power of a test at nominal size.

\subsection{The pilot and the production null cells}\label{the-pilot-and-the-production-null-cells}

The pilot used an open-label design with a baseline visit and eight
post-baseline visits at 2.5-week intervals, all on drug, with one
randomization path, constant expectancy and no blinding. The total
sample size was \(N = 70\). Three cells were run at gating slopes
\(\psi_g \in \{0, 1.0, 1.5\}\) under the class-scaling DGP, with 240
replicates each. The production null cells used the same design under
the original DGP at \(c_{bm} = 0\), at \(N = 35\) and \(N = 70\), with
2,000 replicates each. The 100-replicate pre-flight of the earlier
draft used the same design at \(N = 70\) under the null.

\subsection{The corrected study}\label{the-corrected-study}

The corrected study uses the Hybrid design: open-label titration,
blinded discontinuation, then a brief crossover, with a baseline visit
and visits at weeks 4, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 16 and 20, and four
randomization paths. The total sample size is \(N \in \{35, 70, 140\}\)
across paths, so \(N = 140\) allocates about 35 per path. The DGP cells
are the single-Gaussian null (the original DGP at \(c_{bm} = 0\)), the
class-scaling DGP at \(\psi_g \in \{1.0, 1.5\}\), the random-slopes DGP
at the same gating slopes, and a correlation-encoding cell with
\(c_{bm}\) matched to the marginal on-drug \(\mathrm{Cor}(B, BR)\) of the
mixture cells. The analyses have identical fixed effects: the linear
mixed model with model-based and with CR2 inference, \texttt{hlme} with one
and two classes, the unconditional gating Wald, the within-class
\texttt{bm:Dbc} Wald, BIC selection, and the single-resample bootstrap
likelihood-ratio test with the BLRT-gated gating test. The driver is
\texttt{analysis/scripts/latent-class-mixture-application/12-referee-rerun.R}.
It writes \texttt{summary.rds}, \texttt{calibration.rds} and \texttt{all.rds} to
\texttt{analysis/data/latent-class-mixture-application/referee-rerun/}. The
seed scheme follows the production driver: a fixed seed base plus the
replicate index plus a cell offset, with \texttt{mc.set.seed\ =\ FALSE}. The
seed base and the replicate count per cell are recorded in the driver
and in \texttt{summary.rds}.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

The pilot and the production null cells establish two facts. An
unconditional Wald test on the gating slope of a two-class mixed model
is anti-conservative at \(N = 35\) and \(N = 70\), and the inflation does
not decline with \(N\). This is the expected behavior of a Wald test on
a parameter that exists only under the alternative. A BIC gate removes
the inflation by rarely firing. Neither fact bears on whether a
class-aware analysis can compete with a linear mixed model when the
class structure is real, because the open-label design supplies no
off-drug contrast and the linear mixed model of the pilot tested a
different term. The corrected study answers that question with
matched fixed effects on the Hybrid design and with a bootstrap test
of class count.

Three questions remain for the corrected study. The first is whether
a class-aware test is usable as a primary analysis at any N-of-1
sample size, or only as a sensitivity analysis. The second is how the
analysis half-life should be chosen when the DGP is a mixture, since
class-conditional carryover may differ. The third is whether a design
that maximizes the per-participant contrast gains power under a
latent-class DGP that the Hybrid design surrenders.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{limitations}

The stored results come from one design that carries no interaction
information. The linear mixed model's conservatism on that design is
reported but not diagnosed. The production run was incomplete, and
its non-Gaussian and two-class cells were never produced. The
corrected study uses a single-resample bootstrap test, which estimates
the null distribution across replicates rather than within each
replicate. The two-class \texttt{hlme} fits use a multi-start budget of five
starts and 30 iterations, and the fraction of fits at the iteration
cap is not stored. The application to the prazosin-PTSD data of the
original study has not been carried out.

\section{Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

The latent-class encoding of a biomarker interaction is a growth
mixture model with the biomarker as a class-membership covariate. Its
marginal correlation equals the geometric mean of the between-class
variance fractions of the biomarker and the drug response, so a
single multivariate normal reproduces its second moments when the
biomarker and the drug response are independent given class. Testing
the gating slope without first establishing two classes is a
non-regular problem, and the pilot's unconditional Wald test was
anti-conservative at every sample size examined. The corrected study
on the Hybrid design, with matched fixed effects and a bootstrap test
of class count, is the comparison this paper set out to make.

\section*{Glossary}\label{glossary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Glossary}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Gating slope} (\(\psi_g\), \texttt{gs}): the coefficient of the
  standardized biomarker in the logistic class-membership model; the
  steepness of \(\omega(B_i)\) in \(b_i\).
\item
  \textbf{\texttt{ng}}: the number of latent classes in an \texttt{lcmm::hlme} fit; one
  or two in this paper.
\item
  \textbf{BIC gate}: the rule that the gating Wald test is performed only
  when the two-class fit has a lower BIC than the one-class fit; the
  stored quantity is \texttt{delta\_bic}, BIC of the one-class fit minus BIC
  of the two-class fit, and the gate opens when it is positive.
\item
  \textbf{BLRT gate}: the rule that the gating Wald test is performed only
  when the single-resample parametric-bootstrap likelihood-ratio test
  rejects one class in favor of two.
\item
  \textbf{Single-resample bootstrap}: one bootstrap sample per replicate
  from the one-class fit, with the null distribution of the
  likelihood-ratio statistic estimated across replicates.
\item
  \textbf{OL}: the open-label design, in which every post-baseline visit
  is on drug and there is one randomization path.
\item
  \textbf{OL+BDC}: open label followed by blinded discontinuation, with
  two randomization paths.
\item
  \textbf{Hybrid design} (\texttt{Hybrid}): open-label titration, blinded
  discontinuation, then a brief crossover, with visits at weeks 4, 8,
  9, 10, 11, 12, 16 and 20 and four randomization paths.
\item
  \textbf{\texttt{bm:Dbc}}: the biomarker-by-exposure interaction term of the
  analysis model, whose coefficient is \(\beta_{bm:D}\).
\item
  \textbf{Exposure-decayed drug indicator} (\(D_{bc}\), \texttt{Dbc}): 1 on drug,
  \(\exp(-\lambda t_{sd})\) off drug after the first exposure, and 0 at
  off-drug visits before the first exposure.
\item
  \textbf{Design expectancy} (\(D_e\), \texttt{De}, \(\eta_t\)): the participant's
  probability of being on drug by design, 1 open label, 0.5 blinded
  and 0 at baseline.
\item
  \textbf{Entropy} (\texttt{entropy}): one minus the mean over participants of
  the posterior class-membership entropy divided by \(\ln(\texttt{ng})\),
  as computed in the \texttt{hlme} wrapper; 1 means perfectly separated
  classes.
\item
  \textbf{Class-scaling DGP} (\texttt{02-heterogeneous-slopes-dgp.R}): a trial
  drawn from the original DGP at \(c_{bm} = 0\) in which the
  drug-response component at on-drug visits is multiplied by 1 in the
  responder class and by 0 in the non-responder class, with class
  membership from the logistic gate.
\item
  \textbf{Random-slopes DGP} (\texttt{07-true-heterogeneous-slopes-dgp.R}): the
  Study 3 DGP, in which each participant's drug-effect slope is drawn
  from a class-conditional normal distribution, with class membership
  from the logistic gate.
\item
  \textbf{Correlation encoding} (\texttt{mvn}): the interaction is a correlation
  \(c_{bm}\) between the biomarker and the drug response that is present
  on drug and absent off drug.
\item
  \textbf{Mean-shift encoding} (\texttt{mean\_moderation}): the interaction is a
  shift of the drug-response mean by the centered biomarker, with
  coefficient \(\beta_{bm}\).
\item
  \textbf{Original DGP}: the data-generating process of Hendrickson et al.
  (2020), with the correlation encoding.
\item
  \textbf{Within-class Wald}: the Wald test on \texttt{bm:Dbc} from the two-class
  \texttt{hlme} fit, in which the coefficient is common to the classes.
\item
  \textbf{Calibration ratio} (\(\kappa\)): the mean model standard error
  divided by the empirical standard deviation of the estimates;
  \(\kappa > 1\) means a conservative test.
\item
  \textbf{MCSE}: the Monte Carlo standard error of a rejection rate,
  \(\sqrt{\hat\pi(1-\hat\pi)/n}\) over \(n\) replicates.
\end{itemize}
