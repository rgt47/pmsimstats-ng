\section*{Abstract}\label{abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

\textbf{Background.} Aggregated N-of-1 trials pool repeated within-patient
on/off contrasts to validate predictive biomarkers. Drug carryover
attenuates the biomarker-by-treatment interaction. The analyst must
decide how to represent residual exposure, how to size the test, and
whether the half-life must be known.

\textbf{Methods.} We ran an exploratory simulation under the structure of
a companion paper's data-generating process, with a predictive-only
biomarker. We compared an analysis ladder in six scenario families.
Rung 0 is the starting analysis (A0). Rung 1 is a binary drug
indicator with \texttt{corCAR1} residual correlation (A1). Rung 2 adds CR2
cluster-robust inference (A2). Rung 3 is an exposure-weighted
predictor at the true half-life (A3, an oracle). Rung 4 chooses the
half-life by AIC on the model without the interaction (A4).

\textbf{Results.} Pooled over three designs in the base grid, A0 rejected
the null at 0.107 and A1 at
0.038. CR2 moved size to 0.054.
In the Hybrid design A4 exceeded A2, the CR2 binary indicator, by
0.4 points on average over
40 alternative cells. The margin reached
4.1 points at long half-lives. A4 was
below A2 in 13 cells, by at most
1.5 point. In the run with independent
seeds, the size of A4 minus that of the oracle was
+0.0008 (90 percent interval
-0.0020 to +0.0035),
equivalent within \(\pm 0.01\). For 80 percent power at a 1-week
half-life, a Hybrid trial analyzed with A4 needs about
154 patients at
\(c_{bm} = 0.4\) and 613 at
0.2.

\textbf{Conclusions.} For a Hybrid trial with a biomarker known to be
predictive only, under this process, A4 is a correctly sized test.
This held at every sample size checked, from \(N = 35\) to
1,012. A4
needs no half-life. Its standard error is too small under the alternative, so
it should be used for the test, not for an interval. Its gain
over the binary indicator is modest except at long half-lives. Even
the oracle recovers only part of the power that carryover removes.
The rest has to be met by the design. Whether a biomarker is also
prognostic is rarely known before a trial. In that case the analysis of the
companion paper applies.
This paper's results bear on its choice of half-life and its CR2
test. The results are conditional on the correlation encoding. A
second parameter set gave the same ranking of the analyses.

\textbf{Keywords:} N-of-1 trials; carryover; predictive biomarker;
cluster-robust inference; post-selection inference; simulation
study.

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

In a single N-of-1 trial, one patient is crossed repeatedly between
active treatment and a comparator. The within-patient contrast
between on-drug and off-drug periods estimates that patient's
individual treatment effect. An aggregated N-of-1 trial pools many
such series. Pooling allows population-level inference at sample
sizes smaller than a parallel-group trial would require
(\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{1}). The estimand here is the biomarker-treatment
interaction, that is, whether a baseline covariate \(B_i\) modifies
the treatment effect. We evaluate analysis approaches for this
estimand in the Hybrid N-of-1 design defined in Section \ref{sec-designs}.

The main inferential complication in N-of-1 trials is carryover. A
drug's pharmacological effect does not stop at discontinuation. It
decays as a function of the drug's half-life. Observations taken
after discontinuation therefore still carry part of the treatment
effect. An exposure regressor that ignores this decay is a
mismeasured version of what the patient received. The consequences
are twofold. First, the interaction contrast is attenuated.
Occasions that still carry a large fraction of the drug effect are
coded the same as occasions that carry none. This biases the
biomarker-by-exposure coefficient toward zero, as any covariate
measured with error would. Second, those occasions vary in a way the
model does not represent. The unexplained variation inflates the
standard error of the same coefficient. Power falls for both
reasons.

This is a loss of power, not of validity. Under the null,
\(c_{bm} = 0\), there is no biomarker-response association at any
level of residual exposure. A miscoded regressor then has no
interaction to distort, and test size does not depend on the
exposure coding. The conservatism reported in Section
\ref{sec-rung2} has a separate origin in the working covariance. It
affects both exposure codings, the exposure-weighted predictor
slightly more. Carryover misspecification is thus a question of
efficiency, and rung 3 of the study is framed in terms of recovered
power.

A companion study (\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats_dgp_architectures2026}{2}) builds on the
data-generating process of Hendrickson et al. (\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{1}).
In that process the interaction is encoded as a correlation between
the biomarker and the drug response. The correlation is present on
drug and decays off drug: \(\text{Cor}(B_i, BR_{it}) = c_{bm} \cdot
\phi(t_{sd})\). We call this the correlation encoding of the
interaction. The companion study shows that carryover reduces power
under this encoding, and that the reduction depends on the design.
The loss under a given analysis is governed by the on/off correlation
difference. That is the on-drug correlation minus the mean off-drug
correlation, which the analysis's exposure coding determines. At a
half-life of one week in this study's factorial grid, the binary
indicator loses 43 to 63 percent of its power under the
Hybrid and OL+BDC designs. Under the crossover design the
relative loss is -14 to 20 percent, where a negative loss is a gain
(Section \ref{sec-rung3}). We restrict attention to the correlation
encoding. The carryover problem is largest there, and the starting DGP
uses it. Earlier work under this encoding fixed the decay shape at
exponential and evaluated only one analysis specification
(\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{1}). Simulation studies of aggregated N-of-1 analyses
under carryover exist (\citeproc{ref-chen2014}{3}--\citeproc{ref-gartner2023}{5}).
Percha et al. (\citeproc{ref-percha2019}{6}) simulate single-patient series with
carryover. Gärtner et al. (\citeproc{ref-gartner2023}{5}) include an exponentially
decaying wash-in and wash-out term. Their simulated treatment effects
also depend on patient covariates. Their test, however, is of the
treatment effect averaged over patients. None of these studies tests
a biomarker-by-treatment interaction, which is the estimand here.
Liao et al. (\citeproc{ref-liao2023}{7}) estimate carryover from the data with a
distributed-lag model, again for the treatment effect. For a binary
subgroup of equal size, an interaction as large as the main effect
needs about four times the sample of the main-effect test
(\citeproc{ref-brookes2004}{8}). The biomarker here is continuous, so the factor is a
guide only. The correlation
encoding is a modeling assumption of this project, not a validated
description of any trial. Every recommendation below is conditional
on it.

This manuscript shows how a sequence of changes to the analysis model
improves both size and power. We call the sequence an analysis ladder.
Each rung changes one thing about the analysis. The rungs follow the
order in which the questions arise for an analyst. Section
\ref{sec-ladder-def} defines the rungs and Table
\ref{tab:tab-spec-summary} lists them.

Rung 0 is the starting analysis (A0), the analysis used with the
starting DGP (\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{1}). It fits a random intercept, uses a binary
on-drug indicator, and has no residual correlation structure. The
first question is whether its test is correctly sized. Rung 1 (A1)
keeps the binary indicator and uses an AR(1) residual correlation
through \texttt{corCAR1} with the working-covariance standard error. A0 and
A1 differ in three respects at once. Isolation analyses separate the three and
attribute the liberal size of A0 to its missing residual correlation
(Section \ref{sec-rung1}).

The second question concerns the size of the test. The working
covariance of the mixed model is a random intercept with an AR(1)
residual correlation. It does not match the covariance that the
data-generating process induces, and the working-covariance test is
conservative. Rung 2 (A2) replaces the working-covariance standard error by a
CR2 bias-reduced cluster-robust standard error with Satterthwaite
degrees of freedom (\citeproc{ref-bell2002bias}{9},\citeproc{ref-pustejovsky2018}{10}). The rung
changes only the
standard error, the reference distribution and the \(p\)-value.

The third question is how residual exposure should enter the linear
predictor. The analyst may ignore the decay and code exposure as on or
off, as the starting analysis does (A1 and A2). Or the analyst may
represent it by committing to a decay form and half-life in a
continuous exposure-weighted predictor. The predictor changes the
regressor that carries the interaction. Rung 3 (A3) is the
exposure-weighted predictor at the true half-life with CR2 inference.
We compare the two positions against exponential and Weibull decay
forms in a full factorial.

The fourth question follows from the third position. Rung 3 uses the
true half-life, which no real analyst knows, so A3 is an oracle
benchmark rather than a candidate analysis. Rung 4 (A4) lets the data
choose the half-life by AIC over a candidate grid. The selection uses
fits of the model without the interaction. A4 then tests the
interaction at the chosen value. The selection step adds a post-selection
inference risk
(\citeproc{ref-berk2013valid}{11}--\citeproc{ref-kuchibhotla2022}{13}). Leaving the tested
term out of the selection limits that risk. We measure what the
selection costs against the oracle in size, power and standard-error
calibration.

We then stress the ladder in scenario families that a real trial may
present. These are a misspecified half-life and decay shape, serial
correlation, patient dropout and a patient-specific slope variance.

The biomarker in this study is predictive only. It changes the drug
response and nothing else. A biomarker that is also
prognostic raises a different question. The interaction test may then
confuse an association with the natural history or the placebo
response with moderation of the drug effect. A prognostic shift that is
constant
over visits is absorbed exactly by the biomarker main effect of every
analysis here. It cannot move the interaction estimate, so it needs no
simulation. Prognostic associations that vary over time are the
subject of a companion paper (\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats_prognostic2026}{14}). There the
design-expectancy terms remove the bias from a biomarker that predicts
the placebo response. A biomarker that predicts the natural-history
trend biases every analysis of this paper. The companion paper also
needs the baseline visit as an outcome row to control that bias.

Two defects in the simulation code were found during the study and
corrected. The first was a pre-exposure coding error in the
exposure-weighted predictor that affected the crossover design. The
second was a cross-factor correlation constant that departed from the
documented data-generating process. Every result in this manuscript
was produced under the corrected code. We
follow the ADEMP reporting framework of Morris, White, and Crowther
(\citeproc{ref-morris2019simulation}{15}) throughout. We also follow later reporting
guidance on common random numbers and on comparative simulation
studies (\citeproc{ref-siepe2024}{16},\citeproc{ref-pawel2024}{17}). The study is exploratory. Its rungs
and scenarios were not fixed in advance. The recommendation is the end
of a documented sequence of choices rather than a pre-specified
comparison. Section \ref{sec-design} describes the simulation design
in ADEMP order. Section \ref{sec-results} gives the results rung by
rung and then by scenario. Section \ref{sec-discussion} states the
recommendation and its limits.

\section{Simulation design}\label{sec-design}

The study simulates complete aggregated N-of-1 trials and analyzes
each one under every specification. A single replicate proceeds as
follows. Patients are allocated across the randomization paths of
the chosen trial design (Section \ref{sec-designs}). For each
patient we draw a pre-treatment biomarker together with three latent response
components. These are the drug-response component, the
natural-history component, and the placebo-response component. The
four quantities are drawn jointly across all eight measurement
occasions from a multivariate normal distribution. The means follow
modified Gompertz trajectories. The covariance matrix contains
within-component AR(1) autocorrelation, cross-component
correlations, and the biomarker-response correlation. That last
correlation encodes the interaction and depends on treatment state
(Section \ref{sec-dgm}). The observed symptom score at each occasion is the
patient's baseline
severity minus the sum of the three components. A beneficial effect
therefore lowers the score. Carryover enters through a decay
function of time since discontinuation. The decay keeps part of the
drug-response component present at nominally off-drug occasions.
Every analysis specification in a given cell is then fitted to the
same simulated dataset. We record the interaction coefficient, its
standard error, and its \(p\)-value from each. This is repeated 500
times for every parameter combination in the grid of Section
\ref{sec-grid}. It is repeated 1,000 to 5,000 times in the Hybrid
run with independent seeds, and 2,000 times in the supplementary size
diagnostic.

Fitting all specifications to a common dataset is deliberate. It
makes every contrast among them paired. Paired differences have
substantially less Monte Carlo variance than differences between
independent draws. Throughout, \(N\) denotes the \textbf{total} number of
patients in a trial. Patients are allocated as evenly as possible
across the design's randomization paths, so a design with \(P\) paths
assigns about \(N/P\) patients to each. At \(N = 70\) this is 35 per
path under the two-path designs and 17 or 18 per path under the
four-path Hybrid design.

\subsection{Aims}\label{sec-aims}

We have four aims. The first is to show how each change to the
analysis model alters size and power in the biomarker-treatment
interaction test. The
setting is a Hybrid aggregated N-of-1 trial under the correlation
encoding. The changes form a ladder of five rungs (Table
\ref{tab:tab-spec-summary}). Rung 0 is the starting analysis. Rung 1
uses a binary drug indicator with a residual AR(1) correlation. Rung 2
adds CR2 cluster-robust inference to fix size. Rung 3 replaces the
binary indicator by an exposure-weighted predictor at the true
half-life. Its purpose is to recover part of the power that carryover
removes. Rung 4 chooses the half-life by AIC, on the model without the
interaction, to remove the need to know it. Each
rung is judged against its predecessor by a paired contrast on the
same simulated trials. A side analysis measures what it costs to
choose the half-life with the interaction in the model instead.

The second aim is to find where a rung does not help. The scenario
families of Table \ref{tab:tab-families} stress the ladder with a
misspecified half-life and decay shape, serial correlation, dropout
and a patient-specific slope variance. The third aim is to arrive at
a recommended analysis for the Hybrid design. We also state its
conditions and limits. A fourth aim is to document
the evidence for each rung so that a reader can check it.

\subsection{Trial designs}\label{sec-designs}

The three designs differ in how on-drug and off-drug occasions are
arranged. They therefore differ in how much post-discontinuation
information each generates. All three schedule eight measurement
occasions per patient. Each randomization path is a fixed
on-drug/off-drug schedule. Each design also carries an expectancy
weight, defined as the participant's probability of being on drug
by design. The weight is one during open-label periods and one half
during blinded periods. It scales the placebo-response component.

\textbf{Crossover (CO).} A traditional two-period within-patient
crossover, blinded throughout. Occasions are spaced 2.5 weeks apart
from 2.5 to 20 weeks. One path is active for the first four
occasions and then on comparator. The other path reverses that
order, so it never discontinues and contributes no
post-discontinuation observations.

\textbf{Open-label followed by blinded discontinuation (OL+BDC).}
Open-label titration at 4, 8, 12, and 16 weeks is followed by weekly
follow-up to week 20. A blinded late switch to placebo occurs during
the follow-up. The two
paths discontinue after week 17 or 18, so only two or three
occasions follow.

\textbf{Hybrid.} The reference design of this program, taken from the
starting DGP. It begins with open-label titration at weeks 4 and 8.
Blinded discontinuation follows weekly from week 9 to 12. A brief
two-occasion crossover at weeks 16 and 20 completes it. The four paths vary
discontinuation timing and crossover-arm order. It has the most
on/off transitions of the three designs. It is the only one that
allows more than one discontinuation per patient. The recommendation
of this manuscript is made for this design. CO and OL+BDC are
carried through the factorial grid and the corrected grid. They show
where the recommendation does and does not transfer.

One consequence bears directly on the results. The first
post-discontinuation occasion falls one week after switching under
OL+BDC and Hybrid, but 2.5 weeks after switching under CO. At the
half-lives examined here, roughly half the drug effect persists into
the first off-drug occasion under the two non-crossover designs. Less
than a fifth persists under CO. The designs therefore differ
substantially in how much carryover they can accumulate.

\subsection{Data-generating mechanism}\label{sec-dgm}

Under the correlation encoding, the biomarker and the drug-response
component are drawn jointly from a multivariate normal distribution.
Their correlation depends on treatment state: \(\text{Cor}(B_i,
BR_{it}) = c_{bm} \cdot \phi(t_{sd})\), where \(\phi\) is the decay
form and \(t_{sd}\) is the time since drug discontinuation. The
data-generating process has the structure of the recommended DGP of
the companion study. Its parameter values are not those of the
companion study (Table \ref{tab:tab-params}). Two parameter sets
were run. Parameter set 1, used throughout this paper, is the
starting values of Hendrickson et al.~at commit 58b32a9, which the
companion paper also uses. Parameter set 2 is the simulation core's
own set (\texttt{simcore2026}). Its response SDs and its biomarker and
baseline scales are not Hendrickson's values. The placebo-response
SD in particular is \(2\eta_t\) in set 2 against \(10\eta_t\) in set 1,
where \(\eta_t\) is the design expectancy. This makes the placebo
pathway much weaker in set 2. Parameter set 2 serves as a sensitivity
analysis (Section \ref{sec-sens-params}). The structure differs from the
starting DGP in four respects. The biomarker-response correlation is
zero before first exposure and decays after discontinuation at the carryover
half-life. The within-component correlation is a separable AR(1)
structure at \(\rho = 0.7\). That is, every component has the same
first-order autoregressive correlation. A single cross-component
constant \(c_1 = 0.2\) is scaled by \(\rho^{|w_t - w_s|}\) at every lag.
Carryover on the drug-response mean decays from the last on-drug
mean. A covariance matrix that is not positive definite stops the
simulation rather than being repaired (Section
\ref{sec-corrections}).

The decay function \(\phi : \mathbb{R}_+ \to [0, 1]\) maps \(t_{sd}\) to
a residual-effect multiplier. Two forms are used. Each is matched to
a common half-life \(t_{1/2}\). Exponential decay,

\[\phi_{\text{exp}}(t_{sd}) =
  \exp\left(-\lambda t_{sd}\right), \qquad
  \lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{t_{1/2}},\]

is classical first-order elimination and the default pharmacokinetic
assumption. Weibull decay,

\[\phi_{\text{weib}}(t_{sd}) =
  \exp\left(-\left(\lambda_w t_{sd}\right)^{k}\right), \qquad
  \lambda_w = \frac{(\ln 2)^{1/k}}{t_{1/2}},\]

has a shape parameter \(k\). The elimination tail is heavier than
exponential when \(k < 1\) and lighter when \(k > 1\). The form reduces
to exponential at \(k = 1\). The factorial grid uses \(k \in \{0.7,
1.5\}\) alongside exponential. The decay-shape misspecification cells
(family 2) use \(k \in \{0.5, 2\}\). All forms pass through the same
half-life-defining point, \(\phi = 0.5\) at \(t_{sd} = t_{1/2}\). They are
therefore comparable at a fixed nominal half-life, even though their
shapes differ on either side of it. The decay form the analysis
assumes differs by scenario. In the factorial grid the
exposure-weighted predictor is built with the data-generating form,
shape and half-life. This is the driver's default when no analysis
form is given. The Weibull cells of the factorial grid therefore test
the representation under a correctly specified decay. They do not
test its robustness to the shape. Every other analysis assumes
exponential decay. The Weibull cells of family 2 are the
shape-misspecification
check.

\subsection{Estimand and analysis specifications}\label{sec-spec}

The target of inference is the on-drug biomarker-response
correlation \(c_{bm}\), a dimensionless quantity. Every analysis tests
it through the coefficient of \(\text{bm} \times X\). Its target on the
calibrated scale is \(\theta = -c_{bm}\sigma_{BR}\), with
\(\sigma_{BR} = 8\) in this parameter set (Table \ref{tab:tab-params}).
That is the expected difference in on-drug response per SD of the
biomarker.
Bias, empirical SE and coverage are reported on this scale. Type I
error is the rejection rate at \(c_{bm} = 0\) and power the rejection
rate at \(c_{bm} > 0\). What the coefficient estimates depends on \(X\).
Under A3, \(X\) is exposure-weighted at the true half-life and the
coefficient targets \(\theta\). Under A1 and A2, \(X\) is the binary
indicator, which codes residual exposure off drug as none, so under
carryover the coefficient differs from \(\theta\). Under A4 the
half-life is selected, and the coefficient targets \(\theta\) only when
the selected value is the true one. The base analysis model is
\texttt{nlme::lme(Sx\ \textasciitilde{}\ bm\ *\ X\ +\ t,\ random\ =\ \textasciitilde{}1\ \textbar{}\ ptID,\ correlation\ =\ corCAR1(form\ =\ \textasciitilde{}\ t\ \textbar{}\ ptID))}.
The analyses differ in how \(X\) is constructed and how the standard
error is obtained. This manuscript labels them A0 to A4 by rung (Table
\ref{tab:tab-spec-summary}). The table also gives the code each
analysis carries in the companion papers and earlier drafts, where A4
is G10. It gives as well the \texttt{E}-prefixed code under which each is
stored in the code and data files (Reproducibility section).

\subsubsection{The analysis ladder}\label{sec-ladder-def}

The ladder orders the analyses by what each one changes. Table
\ref{tab:tab-spec-summary} gives the formula, the inference and the
assumptions of each. Rung 0 is the starting analysis (A0). Rung 1 is
the binary-indicator analysis with \texttt{corCAR1} residual correlation and
the working-covariance standard error (A1). Rung 2 is A1 with CR2
cluster-robust inference (A2). Rung 3 replaces the binary indicator by
the exposure-weighted predictor at the true half-life, with CR2 (A3).
Rung 4 chooses the half-life by AIC on the model without the
interaction, with CR2 (A4). Each rung changes one thing from its
predecessor, with one exception. The step from A0 to A1 changes three
things at once. The isolation analyses E0n, E0nc and E1nc separate
them (Section \ref{sec-rung1}). A3 uses the true half-life. No
analyst has it, so A3 is an oracle benchmark, not a candidate
analysis. It shows what the selection step of rung 4 costs.

\subsubsection{Rung 0: the starting analysis (A0)}\label{sec-e0}

\textbf{A0} is the starting analysis, adapted from the analysis published
with the starting DGP (\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{1}). We read the published
analysis from the published repository (commit \texttt{58b32a9},
\texttt{R/lme\_analysis.R}, and the driver of its publication results). We
did not use this project's vendored copy, which had acquired a
residual correlation structure at some later point. The published
analysis fits \texttt{lme4::lmer} with a random intercept and no residual
correlation structure. It uses the binary on-drug indicator and takes
Satterthwaite \(p\)-values from \texttt{lmerTest}. It also adds a baseline row
for each patient, an off-drug outcome at time zero, and it has no
expectancy term. A0 departs from it in two respects. It adds the
expectancy main effect \(D_e\) whenever expectancy varies within the
design, and it has no baseline row, because the analyzed data of this
study start at the first post-baseline visit. Under the crossover
design expectancy is constant, so \(D_e\) drops out. A0 also drops
patients who are never on drug. A0 differs from A1 in three respects
at once. These are the absence of \texttt{corCAR1}, the \(D_e\) term, and the
software and degrees-of-freedom method. A separate simulation changes
them one at a time (E0n, E0nc and E1nc; Section \ref{sec-rung1}). A
second one starts from the published analysis itself (Section
\ref{sec-rung1}).

\subsubsection{Rung 1: binary indicator with residual correlation (A1)}\label{sec-g1}

Here \(X = D_{it}\), the binary on-drug indicator (one on drug, zero
off). Carryover is not represented. An off-drug occasion that still
carries most of the drug effect is coded the same as one that
carries none. This is the default an analyst obtains without
considering carryover. We treat it as the benchmark against which
the other specifications must justify their additional structure.
It uses no half-life, so it does not depend on the analyst's
pharmacokinetic assumptions.

\subsubsection{Rung 2: CR2 cluster-robust inference (A2 to A4)}\label{sec-cr2-method}

The standard error of \(\beta_{bm:X}\) read from the fitted \texttt{lme}
object is valid only if the assumed residual covariance is correct.
The working covariance combines a patient random intercept with a
\texttt{corCAR1} AR(1) residual correlation. The companion calibration
study (\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats_calibration2026}{18}) shows that this does not match
the covariance the data-generating process induces. We quantify the
consequence with the calibration ratio

\[\kappa = \frac{\overline{\widehat{SE}}}{SD\big(\hat\beta_{bm:X}\big)},\]

the mean model-reported standard error across replicates divided by
the empirical standard deviation of the replicate point estimates.
\(\kappa = 1\) is correctly calibrated and \(\kappa > 1\) is a
conservative test. The mean of the standard errors is below their
root mean square. \(\kappa\) as defined is therefore biased downward
when the standard errors vary across replicates. We keep the mean
because every stored driver output uses it. With the root mean square
instead, the family-level \(\kappa\) of the CR2 analyses rises by
0.01 to 0.04. The rise is
slightly larger for A4, whose standard errors vary more with the
selected half-life. A2, A3 and A4 are close enough that their order
can change. The alternative \(\kappa\) of A4 then reaches
0.97 to
1.02 by family. The empirical
SD in the denominator is also sensitive to a heavy-tailed estimate
distribution. Section \ref{sec-dropout} meets that case under heavy
missingness and reports a robust ratio beside it. Coverage of the nominal 95\%
interval is the complementary
measure. It is reported where the stored per-replicate output allows
it. The remedy for the mismatch is a cluster-robust sandwich
variance. It derives the variance of \(\hat\beta_{bm:X}\) from
observed between-patient variation rather than from the assumed
covariance. With only \(N = 35\) or \(70\) clusters the naive
Liang-Zeger form is itself biased downward. We therefore use the CR2
bias-reduced estimator of Bell and McCaffrey (\citeproc{ref-bell2002bias}{9}) with
Satterthwaite degrees of freedom, as implemented in \texttt{clubSandwich}
(\citeproc{ref-pustejovsky2018}{10}). It replaces only the standard error, the
reference distribution and the \(p\)-value. Point estimates are
unchanged. The Kenward-Roger correction (\citeproc{ref-kenward1997}{19}) is the
standard small-sample correction within the working covariance. It
corrects the degrees of freedom and the variance under the working
covariance, so it does not address a misspecified one. It is not
evaluated here. A2
is A1 with CR2 inference. A3 and A4 also use CR2, so A2 and A3 are the
like-for-like comparators for the power of A4. A1 is conservative.
Part of any power difference against it is the increase in power from
correcting a conservative test.

\subsubsection{Rung 3: exposure-weighted predictor (A3)}\label{sec-g3}

Here \(X = D_{bc,it}\), a continuous exposure-decayed predictor. It
is built from the analyst's assumed decay form and analysis
half-life \(t^{a}_{1/2}\). At on-drug timepoints \(D_{bc,it} = 1\). Off
drug after first exposure it decays as \(D_{bc,it} =
(1/2)^{t_{sd,it}/t^{a}_{1/2}}\). It loses half its value every
\(t^{a}_{1/2}\) weeks after discontinuation. Before any exposure
\(D_{bc,it} = 0\). This pre-exposure rule was absent in earlier drafts
(Section \ref{sec-corrections}). The half-life assumption enters only
through the predictor values passed to \texttt{lme}. The exposure-weighted
analysis is therefore a different model for each assumed half-life.
As \(t^{a}_{1/2} \rightarrow 0\), \(D_{bc,it} \rightarrow D_{it}\). The binary
indicator
of A1 and A2 is therefore the limit of the exposure-weighted
predictor at zero assumed half-life. At rung 3 the assumed half-life
is set equal to the data-generating one. This is the true half-life,
which no real analyst knows. Rung 4 removes that assumption. Under
the correlation encoding, the regression of the response on the
biomarker at an occasion is linear in \(c_{bm}\,\phi(t_{sd})\).
\(D_{bc,it}\) at the true \(\phi\) is therefore the correctly specified
regressor. In the Weibull cells the predictor is built with the
Weibull form, \(\phi_{\text{weib}}\), in place of the exponential one.
The factorial grid compares this correct regressor with the binary
indicator, which is incorrect under carryover. It measures how much
the binary indicator loses.

\subsubsection{Rung 4: AIC-selected half-life (A4)}\label{sec-g4}

A4 uses the formula of A3 but chooses the analysis half-life
\(\hat{t}^{a}_{1/2}\) from the data. Write
\(M_0(t^{a}_{1/2})\) for the \texttt{lme} fit of the model without the
interaction, \(\text{Sx} \sim bm + t + D_{bc}\), with \(D_{bc,it}\)
constructed at a candidate half-life. The selected half-life is

\[\hat{t}^{a}_{1/2} = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{h \,\in\, \mathcal{T}}
  \text{AIC}\big(M_0(h)\big), \qquad
  \mathcal{T} = \{0.25,\ 0.5,\ 1.0,\ 2.0\}\ \text{weeks},\]

with \(\text{AIC}(M) = -2\log L(M) + 2p\) (\citeproc{ref-akaike1974new}{20}). The grid
\(\mathcal{T}\) is the default of the simulation driver. It was not
tuned to these results. Its range follows from the visit spacing. The
first off-drug visit comes at least one week after the drug stops.
At \(h = 0.25\) week the predictor there is \(2^{-4} = 0.06\), so the
smallest candidate is already close to the binary indicator. Zero is
therefore not a candidate. A4 cannot return the binary indicator
exactly, but it can come within 0.06 of it at every off-drug visit.
At \(h = 2\) weeks the predictor is 0.71 one week after the drug stops.
Longer half-lives are approximated by 2. The cells of family 2 place
the true half-life at 0.2 and at 4 weeks, outside the grid, to test
this choice. The candidate fits are compared by maximum likelihood.
The candidates differ in a fixed-effects column, and REML likelihoods
are not comparable across fixed-effects designs. The model with the
interaction is then fitted by REML at \(\hat{t}^{a}_{1/2}\). The
interaction coefficient, its CR2 standard error and its \(p\)-value are
read from that single fit. They are not pooled across \(\mathcal{T}\).
The
selection model leaves out the interaction. The selection therefore
does not use the coefficient under test. It does use the same data,
so the two are not independent. The test treats \(\hat t^{a}_{1/2}\)
as fixed in advance, and a post-selection risk remains
(\citeproc{ref-berk2013valid}{11}--\citeproc{ref-kuchibhotla2022}{13}). Section
\ref{sec-rung4} measures that risk against the same predictor at the
true half-life (A3). It also reports the distribution of the selected
half-life. The selection does not recover the half-life, so the A4
coefficient does not in general target \(\theta\). A4 is evaluated here
as a test of \(c_{bm} = 0\), not as an estimator of \(\theta\).

\subsubsection{Side analysis: selecting on the interaction model}\label{sec-comparators}

The obvious alternative makes the same choice with the interaction
term in the selection model, \(\text{Sx} \sim bm + t + D_{bc} +
bm{:}D_{bc}\). It mirrors \texttt{characterize\_carryover()}
(\texttt{R/carryover\_analysis.R}), the package's own real-data
half-life-selection routine. Earlier drafts used it as the
recommended analysis. The selected half-life then depends on the
coefficient under test. We fitted it with CR2 to the same simulated
trials as A4. It rejected the null more often than A4 in every
scenario family, by +0.005 to +0.008. Its standard
error was also a few percent too small under the alternative. A4 leaves the
interaction out of the selection model for this reason. The variant is
not reported further.

\subsection{Choice of analysis specifications}\label{sec-choice}

The specifications were chosen to represent distinct, established
positions an analyst can take toward an unmeasured decay. They were
also chosen to answer the specific questions each rung raised. They
were not chosen to survey the wider space of N-of-1 analysis models.
Several other approaches are established in the N-of-1 or crossover
literature. These are Bayesian hierarchical models, GEE as a primary
model, randomization tests, flexible spline decay curves, state-space
treatments of drug concentration and two-stage meta-analytic
combination. None is evaluated here. Section \ref{sec-related}
returns to three of them. An earlier draft also
tested carryover terms interacted with the biomarker (\texttt{bm:L} and
\texttt{bm:t\_\{sd\}}). Both had lower power than A1 at every cell. We
attribute this to the data-generating process, which has a single
true decay parameter. A second interacted coefficient then adds
estimation noise with no signal to recover. The terms were dropped in
favor of choosing the decay half-life from the data (rung 4). That
choice addresses the same unknown.

\textbf{Sample-size study.} A separate run gives the total \(N\) that A4
needs for 80 percent power. It fits A4 and A2 to the same simulated
trials over a grid of \(N\). The grid covers the three designs,
\(t_{1/2} \in \{0, 0.5, 1\}\) week and \(c_{bm} \in \{0.2, 0.3, 0.4\}\).
It has five values of \(N\) per \(c_{bm}\), chosen to bracket 80 percent
power, and 500 replicates per cell. Every cell has its own seed. The
run was made under both parameter sets, because the required \(N\)
depends strongly on the variances (Table \ref{tab:tab-params}).
Within a design, half-life and analysis, power is modeled as a probit
curve in \(c_{bm}\sqrt{N}\). The curve is fitted by binomial maximum
likelihood over the 15 cells and inverted at 0.8. The
noncentrality of the interaction test is close to proportional to
\(c_{bm}\sqrt{N}\), so one curve serves all three values of \(c_{bm}\).
The reason is simple. The target coefficient is proportional to
\(c_{bm}\), and for a fixed design its standard error falls as
\(1/\sqrt{N}\). The proportionality is not exact, because the residual
variance of the drug response given the biomarker also depends on
\(c_{bm}\).
The interval for \(N\) reflects Monte Carlo error under that model only.
A second run checks size at the larger samples of this study. It
fits A4 and A2 at \(c_{bm} = 0\), for the three designs and
\(t_{1/2} \in \{0, 1\}\) week, with 1,000 replicates per cell. The
values of \(N\) are 300 and 600 under parameter set 1, and
152 and 300 under parameter set 2. Two further OL+BDC runs use
1,012 under parameter set 1 and 480 under parameter set 2, just above

\subsection{Performance measures and computing environment}\label{sec-perf}

We follow Morris et al. (\citeproc{ref-morris2019simulation}{15}, Tables 5-6) in the
choice of performance measures. For each cell we report Power, Type I
error, Bias, Empirical SE (calibrated scale), Coverage of the nominal
95\% Wald CI, MSE, non-convergence rate, and the MCSE of each. For the
analyses that select the half-life (A4), a fit can fail in the ML
selection or in the REML refit at the selected value. The stored output
records
only whether the final fit succeeded, so the non-convergence rate
bounds the refit failure rate from above. Over the
55,000 A4
fits in the cells that use it, 71
failed
(0.13
percent). We chose \(n_{sim} = 500\) so
that the MCSE on power at \(p = 0.5\) would not exceed 0.022. At
\(n_{sim} = 500\) the two-MCSE band around a nominal 0.05 rejection
rate is approximately \([0.031, 0.069]\). That band is a check on
gross inflation, not a demonstration of nominal size. A single
500-replicate cell whose true size is 0.065 falls inside the band
with probability about 0.64. One whose true size is 0.075 falls
inside it with probability about 0.31 (normal approximation). Claims
about size therefore rest on the pooled 2,000-replicate cells of
Section \ref{sec-rung4} and on paired contrasts between
specifications. Single-cell band membership is reported as such. No simulation
runs while this
manuscript renders. All specifications are fit to the same simulated
dataset within each cell (common random numbers). Every pairwise
contrast between specifications therefore has reduced Monte Carlo
variance relative to independent draws. Where a paired standard
error is quoted, it is computed from the per-replicate rejection
indicators. The drivers also seed replicate \(r\) of every cell from a
job-level base plus \(r\). Cells at the same design and \(N\) within a
job therefore share a random-number stream. Contrasts between such
cells are paired too. Counts of cells inside a band are not
independent confirmations and are not presented as such.
Comparisons across jobs, and across simulation hosts, are unpaired
and carry the full Monte Carlo error of both cells. Different hosts
do not produce identical draws. Exact file paths and versions are
given in the Reproducibility section.

\subsection{Corrections to the simulation code during the study}\label{sec-corrections}

Two defects in the simulation driver were found and corrected while
this study was in progress. One policy was also changed. Each is
stated here so that the reader can tell which results it affects.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Pre-exposure coding of the exposure-weighted predictor
  (corrected 2026-10-06).} Before any exposure, \(D_{bc,it}\) was
  coded as \(\phi(0) = 1\) rather than 0. A patient on the
  placebo-first path of the crossover design was therefore treated
  as fully exposed at every visit. The same correction changed the
  half-life selection. The REML AIC comparison was replaced with the
  ML comparison and REML refit of Section \ref{sec-g4}. A paired
  check fitted each simulated trial under the old and new coding
  (\texttt{26-co-dbc-fix-paired.R}). Earlier drafts reported a collapse of
  the exposure-weighted family under the crossover design. The check
  showed that the entire collapse was this defect. With the
  correction, the exposure-weighted analyses match A1 under CO.
  Hybrid cells were unaffected, because no patient starts on placebo
  there.
\item
  \textbf{Cross-factor correlation constant (corrected 2026-10-21).} The
  documented data-generating process uses a single cross-component
  correlation \(c_1 = 0.2\) scaled by \(\rho^{|w_t - w_s|}\) at every
  lag. The driver had used 0.1 at lags greater than zero. The
  factorial grid was rerun in full under the corrected constant.
  Every other comparison was run after it. No result from before
  this correction is reported. The 3,000-replicate size diagnostic
  of earlier drafts was replaced by the 2,000-replicate
  corrected-code diagnostic of Section \ref{sec-rung2}. The new
  diagnostic reproduces its conclusion.
\item
  \textbf{Non-positive-definite covariance matrices are refused
  (2026-10-26).} As in the recommended DGP, the driver stops on such
  a cell rather than changing it
  (\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats_dgp_architectures2026}{2}). No cell reported here was
  affected.
\end{enumerate}

The exploratory sensitivity blocks of earlier drafts predate both
corrections and are no longer part of this manuscript. Those blocks
covered within-component autocorrelation, analyst-truth half-life
mismatch, dropout, the effect-size curve, and single-cell
comparisons of further specifications and of the CR2 variants. Their
material remains in the archived drafts. The autocorrelation,
half-life and dropout scenarios were rerun under the corrected code
in the supplementary grids of Section \ref{sec-scen-grids}. The dropout block of those
drafts used the per-occasion convention described in Section \ref{sec-grid}.
Under that convention a stated rate of 0.1 to 0.3 removed 36 to 73
percent of the observations. Its rates are therefore not comparable
with the patient-level rates reported here.

\subsection{Evidence rung by rung}\label{sec-rungs}

Each subsection opens with what the rung changes and ends with what the

\subsubsection{Rung 0 to 1: residual correlation}\label{sec-rung1}

Rung 1 replaces the starting analysis (A0) by A1. A0 fits \texttt{lme4::lmer}
with a random intercept and takes Satterthwaite \(p\)-values from
\texttt{lmerTest}. It has no residual correlation structure. It adds the
expectancy main effect \(D_e\) whenever expectancy varies within the
design, and it drops patients who are never on drug (Section
\ref{sec-e0}). A1 fits
\texttt{nlme::lme} with a random intercept and a \texttt{corCAR1} residual
correlation. It has no expectancy term, keeps every patient, and uses
the working-covariance standard error. A0 and A1 therefore differ in three
respects at once. These are the software and degrees-of-freedom method,
the residual correlation, and the expectancy term with the exclusion of
patients. Table S1 reports Type I error at the six
null cells of the corrected grid. The grid has three designs and two
sample sizes, all at \(t_{1/2} = 1\) week. It has no null cell at other
half-lives outside Hybrid.

A0 rejects at 0.074 to 0.150 in these six
cells. It is above the two-MCSE band in every cell: at
0.128 to 0.150 under CO, 0.088 to 0.126 under OL+BDC
and 0.074 to 0.076 under Hybrid. A1 rejects at
0.024 to 0.052, below nominal at
5 of the six cells. The step from A0 to
A1 therefore moves the test from liberal to conservative.

\textbf{A0 is liberal in every design, least so under Hybrid.} Across the four Hybrid and OL+BDC null scenarios of
Table S7, A0 rejects at
0.094 to 0.096.
It reaches 0.096 under OL+BDC. In
the 2,000-replicate Hybrid cells of Table S3 it
is 0.067 to 0.069, above its band
(0.040 to 0.060 at 2,000 replicates). Its inflation is therefore clear in every design and smallest
under Hybrid. The raw power of A0 at
the alternatives is
not comparable with that of the other analyses because the test is
liberal. Section \ref{sec-which} compares it after adjusting every
analysis to the same size.

A separate run attributes the change from A0 to A1.
It fits five analyses to the same simulated trials and changes one
element at a time. E0n refits the formula and the sample of A0 with
\texttt{nlme} and no residual correlation. It changes only the software and
the degrees-of-freedom method. E0nc adds \texttt{corCAR1} to E0n. E1nc drops
the expectancy term and the exclusion rule of A0 and keeps \texttt{nlme}
without residual correlation. A1 adds \texttt{corCAR1} to E1nc. The run
covers the 30 cells of the grid at 1,000
replicates per cell. E0nc failed to converge in at most
5 replicates of a cell. The other four analyses
converged in every replicate. Table S2 gives the
results at \(t_{1/2} = 1\) week.

\textbf{The residual correlation carries the whole size correction.}
Adding \texttt{corCAR1} to E0n lowers Type I error from
0.147 (MCSE 0.008)
to 0.049
(0.005) under CO, from
0.091 (0.006)
to 0.029
(0.004) under OL+BDC, and from
0.070
(0.006) to
0.035
(0.004) under Hybrid. Pooled over
the six null cells the paired change is
-0.065 (paired SE
0.009). Adding \texttt{corCAR1} to the A1
formula (E1nc to A1) has the same effect,
-0.065
(0.009). Without the residual
correlation the standard error is too small. The calibration ratio
\(\kappa\) of E0n is 0.74 to
0.94 at the null cells. With
\texttt{corCAR1} it is 0.98 to
1.13. The overcorrection below
nominal under Hybrid and OL+BDC is the conservatism that rung 2
removes.

\textbf{The software and the degrees-of-freedom method have no effect.} A0 and
E0n give \(p\)-values that differ by less than
0.0001 in every
replicate. Their rejection
rates agree in all 30 cells.

\textbf{The expectancy term and the exclusion rule have almost no effect.}
The exclusion rule of A0 drops no patient in any replicate of this
grid. The reason is that the drug indicator varies within patients in
every design.
Under CO the expectancy term is absent from A0, so E0n and E1nc are
the same model. Under Hybrid and OL+BDC, removing the expectancy term
changes Type I error by -0.003 to
+0.000 at the null cells (paired SE at most
0.002). It changes power by
-0.010 to +0.006 at the
alternative cells (paired SE at most 0.004).
A0 is therefore liberal because it has no residual correlation
structure.

\textbf{The published analysis itself is liberal for the same reason.} A0
is an adaptation (Section \ref{sec-e0}). A further run fits the
analysis exactly as published to the stored trials of the 30-cell
grid. It keeps the baseline row and has no expectancy term (E0p). It
then changes one element at a time. E0pn refits E0p with \texttt{nlme},
E0pnc adds \texttt{corCAR1}, and dropping the baseline row from E0pnc gives
A1. The run reproduces the stored A0 fit in all but 9 of
the 15,000 replicates, which are
excluded. A further 2 replicates are excluded because one
fit did not converge. At the six null cells the published analysis
rejects at 0.097. It rejects at 0.145 under
CO, 0.076 under OL+BDC and 0.071
under Hybrid. Refitting it with \texttt{nlme} changes no decision. Adding
\texttt{corCAR1} lowers the pooled rate by
0.053 (paired SE
0.005) to 0.045. As for
A0, the residual correlation removes the excess.

\textbf{The baseline row carries much of the power.} At the six reference
alternatives the published analysis with \texttt{corCAR1} (E0pnc) has power
0.481, against 0.332 for
A1. The two differ only in the baseline row. Dropping it lowers power
by 0.129 to 0.173 across the three
designs and changes size by -0.007 (paired SE
0.003). The baseline row adds an
off-drug observation of every patient, which presumably sharpens the
contrast between on-drug and off-drug occasions. No rung of the ladder uses it,
because the analyzed data of this study start after baseline. The
comparisons between rungs are unaffected, since all of them omit it.
The absolute power of every rung is presumably lower than it would be
with the baseline row, but we did not fit A2 to A4 with one.

\textbf{The residual correlation also carries the change in power.} Pooled
over the six reference alternatives, adding \texttt{corCAR1} to E0n changes
power by -0.134 (paired SE
0.013), and adding it to E1nc by
-0.134 (0.013). At
\(t_{1/2} = 1\) week it lowers power in all 12 alternative
cells, by 0.069 to 0.176.
Part of this loss is the removal of the excess rejections of a liberal
test. There are 3 exceptions, all at
\(t_{1/2} = 0\) under Hybrid and OL+BDC. There \texttt{corCAR1} raises power by
0.019 to 0.034 (paired SE
0.011 to 0.015). The
gain exceeds two paired SE in
1 of these cells. The residual correlation makes the estimate more precise
in these designs. Under Hybrid at \(N = 35\) and \(t_{1/2} = 0\) the
empirical standard deviation of the estimate falls from
2.024 to
1.696. Under CO
the empirical standard deviation changes by at most
5
percent, and the reported standard error rises by
26
to
27
percent. Under CO the residual correlation therefore corrects only
the standard error.

\textbf{The residual correlation changes the estimate under carryover.}
At \(t_{1/2} = 1\) week and \(c_{bm} > 0\), adding \texttt{corCAR1} moves the
mean estimate toward zero. The shift is
11
to 17 percent under Hybrid
and OL+BDC, and 2 to
3 percent under CO. At \(t_{1/2} = 0\) the
change is at most
1
percent. The binary indicator does not represent carryover, so its
coefficient depends on how the fit weights the off-drug occasions. A
plausible mechanism is that generalized least squares under a serial
correlation gives less weight to an occasion that is strongly
correlated with its neighbors. The first off-drug occasions sit next to
on-drug ones, and they carry most of the residual effect. We did not
verify this mechanism.

Rung 1 therefore turns a liberal test into a conservative one. In the
base grid A1 rejects the null at 0.038 against
0.107 for A0. It also costs power. A1 has
0.332 against 0.457 for A0
(Figure \ref{fig:fig-ladder-power}). The comparison of power
is not like for like, because A0 rejects too often under the null.
After adjustment to the same size the two are within about five
points of each other in most cells, and within eight in all (Section
\ref{sec-which}). Rungs 2 to 4 recover power with a correctly sized
test.

\subsubsection{Rung 2: CR2 cluster-robust inference}\label{sec-rung2}

Rung 2 replaces the working-covariance standard error of A1 by a CR2
cluster-robust standard error with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom
(A1 to A2). The point estimates do not change. Across the null cells
of the factorial grid the working-covariance test rejects at about
0.041 on average (Section \ref{sec-rung3} gives the range). We asked whether CR2 corrects it without disturbing
the ranking of the analyses. Table S3 diagnoses
the conservatism at four Hybrid null cells with 2,000 replicates each.
That count is enough to resolve the calibration ratio \(\kappa\) of
Section \ref{sec-cr2-method} (two-MCSE band around 0.05:
\([0.040, 0.060]\)).

The working-covariance test is conservative for both regressors at
every cell. It is most conservative with the exposure-weighted
predictor (\(\kappa\) 1.05 to 1.06, Type I error
0.033 to 0.037). The CR2 standard error brings
\(\kappa\) to within 0.10 of one. It also brings Type I error inside
the band for A2, A3 and A4 at all four cells. A4 rejects at
0.048 to 0.053 with \(\kappa\)
0.94 to 0.98, against
0.98 to 0.99 for A3. A0 rejects at
0.067 to 0.069 at all four cells.

Table S1 also reports Type I error at the six null cells of the base
grid. A1 is at 0.02 to 0.05 throughout. A2, A3 and A4 are
at 0.04 to 0.06, inside the
two-MCSE band at every cell. Under parameter set 2, A2 lay above the
band under OL+BDC at \(N = 35\) (0.074), so we checked that design with
5,000 independent null datasets per cell. Under parameter set 1 that
check gave A2
0.050 (MCSE
0.003) at \(N = 35\) and
0.054 (MCSE
0.003) at \(N = 70\) in OL+BDC
(\texttt{analysis/scripts/cr2-olbdc-check/}, output in
\texttt{analysis/data/cr2-olbdc-check-published/}). CR2 therefore keeps its
size under OL+BDC at these sample sizes. The rate at \(N = 70\) is
1.2
MCSE from 0.05.
The median Satterthwaite degrees of freedom of the CR2 test were
12 at \(N = 35\) and
24 at \(N = 70\). So few
degrees of freedom widen the reference distribution. That is part of
the power cost of CR2 at small \(N\).

\subsubsection{Rung 4: choosing the half-life}\label{sec-rung4}

Rung 4 replaces the true half-life by the half-life that AIC selects
from the data, on the model without the interaction (Section
\ref{sec-g4}). We asked two questions. The first is whether the
selection recovers the power that the predictor obtains at the true
half-life. The second is what the selection step costs in size and in
standard-error calibration. A3 and A4 were fitted to the same trials
in every family, so every comparison with A3 below is paired.

\textbf{Power.} In the base grid A4 changes power against A3 by
-0.008 (paired SE
0.010). Over the families of Table
\ref{tab:tab-families} the change is
-0.011 to +0.002. Table S4 shows that the selection
does not recover the half-life. At a true value of 0.5 week the
smallest candidate is chosen in about
59 to 62
percent of replicates and the true value in about
22 to 27
percent. At 1 week the choice is spread over all four candidates. The
small power cost therefore reflects the insensitivity of the exposure
weight to the half-life over this range, not recovery of it.

\textbf{Size.} At the four 2,000-replicate Hybrid null cells of Table S3,
A4 rejects at 0.048 to 0.053 against
0.047 to 0.049 for A3. Pooled by family, the change
in size from A3 to A4 is -0.005 to +0.005. The
Hybrid run with independent seeds (family S) gives the sharpest
check. It fitted A3 and A4 to the same simulated trials. There were
four Hybrid null cells at 5,000 replicates each, with an independent
random-number stream for every cell (Table S5). There were also eight
alternative cells at 1,000 replicates each (Table S6). Under the null A4
differs from A3 by
-0.0006 to
+0.0036. The
difference is within one paired standard error in
three
cells. In the fourth it is 2.2 standard errors. There A3 rejects at 0.0458 and A4 at 0.0494, both inside the two-MCSE band.
Pooled over the four cells the difference is
+0.0008 (paired SE
0.0017). The calibration ratio of A4 at the
null cells is
0.93
to 0.98.
At the alternatives A4 is below A3 by
0.001 to
0.019 in power. Its
calibration ratio there is
0.91 to
0.98, against
0.95 to
1.03 for A3. The selection
step therefore costs no size. It does leave the standard error of A4
too small under the alternative, by up to
9
percent in this run (Section \ref{sec-calibration}).

Rung 4 therefore removes the need to know the half-life. A4 is the
leading analysis of the ladder among those that do not need the true
half-life. Its size is that of the oracle within Monte Carlo error.
Its power is within about one point of the oracle's, pooled by
family.

at

the side nearer zero. This was checked at one cell only, and it costs
about 200 refits of A4 per trial.

\subsection{Sample size for A4}\label{sec-samplesize}

Table \ref{tab:tab-sample-size} gives the total \(N\) at which A4 has 80
percent power. Figure \ref{fig:fig-sample-size} shows the power
curves behind it for parameter set 1. Halving the
effect roughly quadruples \(N\). Under parameter set 1 a
Hybrid trial with a 1-week half-life needs about
154 patients at
\(c_{bm} = 0.4\), 273 at
0.3 and 613 at 0.2.
Carryover raises the requirement in the two designs with a
discontinuation phase. At \(c_{bm} = 0.3\) the Hybrid design needs
138 patients without
carryover and 273 at a
1-week half-life, and OL+BDC
186 and
449. The crossover design
is almost insensitive to the half-life but needs about twice the
Hybrid sample without carryover. Under parameter set 2 the
required \(N\) is
0.47 to 0.53
times its value under parameter set 1, presumably because the response
components vary less.
We did not decompose the difference. The ordering of designs and
half-lives is the same.

The binary indicator with CR2 (A2) needs the same \(N\) as A4 within a
few percent. The exception is a 1-week half-life in the two designs
with a discontinuation phase. There it needs 15 to 18
percent more patients than A4 in the Hybrid design and 22
percent more in
OL+BDC, over both parameter sets. That is the gain from modeling
carryover expressed in patients. Table S14 gives the \(N\) for A2 in
every cell.

A4 was also simulated in the Hybrid design under parameter set 1
with the simulation code of the companion paper
(\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats_dgp_architectures2026}{2}). That run had its own seeds and
grid. The companion paper does not report it. We tested for a
difference between the two studies' power curves by likelihood ratio.
The test gives
\(p = 0.10\) without
carryover and
\(p = 0.91\) at a
1-week half-life. The two studies' \(N\) for 80 percent power differ by
at most
6
patients over \(c_{bm}\) from 0.25 to 0.40. That paper also gives a
closed-form paired-difference rule for the sample size. In the runs
with its code under parameter set 1, A4 had power
0.844 to 0.936 at the sample
size of that rule, in every cell where that sample size was at most
400 (Table S15). The
rule is therefore conservative for A4 under parameter set 1.
Serial correlation moves the required \(N\) more than the half-life
does. A 1-week half-life roughly doubles the Hybrid \(N\) (Table
\ref{tab:tab-sample-size}). The same code's runs cover half-lives of
0, 0.2 and 1 week (Table S13). In them the Hybrid \(N\) at
\(c_{bm} = 0.25\) is
0.35 to 0.40 times its
\(\rho = 0.7\) value at \(\rho = 0.9\). It is
1.13 to 1.40 times that
value at \(\rho = 0.5\).

A required \(N\) is useful only if the test keeps its size at that \(N\).
Every other size check in this paper is at \(N = 35\) or 70. The null
run of Section \ref{sec-grid} checks A4 and A2 at the larger
samples. Table S17 gives the results. Over the
30 null cells A4
rejects at 0.040 to 0.058, inside
the two-MCSE band at 1,000 replicates
(\([0.036, 0.064]\)) in every cell. A2 rejects
at 0.036 to 0.063. Under the
null the calibration ratio of A4 is
0.97 to
1.01. The checks reach
\(N = 600\) under parameter set 1, and 300 for CO and 600 for Hybrid
under parameter set 2.
OL+BDC at a 1-week half-life and \(c_{bm} = 0.2\) needs more. It was
checked at its own entries,
1,012
patients under parameter set 1 and
480
under parameter set 2. A few CO entries, and one Hybrid entry under
parameter set 1, lie just above the largest \(N\) checked, at most
614 under parameter set 1 and
316 under parameter set 2.
Size showed no trend with \(N\), so we expect it to hold there as well.

The standard error of A4 is still too small under the alternative in
these runs. Its mean calibration ratio over the alternative cells at a
1-week half-life is 0.93,
against 0.99 for A2. The shortfall
does not shrink as \(N\) grows (correlation with \(N\)
0.06). It does not bias Table
\ref{tab:tab-sample-size}. The table rests on the rejection rate of
the A4 test as performed, and that test has the right size. The
shortfall matters for the interval, as in Section
\ref{sec-calibration}.

The curve model is an approximation. Fitting a separate curve for each
\(c_{bm}\) changes \(N\) by a factor of
0.95 to 1.06.
Of the 36 curves, 6 fail a
goodness-of-fit test at the 5 percent level. That is about
3
times the expected rate. The curves are therefore a smooth summary,
not an exact model. Fits that failed were dropped from the rejection
rates. In the sample-size runs at most
3.6
percent of A4 fits in a cell failed. The highest rates were in the
three OL+BDC cells at \(N = 740\) under parameter set 1. The cause
is the optimizer. Every fit in this study uses the \texttt{optim} optimizer
of \texttt{nlme}, and on these trials its REML fit stops with a singular
matrix. The default \texttt{nlminb} optimizer fits the same trials. We
regenerated every trial with a failed fit from its stored seed and
refitted the failed analysis with \texttt{nlminb}. All
219 failed fits succeeded. With the refits included, the
rejection rate of an affected cell changes by
-0.003 to
+0.007, so dropping the failures does
not bias Table \ref{tab:tab-sample-size}. The
intervals of Table
\ref{tab:tab-sample-size} cover Monte Carlo error under the model
only, so the values are best read as accurate to about 6 percent.
They assume complete data, the exponential decay form, \(\rho = 0.7\)
and a predictive-only biomarker.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec-discussion}

\subsection{What each rung bought}\label{sec-bought}

The ladder was not planned in advance. Each rung answered the question
the previous one raised. The recommendation rests on the chain of
rungs and on the stress scenarios rather than on any single
comparison. Everything below is conditional on three things. These
are the correlation encoding of the companion study, the parameter
values (Table \ref{tab:tab-params}), and the Hybrid design. A second
parameter set changed the absolute power but not the ranking (Section
\ref{sec-sens-params}). The analysis model is close to the conditional mean of that
Gaussian DGP. Part of what the
rungs show is therefore that a correctly specified regressor
outperforms a misspecified one. The findings concern which choices cost
power or size under that DGP, not the DGP itself.

The direction of rung 3 depends on the encoding. Under parameter set
2 the factorial grid also ran the mean-moderation encoding. There the
biomarker shifts the
drug-response mean at on-drug visits only, so no moderation persists
after discontinuation. There the binary indicator is the
correctly specified regressor. At a one-week half-life exposure
weighting changed power by -6.6
points under Hybrid and -5.0
under OL+BDC, against +6.0 and
+5.5 under the correlation
encoding. Rung 3 therefore pays only when the moderation fades with
the drug effect. Which encoding holds for a given drug is an empirical
question that these trials have not yet answered.

Rung 1 showed that the starting analysis is liberal and that A1 is
conservative. A0 is liberal in every design, from
0.074 to 0.076 under Hybrid to 0.128 to 0.150 under
the crossover design. It
differs from A1 in three respects at once. The isolation analyses of
Section \ref{sec-rung1} separate them. The missing residual
correlation accounts for the whole inflation. The software, the
degrees-of-freedom method, the expectancy term and the exclusion of
patients change size by at most
0.003.
A1 costs power against A0 in the base grid, but the power of A0 is
the power of a test that rejects too often. At equal size the two are
within a few points, and A4 is ahead of A0 under Hybrid and OL+BDC but not
under CO (Table S16).

Rung 2 showed that the working-covariance test is conservative. It
rejects at about 0.041 on average under the null. The conservatism comes from a working
covariance that does not match the data. A CR2 cluster-robust standard
error with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom brings the pooled size of
every analysis to within about half a point of nominal. Single
500-replicate cells range more widely, from
0.030
to 0.066
(Table \ref{tab:tab-ladder-score}). It changes power by a mean
of +3.4 points under
Hybrid, +4.9 under
OL+BDC and -3.4 under the
crossover design.

Rung 3 showed that the choice of exposure representation is a question
of power, not validity. Only the representation that changes the
regressor carrying the interaction recovers any power. Even at the
true half-life, exposure weighting recovered only
20\% to 22\% of the loss at a one-week half-life under
Hybrid and about 12\% under OL+BDC. Most of the loss is
not recoverable by the analysis and has to be met by the design. The
exposure-weighted predictor was within Monte Carlo noise of the
binary indicator where it did not gain. The earlier finding that it
failed under a crossover design was a coding defect, and we withdraw
it. Under this DGP the exposure-weighted predictor at the true decay is
the correctly specified regressor. Rung 3 therefore measures the cost
of misspecifying the regressor, not a general superiority.

Rung 4 showed that the true half-life, which rung 3 gave the analyst, is
not needed. A4 chooses it by AIC on the model without the
interaction. That changes power against the oracle A3 by
-0.011 to +0.002 and size by
-0.005 to +0.005 across the six families. This
includes the crossover design, once the selection compares ML fits and
the predictor is coded correctly before first exposure. Rung 4 does not
show that the selection recovers the half-life. The selected value is
spread over the candidates (Table S4). The small power
cost means the exposure weight is insensitive to the half-life over
this range.

Selecting the half-life with the interaction in the selection model
instead raised size above the oracle's in every family (Section
\ref{sec-comparators}). That is why A4 leaves the term under test
out of the selection. The scenario grids confirmed the ordering across
sample size and effect size. We examined dropout of 10 to 30 percent
of patients, completely at random or dependent on baseline severity.
It changed neither the size of the CR2 analyses nor their ordering.
Only the heavier per-occasion convention collapsed power. That
convention removes up to 73 percent of the observations. A patient-specific
linear trend of 0.3 outcome units per week, unrelated to the biomarker,
left size and power unchanged.

Size was evaluated at \(N = 35\) and \(N = 70\) in the main study, and
for A2 and A4 up to \(N = 1{,}012\) in the sample-size study. The
working-covariance tests were
conservative at both, which points to a working covariance that does
not fit rather than to a small-sample effect. The small-sample
refinements of CR2 would matter less in larger trials, but the need for
a robust standard error would not.

\subsection{The recommended analysis for a Hybrid trial}\label{sec-recommend}

For a Hybrid aggregated N-of-1 trial with a biomarker that is
predictive only, simulated under the correlation encoding, we
recommend A4 for the test of the interaction. In symbols, the model is

\[\text{Sx}_{it} \sim bm_i + t + D_{bc,it}(\hat t^{a}_{1/2}) +
  bm_i{:}D_{bc,it}(\hat t^{a}_{1/2}),\]

with a patient random intercept and \texttt{corCAR1} residual correlation.
\(D_{bc,it}\) equals one on drug, \(2^{-t_{sd}/\hat t^{a}_{1/2}}\) off
drug after first exposure, and zero before first exposure.
\(\hat t^{a}_{1/2}\) is chosen by AIC over \(\{0.25, 0.5, 1, 2\}\) weeks
from ML fits of the model without the interaction,
\(\text{Sx}_{it} \sim bm_i + t + D_{bc,it}\). The model with the
interaction is then fitted by REML at that half-life. The interaction
is tested with a CR2 cluster-robust standard error and Satterthwaite
degrees of freedom. Two properties recommend it. It requires no
pharmacokinetic input from the analyst. Its size is within one paired
standard error of the oracle at the true half-life in
5 of the six families and
within two in all.
The selection leaves the term under test out of its model. That is
the likely reason, but the evidence is the paired comparison itself.

Two qualifications apply. The first concerns estimation. The selected
half-life is often not the true one (Table S4), so the A4 coefficient
does not in general estimate \(\theta\). Its standard error under the
alternative is too small. The shortfall is a few percent pooled over a
family and up to 21 percent in
a single cell.
Averaged over the cells of a family, its nominal 95 percent interval
covers at
0.936
to
0.959.
At the reference cell it covers at
0.906 (Section
\ref{sec-calibration}). We therefore recommend A4 for
the test only. An estimate and interval for the interaction need
either the true half-life or a selection-aware method. At the
reference cell a parametric bootstrap that repeats the selection gave
a percentile interval that covered at 0.952
(Section \ref{sec-calibration}). We have not checked it elsewhere,
so we do not yet recommend it as routine. The second qualification
concerns the biomarker. With a predictive-only biomarker, A2 to A4
hold nominal size without the design-expectancy terms in every family
examined. The terms matter when the biomarker may also be prognostic.
The companion paper (\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats_prognostic2026}{14}) examines that case.
A trial whose biomarker may be prognostic should follow that paper. It
pairs the selection rule of A4 with the expectancy terms. Where
the biomarker may predict the natural-history trend, that paper also
analyzes the baseline visit, which none of our analyses does.

The recommendation transfers to OL+BDC. The evidence is rungs 3 and 4
and the scenario cells, where A4 and A3 lead in the same near-tie. Under the
crossover design the analyses are within a few
points of one another. A4 has the lowest power of A1 to A4 there,
0.378 against 0.419 for
A1 on the base grid. At equal size A2 and A4 are within Monte Carlo
error of each other under CO (Table S16).
For a crossover trial we therefore recommend A2. It needs no
half-life selection, it is at least as powerful as A4, and its standard error
is calibrated. The recommendation is stated for the correlation
encoding.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{limitations}

The numbers come from the starting values of Hendrickson et al.
(parameter set 1, Table \ref{tab:tab-params}). These values are not
calibrated to data either. The whole study was repeated under a
second set with a much weaker placebo pathway (parameter set 2), and
the ranking of the analyses did not change (Section
\ref{sec-sens-params}). The mean-moderation comparison of Section
\ref{sec-bought} exists under parameter set 2 only. Each set has one
trajectory shape per component, one response SD vector,
\(\rho = 0.7\) except in the \(\rho\) grid, and \(c_1 = 0.2\). No rung of
the ladder includes the baseline visit as an outcome row. In the
published analysis that row adds about
15 points of
power at the reference alternatives (Section \ref{sec-rung1}), so
the absolute power of every rung probably understates what the same
analysis would achieve with it. The recommendation comes from one design.
Above \(N = 70\) size was checked only for A2 and A4. Those checks used
two or three values of \(N\) per design and parameter set, under
complete data. Absolute power values are therefore illustrative. The ranking,
conditional on design, is the
more portable result. The correlation encoding is itself an
unvalidated modeling assumption of this project. The analysis model
is close to its conditional mean. The study therefore cannot show
how the ranking would change under a DGP with a different mean
structure. Every biomarker simulated here is predictive only. A
biomarker that is also prognostic is examined in the companion paper
(\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats_prognostic2026}{14}). Selecting the half-life leaves the A4
standard error too small under the alternative, by up to
21 percent in a single cell. It
also leaves its interval below nominal coverage (Section
\ref{sec-calibration}). Family 5 has no coverage output. A
selection-aware bootstrap interval was evaluated at the reference
cell only, under complete data. Dropout was examined at 10 to 30
percent of patients, monotone, with the MAR mechanism tied to
baseline severity. Baseline severity is not among the analysis
variables, so the mechanism is MAR given the analyzed data only
insofar as early outcomes proxy for it. Informative dropout related
to the outcome or to treatment response was not simulated. The
dropout block has no alternative cell without dropout under the same
code. Under the heavier per-occasion convention power collapsed for
every specification. The recommendation is therefore for trials that
retain most of their observations. The scenario grids use one
alternative effect size per scenario. The patient-specific slope
variance was simulated at one value, in one Hybrid cell, and with A3
and A4 only. A4 was also fitted with a random slope on time. Cells
within a job share random-number streams.
Counts of cells inside a band are therefore not independent, and the
cross-job comparisons are unpaired. The calibration ratio \(\kappa\)
uses the mean rather than the root mean square of the standard
errors. This lowers it by 0.01 to 0.04 (Section
\ref{sec-cr2-method}). The effect-size curve of earlier drafts was
not rerun under the corrected code. The
sensitivity checks vary one factor at a time, so they can miss
interactions between factors.

\subsection{Related approaches not evaluated}\label{sec-related}

Several N-of-1 analysis approaches sit outside the family examined.
Each would require substantial independent development to enter the
comparison. Tang and Landes (\citeproc{ref-tang2020ttests}{23}) propose formula-based
\(t\)-tests that correct a single-subject paired-difference test for
an estimated serial correlation. They have no biomarker-moderation
term. Schmid and Yang (\citeproc{ref-schmid2022bayesian}{24}) describe a Bayesian
hierarchical alternative with partial pooling of individual-level
treatment, trend and autocorrelation parameters, and discuss
extensions for carryover. Daza
(\citeproc{ref-daza2018causal}{25}) frames N-of-1 inference as a counterfactual
time-varying-treatment problem. In that framing carryover is
confounding to be controlled rather than a predictor to be modeled.
Chen and Chen (\citeproc{ref-chen2014}{3}) found that a paired \(t\)-test matched or
exceeded mixed models in simulated N-of-1 series with and without
carryover. Araujo, Julious and Senn (\citeproc{ref-araujo2016}{26}) describe the
variance components that a set of N-of-1 trials identifies. These
two papers motivated a paired-difference analysis in earlier drafts.
It is not evaluated here. The closest prior work on an exponentially
decaying exposure term is that of Gärtner et al. (\citeproc{ref-gartner2023}{5}). They
fit a carryover-adjusted parametric model for aggregated N-of-1
trials, with exponential wash-in and wash-out. Their simulated
treatment effects depend on patient covariates, but the quantity they
test is the treatment effect. This study tests the biomarker
interaction itself, and that is where its contribution lies. Liao et
al. (\citeproc{ref-liao2023}{7}) take a different route to carryover. They estimate
its time course from the data with a Bayesian distributed-lag model
with autocorrelated errors, rather than fixing or selecting a
half-life. That approach is a natural alternative to A3 and A4, and
it would merit a comparison for the interaction. Wang and Schork
(\citeproc{ref-wang2019}{27}) give analytic power for crossover-based N-of-1 trials
with serial correlation, washout and carryover. Their results concern
the treatment effect, but they are the nearest analytic counterpart
to the sample-size study of Section \ref{sec-samplesize}. Blackston et
al. (\citeproc{ref-blackston2019}{4}) show inflated Type I error for aggregated N-of-1
analyses of the treatment effect that ignore moderate or strong
carryover. The interaction behaves differently. A1 ignores carryover
and is conservative, and the inflation of A0 comes from its missing
residual correlation (Section \ref{sec-rung1}). For the interaction,
ignoring carryover costs power rather than validity. Percha et al.
(\citeproc{ref-percha2019}{6}) give design recommendations from simulated
single-series N-of-1 trials with carryover and wash-in. Zucker et
al. (\citeproc{ref-zucker2010}{28}) set out the methodology for combining N-of-1
trials into population estimates. The case against a single lagged
term as a carryover adjustment in crossover trials is classical
(\citeproc{ref-senn2002}{29}--\citeproc{ref-jones2014crossover}{31}).

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec-conclusions}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Rung 0 to 1: do not use the starting analysis as is.} It is
  liberal in every design, from 0.074 to 0.076 at the null
  under Hybrid to 0.128 to 0.150 under the crossover design. It
  differs from A1
  in its
  residual correlation structure, an expectancy term and its software.
  A1 is conservative and costs power against A0 in the base grid.
\item
  \textbf{Rung 2: use a CR2 cluster-robust standard error.} The working-covariance
  test rejects at about 0.041 under the null. CR2 with Satterthwaite
  degrees of freedom brings the pooled size of every analysis to
  within about half a point of nominal. Single 500-replicate cells
  range more widely. CR2 changes power by a mean of
  +3.4 points under
  Hybrid and +4.9 under
  OL+BDC, and by -3.4 under
  the crossover design.
\item
  \textbf{Rung 3: represent residual exposure in the regressor.} An
  exposure-weighted predictor at the true half-life recovers part of
  the power that carryover removes under the Hybrid and OL+BDC
  designs. At a one-week half-life it recovers 20\% to 22\%
  and about 12\%. The rest has to be met by the design, for example
  with
  more participants. It is within Monte Carlo noise of the binary
  indicator where it does not gain.
\item
  \textbf{Rung 4: let the data choose the half-life, on the model without
  the interaction.} AIC selection over four candidates compares ML
  fits of the model without the interaction. The interaction model
  is then refitted at the selected half-life by REML. This removes
  the need for the analyst to supply a half-life. Paired against the
  oracle at the true half-life, it changes size by
  -0.005 to +0.005 and power by
  -0.011 to +0.002 across the six families. The
  selected value is spread over the candidates. The small power cost
  reflects the insensitivity of the predictor to the half-life, not
  recovery of it. Leaving the interaction out
  of the selection keeps the size of the oracle. Selecting with it in
  the model raised size in every family.
\item
  \textbf{Recommendation.} For a Hybrid aggregated N-of-1 trial with a
  biomarker known to be predictive only, simulated under the
  correlation encoding, use A4 to test the interaction. A4 is the
  exposure-weighted predictor at the
  half-life chosen by AIC on the model without the interaction, with
  CR2 inference. Its size is equivalent to the
  oracle's within \(\pm 0.01\) in the run with independent seeds and in
  2 of the other five families. Against A2, the binary indicator with
  CR2, A4 gains 0.4 points of power
  on average in the Hybrid cells, up to
  4.1 at long half-lives, and loses at
  most 1.5 point in the
  13 cells where it is behind. At equal size it is ahead of
  the starting analysis A0 in all four Hybrid cells, by
  2.5 points on average
  (SE 1.9), though
  no single difference exceeds two standard errors. Under the
  crossover design use A2 instead. The recommendation is
  for the test. The A4 estimate is biased when the selected half-life
  is wrong, and its interval covers below its nominal rate. It should
  therefore not be reported as an estimate with a 95 percent
  interval. We examined dropout of 10 to 30 percent of patients,
  completely at random or dependent on baseline severity. Under it A4
  keeps its size and, in all but one cell, its power advantage over
  the binary indicator. It should be accompanied by an effort to
  retain patients, because heavier missingness costs every analysis
  power.
\item
  \textbf{A biomarker of unknown role.} Whether a candidate biomarker is
  also prognostic is rarely known before a trial and is hard to test
  within it. A4 omits the design-expectancy terms, which protect the
  test when the biomarker predicts the placebo response. A biomarker
  that predicts the natural-history trend biases A4 as fitted here.
  The companion paper controls that bias only with the baseline
  visit added as an outcome row. The default for a biomarker of
  unknown role is therefore the analysis of the companion paper
  (\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats_prognostic2026}{14}). This paper supports the parts of that
  analysis it shares with A4: the exposure-weighted predictor, the
  choice of half-life on the model without the interaction, and the
  CR2 test.
\item
  \textbf{Sample size.} Halving the effect roughly quadruples the
  required \(N\). Under parameter set 1 a Hybrid trial with
  a 1-week half-life analyzed with A4 needs about
  154,
  273 and
  613 patients at
  \(c_{bm}\) = 0.4, 0.3 and 0.2, and the binary indicator about
  15 to 18 percent more (Table \ref{tab:tab-sample-size}).
  The requirement is roughly half as large under parameter set 2
  and depends strongly on the serial correlation. A4 held its size
  at every \(N\) checked, up to
  1,012
  under parameter set 1 and
  600
  under parameter set 2.
\item
  \textbf{Limits.} The recommendation is conditional on the correlation
  encoding, on two parameter sets that gave the same ranking (Table
  \ref{tab:tab-params}), on one design and on a biomarker that is predictive only. Size was
  evaluated at \(N = 35\) and \(N = 70\) for every analysis, and up to
  \(N = 1{,}012\) for A2 and A4. No rung uses the baseline visit as an
  outcome row. In the published analysis that row added about
  15 points of
  power. The analysis model is close to the
  conditional mean of the DGP. Part of what the rungs show is
  therefore that a correctly specified regressor outperforms a
  misspecified one.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Glossary}\label{glossary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Glossary}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Analysis codes (A0 to A4).} The five analyses of Table
  \ref{tab:tab-spec-summary}, numbered by rung. The companion papers
  and earlier drafts call them E0, G1, G6, G8 and G10. Stored data
  files label them \texttt{E0}, \texttt{E1}, \texttt{E1cr2}, \texttt{E2cr2} and \texttt{E7null\_cr2}.
\item
  \textbf{A4.} The recommended analysis: the exposure-weighted predictor at
  the analysis half-life chosen by AIC on the model without the
  interaction, refitted by REML with the interaction and tested with
  CR2 (G10 in the companion papers).
\item
  \textbf{Oracle benchmark (A3).} An analysis that uses the true
  data-generating half-life. It is not available to an analyst and is
  not a candidate analysis. It shows what the selection step costs.
\item
  \textbf{Slope variance.} A data-generating option added for the
  independent-seed runs (\texttt{sd\_slope}). It adds a patient-specific linear
  trend, unrelated to the biomarker, with the stated standard
  deviation per week.
\item
  \textbf{Isolation analyses (E0n, E0nc, E1nc).} Three analyses that change
  one difference between A0 and A1 at a time. E0n refits the formula of
  A0 with \texttt{nlme} and no residual correlation. E0nc adds \texttt{corCAR1} to
  E0n. E1nc uses the formula of A1 with \texttt{nlme} and no residual
  correlation. A1 is E1nc with \texttt{corCAR1}.
\item
  \textbf{Ladder.} The ordered set of analyses of Table
  \ref{tab:tab-spec-summary}, from the starting analysis (rung 0) to the
  analysis with an AIC-selected half-life and CR2 inference (rung 4).
\item
  \textbf{Rung.} One analysis of the ladder. Each rung changes one thing
  from the rung before, except the step from rung 0 to rung 1.
\item
  \textbf{Scenario family.} A set of simulation cells that share one
  departure from the base grid. The families are numbered 1 to 5,
  with family S for the Hybrid run with independent seeds (Table
  \ref{tab:tab-families}).
\item
  \textbf{Biomarker-response correlation (\(c_{bm}\)).} The on-drug
  correlation between the biomarker and the drug-response component.
  It is the interaction estimand of this study. Stored data and code
  refer to it as \texttt{c\_bm} or \texttt{coupling}.
\item
  \textbf{Carryover half-life (\(t_{1/2}\)).} The time after
  discontinuation at which the residual drug effect, and under the
  correlation encoding the biomarker-response correlation, has
  fallen to half its on-drug value. The analysis half-life
  \(t^{a}_{1/2}\) is the value the analyst assumes or selects.
\item
  \textbf{Common random numbers.} Fitting every specification to the same
  simulated dataset within a cell, so that differences between
  specifications are paired and have reduced Monte Carlo variance.
\item
  \textbf{CO (crossover).} The two-period blinded crossover design of
  Section \ref{sec-designs}.
\item
  \textbf{Cluster-robust standard error (CR2).} The bias-reduced
  cluster-robust sandwich standard error of Bell and McCaffrey, which
  estimates the variance of the interaction coefficient from
  between-patient variation. It remains valid when the working
  covariance is misspecified. It is paired with Satterthwaite degrees
  of freedom.
\item
  \textbf{Working-covariance standard error.} The standard error that
  \texttt{nlme::lme} reports, computed under the working covariance: a
  patient random intercept with \texttt{corCAR1} residual correlation. It is
  correct only if that covariance is the true one. A test that uses
  it is called a working-covariance test. Each cluster-robust analysis
  fits the same model and changes only the standard error, the
  reference distribution and the \(p\)-value.
\item
  \textbf{Correlation encoding of the interaction.} The data-generating
  convention in which the biomarker-treatment interaction is a
  correlation between the biomarker and the drug response that is
  present on drug and absent or decayed off drug. Code and data
  files label it \texttt{mvn} or covariance moderation.
\item
  \textbf{\(D_{bc}\).} The exposure-weighted predictor: one on drug,
  \(2^{-t_{sd}/t^{a}_{1/2}}\) off drug after first exposure, zero
  before first exposure.
\item
  \textbf{Expectancy (\(D_e\), \(\eta_t\)).} The participant's probability of
  being on drug by design: one during open-label periods and one
  half during blinded periods. It scales the placebo-response
  component.
\item
  \textbf{Hybrid design.} Open-label titration, blinded discontinuation,
  then a brief crossover (Section \ref{sec-designs}).
\item
  \textbf{\(\kappa\) (calibration ratio).} The mean reported standard error
  (working-covariance or cluster-robust, whichever the analysis uses)
  divided by the empirical standard deviation of the estimates across
  replicates. A value of one is correctly calibrated.
\item
  \textbf{MCSE.} Monte Carlo standard error, the standard error of a
  simulation estimate that arises from the finite number of
  replicates.
\item
  \textbf{Natural-history component (TV).} The latent component of the
  outcome that follows the untreated time course.
\item
  \textbf{OL+BDC.} Open-label titration followed by blinded discontinuation
  (Section \ref{sec-designs}).
\item
  \textbf{Placebo-response component (PB).} The latent component of the
  outcome that responds to expectancy rather than to the drug.
\item
  \textbf{Predictive-only biomarker.} A biomarker that changes the drug
  response and nothing else: it is uncorrelated with baseline
  severity, the natural-history component and the placebo-response
  component. Every cell of this study has such a biomarker.
\item
  \textbf{Recommended DGP} (\texttt{refined} in code and data labels). The
  data-generating process of the companion study, in which the
  biomarker-response correlation decays after discontinuation, the
  within-component correlation is separable AR(1), carryover decays
  from the last on-drug mean, and a non-positive-definite covariance
  matrix stops the simulation.
\item
  \textbf{Satterthwaite degrees of freedom.} An approximate degrees of
  freedom for the \(t\) reference distribution of a cluster-robust
  test, computed from the variance of the variance estimate.
\item
  \textbf{Separable AR(1) structure.} The same first-order autoregressive
  correlation for every component across occasions, with a constant
  between-component correlation at every lag.
\item
  \textbf{Starting analysis (A0).} The analysis published with the
  starting DGP: \texttt{lme4::lmer} with a random intercept, no residual
  correlation structure, a binary on-drug indicator, and Satterthwaite
  \(p\)-values. The companion paper calls it the binary-indicator
  analysis.
\item
  \textbf{Starting DGP.} The data-generating process of Hendrickson et al.
  (2020), the starting point of the companion study.
\item
  \textbf{Supplementary study.} A later simulation study that added the
  shape, half-life, serial-correlation and dropout cells of families 2
  to 4. Code and data files label it \texttt{gap-fill}.
\item
  \textbf{Two-MCSE band.} The interval of plus or minus two Monte Carlo
  standard errors around the nominal 0.05 rejection rate, which is
  \([0.031, 0.069]\) at 500 replicates, \([0.036, 0.064]\) at 1,000 and
  \([0.040, 0.060]\) at 2,000.
\end{itemize}
