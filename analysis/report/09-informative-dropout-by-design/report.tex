\section*{Abstract}\label{abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

\textbf{Background.} Participants leave aggregated N-of-1 trials, and
their reasons can depend on how their symptoms change. We asked how
dropout affects the test of a biomarker-by-treatment interaction in
three designs.

\textbf{Methods.} We simulated open-label titration with blinded
discontinuation (OL+BDC), a two-period crossover (CO) and the Hybrid
design at one parameter point, with \(N = 70\) in each. The biomarker
shifted the drug-response mean at on-drug visits, with true
interaction \(-2.25\). Seven dropout conditions were applied to the same
1,000 complete datasets per design.
They were no dropout, completely random dropout and magnitude-driven
dropout at 25 and 40 percent, and three directional mechanisms at 25
percent. We fitted the mixed
model at analysis half-lives of 0.5 week and 0, with model-based and
cluster-robust (CR2) tests. A null regime had
500 datasets per design.

\textbf{Results.} The 0.5-week analysis half-life biased the
interaction estimate by -0.24 in OL+BDC
and -0.18 in the Hybrid design (MCSE
0.02). A binary exposure indicator,
which matches the generating interaction, removed that bias. Without
dropout the empirical standard deviation of the estimate was
0.548 (Hybrid), 0.560 (OL+BDC) and 0.747 (CO).
Dropout at 25 percent raised the standard error by a factor of
1.081 to 1.091 under completely random dropout.
At 40 percent magnitude-driven dropout the factor was 1.132
to 1.163, nearly the same in all three designs. CO lost
more absolute power, mostly because it started lower on its power
curve. Improvement-driven dropout attenuated the estimate by
0.039 to 0.149 points
(paired MCSE at most 0.012), most in CO.
Dropout did not change test size. The model-based test rejected in
0.014 to
0.024 of null Hybrid datasets. The
CR2 test rejected in 0.076 to
0.082 of null OL+BDC datasets.

\textbf{Conclusions.} At this parameter point the Hybrid and OL+BDC
designs carried the same information and lost it to dropout at the
same rate. CO needed about 1.9 times the
sample for the same precision. The analysis half-life must match the
generating interaction. The direction of dropout mattered for bias,
not for precision.

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

\subsection{The question}\label{the-question}

Aggregated N-of-1 trials pool many single-participant series of on-drug
and off-drug periods into one mixed model\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-lillie2011}{1}}. A predictive-biomarker trial
of this kind tests whether a baseline biomarker modifies the drug
effect. The target is the biomarker-by-treatment interaction
\(\beta_{bm:D}\). Participants leave such trials, and their reasons can
depend on how their symptoms are changing. We ask whether three
candidate designs differ in how much power to detect \(\beta_{bm:D}\)
they lose to dropout, and whether the answer depends on the dropout
mechanism or only on its rate.

The three designs are open-label titration followed by blinded
discontinuation (OL+BDC), the traditional two-period crossover (CO),
and the Hybrid design of\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{2}}. The Hybrid design is
open-label titration, then blinded discontinuation, then a brief
crossover. A fourth design, open label throughout (OL), has no off-drug
visits. It cannot separate the drug response from the placebo response
and the natural history, so it carries no information on
\(\beta_{bm:D}\). We exclude it from the comparison for that reason
(Section \ref{sec-designs}).

\subsection{Prior work}\label{prior-work}

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{2}} introduced the Hybrid design and compared it with
OL, OL+BDC and CO by simulation. Their censoring filter assigned each
simulated participant a per-unit-time dropout probability equal to a
flat hazard plus a term in the square of the symptom change since
baseline, shifted by 100. They describe this as a probability that is
higher for participants who worsen or remain symptomatic and lower for
participants who benefit (Methods). They report that increased
censoring lowered power in every design, with a faster decline for
OL+BDC because of participant loss before the blinded phase (Results,
Figure 4A). In their comparison the Hybrid design had higher power
than OL and OL+BDC and slightly lower power than CO without carryover.
Its power fell faster than that of CO once carryover was introduced.
Type I error rose with censoring, most for OL+BDC (Figure 6). Under the
null, the interaction estimate was biased toward negative values for
most censoring patterns in OL+BDC and for several in CO (Figure 7A).
Against an uncensored reference, censoring changed the estimate only
in the OL design (Figure 7B). Their `happy accident' refers to trial
cost. Participants with no response, or with a response clearly lost
on placebo, tend to leave before the later crossover blocks, so those
blocks are spent on participants whose earlier phases were
inconclusive (Discussion). It is not a statement about power.

The present study does not reproduce those results. It uses different
correlation parameters, carryover, analysis model and dropout hazards
(Section \ref{sec-hendrickson-diff}).

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-matthews2013crossover}{3}} studied two-period crossover designs under
non-ignorable dropout. They found the conventional AB/BA design robust
under a missing-at-random analysis, and they identified a sequence set
that is preferable when dropout depends on the within-participant
difference between periods.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-blackston2019}{4}} compared aggregated N-of-1
designs with parallel and crossover trials by simulation, including
selection bias. N-of-1 reporting guidance asks that dropout be reported\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-vohraCONSORTExtensionReporting2015}{5}}, and reviews of published N-of-1
studies assess how completely trials report their methods\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-stunnenberg2022}{6},\citeproc{ref-natesanbatley2023}{7}}. We did not find a
simulation that compares OL+BDC, CO and the Hybrid design on paired
data under several dropout mechanisms.

The missing-data background is standard.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-rubin1976}{8}} defined data
missing completely at random (MCAR), missing at random (MAR) and
missing not at random (MNAR)\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-littlerubin2019}{9}}. Selection models\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-diggle1994}{10}}, pattern-mixture models\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-little1993pattern}{11},\citeproc{ref-hedeker1997pattern}{12}} and shared-parameter models\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-wu1988informative}{13},\citeproc{ref-follmann1995shared}{14}} describe dropout that
depends on unobserved values. Likelihood-based mixed models are the
usual primary analysis when dropout is plausibly MAR\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-mallinckrodt2001dropout}{15}--\citeproc{ref-verbekemolenberghs2000}{19}}. Sensitivity
analyses vary the departure from MAR through delta-adjusted or
reference-based multiple imputation\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-carpenter2013reference}{20}--\citeproc{ref-okellyratitch2014}{24}} and tipping-point displays\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-permutt2016sensitivity}{25}}. The ICH E9(R1) addendum treats dropout as
an intercurrent event to be handled through the estimand\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ich_e9_r1_2020}{26},\citeproc{ref-leuchs2015estimands}{27}}. This paper uses only the
first of these tools, the likelihood-based mixed model. Joint models
for longitudinal and event-time data\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-wulfsohn1997}{28},\citeproc{ref-henderson2000}{29}} and run-in selection\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-berger2003runin}{30}} are related
but are not used here.

\subsection{What this paper contributes}\label{what-this-paper-contributes}

We make three contributions. First, we define the simulated dropout
mechanisms exactly and state their missing-data properties
(Section \ref{sec-model}). Second, we report a paired simulation in
which each dropout condition is applied to the same complete datasets.
Power losses, coefficient shifts and standard-error ratios are then
paired differences with small Monte Carlo standard errors (MCSE).
Third, we show that the analysis half-life must match the generating
interaction. The historical analysis decays the exposure indicator at
0.5 week, while the generating interaction has no carryover. This
mismatch biases the estimate for OL+BDC and the Hybrid design and
changes the design ordering (Section \ref{sec-bias}).

The study covers one parameter point. It is exploratory. No analysis
plan was registered in advance, and its findings should be confirmed
in an independent study. Three components of the working protocol were
not carried out: the sample-size, effect-size and carryover sweeps; a
path-stratified decomposition of the dropout pattern in the Hybrid
design; and analysis-stage sensitivity analyses under alternative
missing-data assumptions. Section \ref{sec-limits} lists what each
would add.

\section{Dropout mechanisms and their missing-data properties}\label{sec-model}

\subsection{The simulated hazards}\label{the-simulated-hazards}

All dropout is generated by the function \texttt{first\_dropout()} in the
study driver
(\texttt{analysis/scripts/informative-dropout-by-design/20-paired-dropout.R}).
The package function \texttt{censordata()} is not used
(Section \ref{sec-censordata}).

Let \(Y_{it}\) be the symptom score of participant \(i\) at post-baseline
visit \(t\) and \(\mathrm{BL}_i\) the baseline score. Higher scores are
worse. The change from baseline is \(\Delta_{it} = Y_{it} -
\mathrm{BL}_i\), so a negative change is an improvement. The step
change is \(r_{it} = Y_{it} - Y_{i,t-1}\), with \(Y_{i0} =
\mathrm{BL}_i\). Within each randomization path and visit, let
\(\bar\Delta_t\) and \(s_t\) be the mean and standard deviation of
\(\Delta_{it}\) across the participants of that path, and define
\(\bar r_t\) and \(s^r_t\) in the same way. The raw hazard \(h_{it}\) of
each condition is:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  MCAR: \(h_{it} = 1\).
\item
  Magnitude of change: \(h_{it} = \min\{1, \beta_0 + \beta_1
  (\Delta_{it}/s_t)^2\}\) with \(\beta_0 = 0.05\) and \(\beta_1 = 0.5\).
  The change is scaled but not centered.
\item
  Worsening-driven: \(h_{it} = \exp\{\gamma(\Delta_{it} -
  \bar\Delta_t)/s_t\}\).
\item
  Improvement-driven: \(h_{it} = \exp\{-\gamma(\Delta_{it} -
  \bar\Delta_t)/s_t\}\).
\item
  Symptom return: \(h_{it} = \exp\{\gamma(r_{it} - \bar
  r_t)/s^r_t\}\).
\end{itemize}

We set \(\gamma = \ln 3\). Under the three directional hazards the raw
hazard triples for each standard deviation of the driving quantity.

The hazard applied at visit \(t\) is \(\min(1, \alpha h_{it})\). The
participant drops out at the first visit \(t\) with \(U_{it} <
\min(1, \alpha h_{it})\), where the \(U_{it}\) are independent uniform
draws. That visit and all later visits are deleted. Baseline is always
observed. The multiplier \(\alpha\) is found by root finding on each
realized path dataset. It solves
\[\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \Bigl[1 - \prod_t \{1 - \min(1, \alpha
h_{it})\}\Bigr] = d,\]
where \(n\) is the number of participants on the path and \(d\) is the
target fraction of participants who ever drop out (0.25 or 0.40). The
achieved fraction therefore matches \(d\) in expectation by construction.
It is not an outcome of the study.

The hazard is per visit, not per unit of time. Visits are 2.5 weeks
apart throughout CO, 4 weeks apart in the open-label phases of OL+BDC
and the Hybrid design, and 1 week apart in their blinded phases. A
common target \(d\) therefore places dropout at different calendar times
and phases in different designs. Section \ref{sec-dropout-achieved}
reports where dropout fell.

The `biased' condition of the earlier unpaired prototype is the
magnitude-of-change hazard. It rises with \(|\Delta_{it}|\) in either
direction. The mean change is an improvement at every post-baseline
visit, so a large \(|\Delta_{it}|\) is more often a large improvement
than a large worsening. That condition is not worsening-driven, and we
no longer label it so. The worsening-driven, improvement-driven and
symptom-return hazards were added to separate the direction of
selection from its magnitude.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Relation to \texttt{censordata()} and to the hazard of Hendrickson et al.~}{Relation to censordata() and to the hazard of Hendrickson et al.~}}\label{sec-censordata}

The package function \texttt{censordata()} combines a flat Bernoulli draw and
a symptom-dependent Bernoulli draw. Its symptom-dependent term is
proportional to \((D + 100)^{e_{b1}}\), where \(D\) is the generated
column \texttt{D\_\textless{}visit\textgreater{}}. That column is the sum of the response components,
which equals \(\mathrm{BL}_i - Y_{it}\), the improvement. The term
therefore increases with improvement.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{2}} describe their
hazard as higher for participants who worsen. Their verbal description
and the code disagree in sign. We did not check the sign against the
original repository commit. The flat term of \texttt{censordata()} is
proportional to the block length \texttt{t\_wk}, not to elapsed time. Because
the present study does not call \texttt{censordata()}, neither point affects
its results.

\subsection{Missing-data classification}\label{sec-mar}

Under the MCAR condition dropout is independent of all outcomes. It is
missing completely at random\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-rubin1976}{8}}.

Under the four outcome-dependent conditions, dropout at visit \(t\)
depends on \(Y_{it}\), and \(Y_{it}\) is then deleted. Dropout therefore
depends on an unobserved value, and the data are MNAR. This is the
selection-model form of\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-diggle1994}{10}}. Two features weaken the practical
consequence. First, the serial correlation of the generating process
is \(0.7^{|w_t - w_s|}\) within each response component, where \(w_t\) is
the calendar week. \(Y_{it}\) is therefore partly predicted by
\(Y_{i,t-1}\), which is observed. We expect the mechanism to behave
nearly as MAR, but we did not quantify this. Second, the
magnitude-of-change hazard is symmetric in the direction of change. It
removes strongly improving and strongly worsening participants alike.

MAR alone does not make the likelihood-based analysis unbiased. Valid
likelihood inference under MAR also requires a correctly specified
model for the full data\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-littlerubin2019}{9},\citeproc{ref-molenberghskenward2007}{18}}.
The fitted mean is linear in calendar time with a random intercept and
a continuous-time first-order autoregressive (CAR(1)) residual. The
generating mean is a sum of Gompertz curves with three
autocorrelated components. MAR dropout can therefore bias
\(\hat\beta_{bm:D}\) here, and any bias we observe cannot be attributed
to MNAR alone.

We did not simulate shared-parameter dropout\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-wu1988informative}{13},\citeproc{ref-follmann1995shared}{14}}. In that mechanism the hazard
depends on a participant's latent drug response, which is the quantity
the biomarker predicts. It is the mechanism most likely to bias
\(\beta_{bm:D}\) and the one that the clinical motivation most directly
suggests. Our symptom-return hazard depends on the observed
off-drug rise in symptoms. That rise is larger for participants with a
larger drug response, so the hazard is related to the latent response
only through the observed outcome.

\subsection{Estimand}\label{estimand}

We treat dropout as an intercurrent event\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ich_e9_r1_2020}{26}}. The
target is the hypothetical-strategy estimand: \(\beta_{bm:D}\) as it
would be had every participant completed the schedule. Its true value
is fixed by the generating process (Section \ref{sec-sim}).

\section{The designs and where dropout falls}\label{sec-designs}

Each design is a set of randomization paths. A path is a sequence of
visits, each on drug or off drug. The interaction is identified by
within-participant contrasts between on-drug and off-drug visits. A
dropout removes the remaining visits of a path, and with them any
contrast they carry.

OL+BDC has eight visits at weeks 4, 8, 12, 16, 17, 18, 19 and 20.
Both of its two paths are on drug through week 16. One path switches
to placebo after week 18 and the other after week 17. The off-drug
visits are therefore the last two or three, and a dropout before them
removes the only off-drug information for that participant.

CO has eight visits 2.5 weeks apart. One path is on drug for the
first four visits and on placebo for the last four. The other path
is the reverse. Each participant contributes four visits on each side
of the switch, and a dropout in the second period removes part of one
side.

The Hybrid design has visits at weeks 4, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 16 and 20.
All four paths are on drug through week 9. Two paths switch to placebo
after week 9 and two after week 10. The last two visits, at weeks 16
and 20, are a drug-placebo crossover in either order. Off-drug visits
occur in the blinded phase and in the crossover.

The OL design is on drug at every visit. The package analysis
\texttt{lme\_analysis()} detects that no participant varies in exposure after
baseline and then fits \texttt{Sx\ \textasciitilde{}\ bm\ +\ t\ +\ bm:t}. The coefficient it tests
is the biomarker-by-time slope, not \(\beta_{bm:D}\). The earlier
prototype reported that slope test in its OL row. Its rejection rate,
0.086 to
0.112, was the power of a different
test. It was neither a test size nor a measure of information on the
interaction. A model with \texttt{bm:Dbc} and the baseline row would be
estimable for OL, but its interaction is not separable from the
natural-history and placebo components. We therefore exclude OL.

These descriptions say where each design's off-drug information lies.
They are not tested hypotheses about which design loses most. The
results in Section \ref{sec-results} address that question directly.

\section{Simulation design}\label{sec-sim}

We report the study in the ADEMP structure of\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morris2019simulation}{31}}.

\textbf{Aims.} To compare OL+BDC, CO and the Hybrid design on the power to
detect \(\beta_{bm:D}\), its bias and the calibration of its test,
without dropout and under six dropout conditions, at one parameter
point, with paired comparisons across conditions.

\textbf{Data-generating mechanism.} Data were generated by the package
function \texttt{generateData()} under the mean-shift encoding
(\texttt{dgp\_architecture\ =\ \textquotesingle{}mean\_moderation\textquotesingle{}},
\texttt{moderation\_scaling\ =\ \textquotesingle{}constant\textquotesingle{}}). The biomarker \(B_i\) has mean 0
and standard deviation 1, so it coincides with its standardized value
\(b_i\). At every on-drug visit, the drug-response component is shifted
by \(\beta_{bm} b_i \sigma_{BR}\) with \(\beta_{bm} = 0.45\) and
\(\sigma_{BR} = 5\). Off-drug visits receive no shift, so the shift has
no carryover. The mean drug response itself carries over after
discontinuation with half-life \(t_{1/2} = 0.5\) week, decaying from the
last on-drug mean. The three response components (drug response,
placebo response, natural history) follow modified Gompertz means with
the reference parameters of the driver. Each component has first-order
autoregressive correlation \(\rho = 0.7\) per week. Components are
correlated at 0.1 at the same visit and 0.05 across visits. Baseline
severity has mean 70 and standard deviation 10. Every covariance matrix
was positive definite, and the driver refuses any matrix that is not.

The components reduce symptoms, so a larger drug response lowers
\(Y_{it}\). The interaction is therefore negative. Its true value is
\(\beta_{bm:D} = -\beta_{bm}\sigma_{BR}/\sigma_{bm} = -2.25\) points per
biomarker unit, under binary drug exposure. Under the null regime
\(\beta_{bm} = 0\) and the true value is 0.

Every design has a total of \(N = 70\) participants, allocated across
its paths: 35 on each path of OL+BDC and CO, and 18, 18, 17 and 17 on
the four Hybrid paths.

\textbf{Pairing.} For each design and replicate, one complete dataset and
one matrix of uniform draws \(U_{it}\) were generated. Each of the seven
conditions was applied to that dataset with those draws. The
conditions therefore differ only in the hazard. The null regime used
its own seeds and was paired across conditions in the same way.
Datasets were not shared across designs, so between-design
comparisons are unpaired.

\textbf{Estimand.} \(\beta_{bm:D}\) under the hypothetical strategy, with
true value \(-2.25\) (alternative) or 0 (null).

\textbf{Methods.} Each post-dropout dataset was analyzed with
\[Y_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_B B_i + \beta_T w_t + \beta_D D_{bc,it} +
\beta_{bm:D} B_i D_{bc,it} + \beta_\eta \eta_t + u_i +
\varepsilon_{it},\]
with a participant random intercept \(u_i\) and CAR(1) residual
correlation in calendar weeks, fitted by restricted maximum likelihood
with \texttt{nlme::lme}. The design expectancy \(\eta_t\) enters for OL+BDC and
the Hybrid design. CO has a constant post-baseline expectancy, so the
term is omitted there, as in \texttt{lme\_analysis()}. The exposure indicator
\(D_{bc,it}\) is 1 on drug, 0 before the first exposure, and
\(2^{-t_{sd}/t^a_{1/2}}\) off drug after exposure, where \(t_{sd}\) is the
time since discontinuation. We used two analysis half-lives:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(t^a_{1/2} = 0.5\) week (analysis \texttt{ta0.5}): the historical
  specification, equal to the half-life of the mean drug response. It
  reproduces \texttt{lme\_analysis()} exactly. We checked this on one dataset
  per design and obtained identical coefficients, standard errors and
  \(p\)-values.
\item
  \(t^a_{1/2} = 0\) (analysis \texttt{ta0}): binary drug exposure. This matches
  the generating interaction, which has no carryover.
\end{itemize}

Each fit was tested two ways. The model-based test uses the \texttt{nlme}
standard error and degrees of freedom. The CR2 test uses the
bias-reduced cluster-robust variance estimator\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-bell2002cr2}{32}} with
Satterthwaite degrees of freedom (\texttt{clubSandwich::coef\_test}), as in
the project's carryover-sensitivity driver. If the CAR(1) fit failed,
the driver refitted without the residual correlation and recorded the
fallback.

\textbf{Performance measures.} For each design, condition, analysis and
test we report the rejection rate at \(\alpha = 0.05\) (power under the
alternative, size under the null), bias against the true value, the
empirical standard deviation of \(\hat\beta_{bm:D}\), the calibration
ratio \(\kappa\) and the coverage of the 95 percent confidence interval.
\(\kappa\) is the mean standard error divided by the empirical standard
deviation of the estimates, and \(\kappa > 1\) indicates a conservative
test. Against the no-dropout condition on the same datasets we report
the paired power loss, the paired shift in \(\hat\beta_{bm:D}\) and the
mean ratio of standard errors. Each carries its paired MCSE, the
standard deviation of the per-replicate difference divided by the
square root of the number of replicates. The MCSE of a rejection rate
is \(\sqrt{\hat\pi(1-\hat\pi)/n_{\text{reps}}}\).

\textbf{Replicates and seeds.} The alternative regime has
1,000
replicates per design and the null regime
500. The seed of each
design and replicate is \texttt{set.seed(20261029\ +\ 100000\ *\ d\ +\ r)}, where
\(d\) is the design index (1 OL+BDC, 2 CO, 3 Hybrid) and \(r\) the
replicate. The null regime adds 50,000. Replicates ran in parallel with
\texttt{parallel::mclapply} on 8 workers, with single-threaded linear
algebra.

\subsection{Comparison with the earlier unpaired prototype}\label{sec-old}

An earlier version of this study used independent datasets for each
cell, 500 replicates per cell, the historical analysis and the
magnitude-of-change hazard under the label `biased'. It reported power
losses at 40 percent of
0.086
(CO),
0.058
(Hybrid) and
0.056
(OL+BDC), with MCSE near 0.02. The paired rerun with the same analysis
gave
0.111 (0.011),

and

The earlier run also reported an OL row. That row tested the
biomarker-by-time slope (Section \ref{sec-designs}), not the

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

\subsection{What the paired design changed}\label{what-the-paired-design-changed}

Applying every dropout condition to the same datasets turned each loss
and shift into a paired difference. This made the CO loss separable
from the others and showed why. On the standard-error scale the three
designs lost the same precision to dropout of a given rate. CO lost
more absolute power because it started lower. The earlier unpaired
prototype had read its 3-point difference in losses as evidence that
CO is the least robust design. With a standard error of about 3
points, that difference was not resolved.

\subsection{Why the results differ from Hendrickson et al.~}\label{sec-hendrickson-diff}

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{2}} found the Hybrid design slightly less powerful than CO
without carryover. Here CO was the least efficient design. The two
studies differ in several ways that could explain this. The original
DGP uses compound symmetry with a common within-component correlation
of 0.8. We used first-order autoregressive correlation of 0.7 per
week. The original DGP encodes the interaction as a correlation
between the biomarker and the drug response. We used the mean-shift
encoding. The present carryover half-life is 0.5 week. Hendrickson et
al.~report that even 0.1 week of carryover reduced the power of the
Hybrid design sharply. Their dropout hazard is per unit time and
built on the shifted squared change, and ours is per visit. Which of
these accounts for the difference was not tested.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec-limits}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{One parameter point.} Sample size, effect size, carryover
  half-life and correlation parameters were fixed. The protocol's
  sweeps over \(N \in \{35, 70, 100\}\) and \(\beta_{bm} \in \{0, 0.3,
  0.6\}\) were not run.
\item
  \textbf{One encoding.} The interaction was generated by the mean-shift
  encoding only. Under the correlation encoding the interaction has a
  different exposure profile, and the results may differ.
\item
  \textbf{No shared-parameter dropout.} The hazards depend on observed
  outcomes. A hazard tied to the latent drug response would be more
  directly informative about the biomarker.
\item
  \textbf{No analysis-stage sensitivity.} Every condition was analyzed with
  the likelihood-based mixed model under an implicit MAR assumption.
  Pattern-mixture, delta-adjusted and reference-based analyses\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-little1993pattern}{11},\citeproc{ref-hedeker1997pattern}{12},\citeproc{ref-carpenter2013reference}{20}--\citeproc{ref-ratitch2013pattern}{22}} and tipping-point displays\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-permutt2016sensitivity}{25}} were not applied.
\item
  \textbf{No path decomposition.} The dropout calibration fixes each path's
  dropout fraction, so the Hybrid paths cannot be compared on
  dropout share. A path-stratified analysis would need a calibration
  across the whole design.
\item
  \textbf{Per-visit hazard.} Visit spacing differs by design, so equal
  targets do not mean equal hazards per week.
\item
  \textbf{Calibrated rates.} The ever-dropout fraction is set by
  construction. The study compares mechanisms at equal rates. It does
  not predict how often participants will leave a given design.
\item
  \textbf{Test calibration.} The model-based test was conservative in the
  Hybrid design and the CR2 test liberal in OL+BDC. Power comparisons
  across designs carry this difference.
\item
  \textbf{Platform.} The run was made on the host R session, not in the
  project container. Results are not draw-for-draw reproducible across
  the two.
\item
  \textbf{Symptom-return mechanism.} Its hazard uses the step change
  between consecutive visits, which differ in spacing. It was only
  weakly related to mean improvement and is one of several possible
  operationalizations.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec-conclusions}

At one parameter point with \(N = 70\), the Hybrid and OL+BDC designs
estimated the biomarker-by-treatment interaction with the same
precision and lost the same share of it to dropout. CO needed about
1.9 times the sample for the same precision.
Its larger absolute power loss under dropout followed mainly from its
lower starting power.

The rate of dropout set the loss of precision. The direction of
dropout set the bias. Improvement-driven dropout attenuated the
estimate by up to
7
percent of its true value. Worsening-driven, magnitude-driven and
MCAR dropout changed it little. Dropout did not change test size.

An analysis half-life of 0.5 week biased the OL+BDC and Hybrid
estimates by about
11 and
8 percent,
because the generating interaction had no carryover. The analysis must
match the exposure profile of the interaction, not only that of the
mean drug response.

\section*{Glossary}\label{glossary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Glossary}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Analysis \texttt{ta0.5}.} The historical analysis: the exposure
  indicator decays after discontinuation with analysis half-life
  \(t^a_{1/2} = 0.5\) week. It reproduces the package function
  \texttt{lme\_analysis()}.
\item
  \textbf{Analysis \texttt{ta0}.} The same model with \(t^a_{1/2} = 0\), so the
  exposure indicator is binary. It matches the generating interaction.
\item
  \textbf{Calibration ratio \(\kappa\).} The mean standard error divided by
  the empirical standard deviation of the estimates. Values above 1
  indicate a conservative test.
\item
  \textbf{CR2.} The bias-reduced cluster-robust variance estimator of Bell
  and McCaffrey, clustered on participant, with Satterthwaite degrees
  of freedom.
\item
  \textbf{CO.} The traditional two-period crossover: four visits on one
  treatment, then four on the other, in randomized order.
\item
  \textbf{Dropout conditions.} \texttt{none}; \texttt{MCAR\_25} (constant hazard, 25
  percent ever drop out); \texttt{magnitude\_25} and \texttt{magnitude\_40} (hazard
  rising in the squared, uncentered, scaled change from baseline;
  stored in the earlier prototype as \texttt{biased\_25} and \texttt{biased\_40});
  \texttt{worsening\_25} and \texttt{improvement\_25} (hazard rising or falling in the
  centered, scaled change from baseline); \texttt{return\_25} (hazard rising in
  the centered, scaled change since the previous visit). Section
  \ref{sec-model} gives the formulas.
\item
  \textbf{Hybrid design.} Open-label titration, then blinded
  discontinuation, then a brief two-visit crossover, with four
  randomization paths\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{2}}.
\item
  \textbf{Mean-shift encoding.} The data-generating form in which the
  biomarker shifts the drug-response mean at on-drug visits by
  \(\beta_{bm} b_i \sigma_{BR}\) (package value
  \texttt{dgp\_architecture\ =\ \textquotesingle{}mean\_moderation\textquotesingle{}}).
\item
  \textbf{OL, OL+BDC.} Open label throughout; open-label titration followed
  by blinded discontinuation.
\item
  \textbf{Paired loss.} The mean over replicates of the rejection indicator
  without dropout minus the rejection indicator with dropout, computed
  on the same datasets.
\item
  \textbf{Analysis half-life \(t^a_{1/2}\).} The half-life the analysis uses
  to build the exposure indicator. It is distinct from the
  data-generating half-life \(t_{1/2}\).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Reproducibility}\label{reproducibility}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Reproducibility}

The paired study is produced by
\texttt{analysis/scripts/informative-dropout-by-design/20-paired-dropout.R},
run from the repository root through
\texttt{bash\ analysis/scripts/informative-dropout-by-design/20-run-paired.sh}.
The wrapper sets \texttt{VECLIB\_MAXIMUM\_THREADS}, \texttt{OPENBLAS\_NUM\_THREADS} and
\texttt{OMP\_NUM\_THREADS} to 1. The environment variables \texttt{P09\_REPS} and
\texttt{P09\_NULL\_REPS} set the replicate counts (this manuscript used
1,000 and
500). The driver writes
\texttt{paired-replicates.rds} (one row per design, regime, replicate,
condition and analysis), \texttt{paired-dropout.rds} (dropout diagnostics)
and \texttt{paired-meta.rds} (settings, elapsed time and session information)
to \texttt{analysis/data/informative-dropout-by-design/}. The script
\texttt{21-summarise-paired.R} in the same directory writes the four summary
files \texttt{paired-cells.csv}, \texttt{paired-loss.csv}, \texttt{paired-contrast.csv}
and \texttt{paired-dropout.csv}. Every number in Sections
\ref{sec-results} to \ref{sec-conclusions} is read from those files
by inline R when this manuscript is rendered.

Seeds are \texttt{set.seed(20261029\ +\ 100000\ *\ d\ +\ r)} for design index \(d\)
and replicate \(r\), plus 50,000 in the null regime. The run reported
here was made on the host R session (R 4.6.1, macOS, outside the
project container and without renv). Results from the container are
not draw-for-draw identical because the linear-algebra libraries
differ. The run took
98
minutes on 8 workers on a shared machine.

The earlier unpaired prototype is
\texttt{analysis/scripts/quick-sim/09-dropout-prototype-10min.R}. It calls
\texttt{set.seed(20260508)} once and seeds each replicate with
\texttt{100000\ *\ i\ +\ rep} for cell index \(i\). Its outputs are
\texttt{analysis/data/quick-sim/09-dropout-replicates.rds} and
\texttt{09-dropout-summary.txt}. The working protocol
\texttt{analysis/scripts/informative-dropout-by-design/00-ademp-protocol.md}
and its scaffold drivers (\texttt{00-common.R}, \texttt{01-study1.R} to
\texttt{04-study4.R}) were not run.
