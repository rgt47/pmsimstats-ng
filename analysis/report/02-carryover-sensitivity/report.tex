\section*{Abstract}\label{abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

\textbf{Background.} Aggregated N-of-1 trials pool repeated within-patient
on/off contrasts to validate predictive biomarkers. Under covariance
moderation, a multivariate-normal differential-correlation
architecture in which the biomarker-treatment interaction lives in a
treatment-state-dependent correlation, a companion study found that
carryover costs up to roughly 31\% of power in relative terms. The
analyst must still decide how to represent residual exposure in the
linear predictor, how to obtain a correctly sized test, and whether
to protect the test against a biomarker that is prognostic rather
than predictive.

\textbf{Methods.} We build the analysis model in stages, each stage
answering one question the previous one raised, within an
ADEMP-structured simulation\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morris2019simulation}{1}} of the
refined-process data-generating mechanism established in the
companion study. Stage 1 asks how residual drug exposure should
enter the linear predictor, comparing the binary on-drug indicator
(G1), that indicator plus a classical lagged-treatment term (G2) and
a continuous exposure-weighted predictor that decays with time since
discontinuation at an assumed half-life (G3), across a full factorial
of decay form, trial design, sample size and interaction strength
(216 cells, 500 replicates each). Stage 2 removes the analyst's
assumed half-life by choosing it from the data by AIC (G4). Stage 3
corrects the size of the test: the model-based standard error is
conservative under the mis-specified working covariance, so each
specification is refitted with a CR2 bias-reduced cluster-robust
standard error and Satterthwaite degrees of freedom (G8, G9). Stage 4
compares the resulting family against two external comparators on
the same simulated trials: the originally published analysis, which
has no residual correlation structure (E0), and a richer family that
adds design-expectancy terms and either fixes or bootstrap-calibrates
the half-life (F1, F1A0B). Stage 5 stress-tests the leading
specification against four prognostic biomarker pathways at two
confound strengths and against richer random-effects structures. A
closing gap-fill study extends the scenario checks to grids crossing
effect size and sample size (decay shape, true half-life, mean
moderation, autocorrelation), replaces the size diagnostic with a
2,000-replicate version, and runs a prognostic-confound ladder up to
the feasibility ceiling and a placebo-tracking biomarker at \(N\) up
to 280. Two defects in the simulation code found during the study
were corrected and every comparison reported here was run under the
corrected code.

\textbf{Results.} The lagged-treatment term adds nothing: G2 and G1 differ
by a mean of 0.002 in power across all 216 cells and agree on every
other measure. Exposure weighting recovers most of the power that
carryover removes, by 8 points at the Hybrid reference cell (0.888
against 0.806 for G1 at \(N = 70\), \(c_{bm} = 0.45\), \(t_{1/2} = 1\)
week) and 11 points under OL+BDC, and is never worse than G1 in any
design; an apparent collapse under the crossover design in earlier
drafts was the coding defect. Choosing the half-life by AIC matches
the oracle-half-life predictor without requiring the analyst to know
the half-life. The model-based test rejects at 0.037 against nominal
0.05; CR2 restores nominal size for every specification. On the
corrected 30-cell grid, G9 (AIC-selected half-life plus CR2) is the
most powerful or tied most powerful specification at every Hybrid and
OL+BDC alternative cell, with Type I error within the Monte Carlo
band at all eight null cells. The published analysis E0 is liberal at
every null cell (0.06 to 0.15). G9 holds nominal size under all four
prognostic pathways without an expectancy term, at both confound
strengths, and is at least as powerful as the bootstrap-calibrated
F1A0B in every alternative cell, including a four-fold half-life
misspecification. A power loss at the strongest confound, seen by
every specification, was traced to the confound being infeasible:
the joint covariance matrix was not positive definite and had been
silently repaired. A closed-form feasibility ceiling now bounds such
scenarios, and on a ladder of feasible confounds reaching to within
0.05 of it, G9's size, power and mean estimate are unchanged on every
pathway. Across the scenario grids G9's Type I error stays inside
the band at every null cell and its power is at or above G1 and F1 at
every alternative but one (mean moderation at \(N = 35\), where the CR2
correction costs six points). Random slopes on time or expectancy
change G9's power by under one point.

\textbf{Conclusions.} For a Hybrid aggregated N-of-1 trial under covariance
moderation we recommend G9: the exposure-weighted predictor with its
half-life chosen by AIC on the null model and refitted by REML,
tested with a CR2 cluster-robust standard error and Satterthwaite
degrees of freedom. It requires no pharmacokinetic input from the
analyst, holds nominal size including under prognostic biomarkers and
after half-life selection, and loses no power to the richer
expectancy-term family. The classical lagged-treatment term should
not be reported as a carryover adjustment. With 10 to 30 percent of
patients dropping out, completely at random or as a function of
baseline severity, G9 had no Type I error above 0.062 and exceeded
G1 and F1 in power at every cell; when dropout
removes most observations, every analysis loses power steeply and
G9's standard error becomes optimistic. The scope is the
covariance-moderation architecture.

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

We begin with the basic design. In a single N-of-1 trial, one patient
is crossed repeatedly between active treatment and a comparator. The
within-patient contrast between on-drug and off-drug periods
estimates that patient's individual treatment effect. An aggregated
N-of-1 trial pools many such series, which allows for
population-level inference at sample sizes smaller than a
parallel-group trial would require\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{2}}. The estimand
is the biomarker-treatment interaction, that is, whether a baseline
covariate \(B_i\) modifies the treatment effect. We evaluate analysis
approaches for inference regarding this estimand in a `hybrid'
N-of-1 design, defined in Section 2.2.

The main inferential complication in N-of-1 trials is carryover. A
drug's pharmacological effect does not switch off abruptly at
discontinuation. It continues to decay as a function of the drug's
half-life, so post-discontinuation observations remain somewhat
indicative of treatment effect. An exposure regressor that ignores
this is, in effect, a mis-measured version of what the patient
actually received, and the damage is twofold. First, the interaction
contrast is diluted. Occasions still carrying a substantial fraction
of the drug effect are coded identically to occasions carrying none,
which biases the biomarker-by-exposure coefficient toward zero, as
any covariate measured with error would. Second, those same
occasions are heterogeneous in a way the model does not represent,
so the unexplained variation inflates the standard error of the very
coefficient that has just been attenuated. The test loses on both
counts, and power falls accordingly.

We note that this is a loss of power, not of validity. Under the
null, \(c_{bm} = 0\), no biomarker-response association exists at any
level of residual exposure, so a mis-coded regressor has no
interaction to distort, and test size is unaffected by exposure
coding. The conservatism observed in Section 3.3 has a separate
origin in the working covariance and applies equally across
specifications. Carryover mis-specification is therefore a question
of efficiency, and the first stage of the study is framed in terms of
recovered power.

A companion study\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats_dgp_architectures2026}{3}}, building on
Hendrickson\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{2}}, shows that under the
covariance-moderation architecture carryover erodes power directly
and design-dependently. In that architecture the interaction lives
in the treatment-state-dependent covariance \(\text{Cor}(B_i,
BR_{it}) = c_{bm} \cdot \phi(t_{sd})\). At a half-life of one week,
the relative loss is roughly 31\% under OL+BDC but only 3 to 5\% under
crossover and Hybrid. We restrict attention here to covariance
moderation, where the carryover problem is largest and where
Hendrickson's original results were obtained. That earlier work fixed
the decay shape at exponential and evaluated only one analysis
specification.

This manuscript reports the process by which we arrived at a
recommended analysis for the Hybrid design, rather than a single
cross-sectional comparison. The analysis model was built in stages,
each stage answering a question the previous one raised, and the
order of the stages is the order in which the questions arise for
an analyst.

The first question is how residual exposure should enter the linear
predictor. Three positions are available toward the unknown decay.
The analyst may deny it, coding exposure strictly on or off
(Unadjusted, G1), which is the benchmark any remedy must beat. The
analyst may estimate it, adding a lagged just-off-drug nuisance term
whose magnitude the data determine (Lag-adjusted, G2), the classical
crossover-trial device of Jones and Kenward\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-jones2014crossover}{4}}.
Or the analyst may assume it, committing to a decay form and
half-life in a continuous exposure-weighted predictor
(Exposure-weighted, G3). G1 and G2 estimate the same coefficient and
differ by exactly one nuisance term, so their contrast isolates that
term; G3 changes the regressor carrying the interaction. We cross
all three against exponential and Weibull data-generating decay
forms in a full factorial (Stage 1).

The second question follows from the third position: an assumed
half-life is an oracle no real analyst has. We therefore let the data
choose it by AIC over a candidate grid (G4) (Stage 2).

The third question concerns the size of the test rather than its
power. The working covariance of the mixed model, a random intercept
with an AR(1) residual correlation, does not match the covariance
the data-generating mechanism induces, and the model-based test is
conservative. A CR2 bias-reduced cluster-robust standard error with
Satterthwaite degrees of freedom\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-bell2002bias}{5}} corrects this, and
the correction is applied to each specification, with a dedicated
check that data-driven half-life selection does not inflate size
(Stage 3).

The fourth question is whether anything outside this family does
better. Two comparators are fitted to the same simulated trials: the
originally published analysis, which uses a binary indicator and no
residual correlation structure (E0), and a richer family developed in
parallel within this program that adds design-expectancy terms to
protect against a prognostic biomarker and either fixes the
half-life or calibrates its selection by a parametric bootstrap (F1,
F1A0B) (Stage 4).

The fifth question is robustness of the leading specification to the
conditions a real trial is most likely to violate: a biomarker that
is prognostic as well as predictive, through four distinct pathways
and at two strengths, and a random-effects structure richer than a
random intercept (Stage 5). This stage also produced a methodological
finding about the simulation itself, a silently repaired
non-positive-definite covariance matrix, which we report because it
changes how such stress tests must be designed.

Two defects in the simulation code were found during the study and
corrected: a pre-exposure coding error in the exposure-weighted
predictor that affected the crossover design, and a cross-factor
correlation constant that departed from the documented
data-generating process. Every result in this manuscript was
produced under the corrected code, with one labeled exception. We
follow the ADEMP reporting framework of Morris, White, and Crowther\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morris2019simulation}{1}} throughout. Section 2 describes the
simulation design in ADEMP order, Section 3 gives the results stage
by stage, and Section 4 states the recommendation and its limits.

\section{Simulation design}\label{simulation-design}

The study simulates complete aggregated N-of-1 trials and analyzes
each one under every specification. A single replicate proceeds as
follows. Patients are allocated across the randomization paths of
the chosen trial design (Section 2.2). For each patient we draw a
pre-treatment biomarker together with the three latent response
components, namely the pharmacological response, the natural-history
trend, and the placebo-belief component. These are drawn jointly
across all eight measurement occasions from a multivariate normal
distribution whose means follow modified Gompertz trajectories. The
covariance carries within-component AR(1) autocorrelation,
cross-component correlations, and the treatment-state-dependent
biomarker-response correlation that encodes the interaction (Section
2.3). The observed symptom score at each occasion is the patient's
baseline severity less the sum of the three components, so that a
beneficial effect lowers the score. Carryover enters through a decay
function of time since discontinuation, which keeps part of the
pharmacological effect present at nominally off-drug occasions. Every
analysis specification in a given stage is then fitted to that same
simulated dataset, and we record the interaction coefficient, its
standard error, and its \(p\)-value from each. This is repeated 500
times for every parameter combination in the grid of Section 2.6,
1,000 times in the random-effects comparison of Stage 5 and in the
large-sample cells of the gap-fill study, and 2,000 times in the
gap-fill size diagnostic.

Fitting all specifications to a common dataset is deliberate. It
makes every contrast among them paired, so that differences are
estimated with substantially less Monte Carlo variance than
independent draws would give. Throughout, \(N\) denotes the \textbf{total}
number of patients in a trial, allocated as evenly as possible
across the design's randomization paths, so a design with \(P\) paths
assigns about \(N/P\) patients to each. At \(N = 70\) this is 35 per
path under the two-path designs and 17 or 18 per path under the
four-path Hybrid design.

\subsection{Aims}\label{aims}

The aim is to arrive at a recommended analysis specification for the
biomarker-treatment interaction in a Hybrid aggregated N-of-1 trial
under covariance moderation, and to document the evidence at each
stage of its construction. The stages ask, in order: how residual
exposure should enter the linear predictor; whether the analyst's
assumed half-life can be replaced by a data-driven choice; whether
the test is correctly sized and how to make it so; whether the
originally published analysis or a richer expectancy-term family
does better on the same trials; and whether the leading
specification survives a prognostic biomarker and a richer
random-effects structure.

\subsection{Trial designs}\label{trial-designs}

The three designs differ in how on-drug and off-drug occasions are
arranged, and so in how much post-discontinuation information each
generates. All three schedule eight measurement occasions per
patient across randomization paths, each a fixed on-drug/off-drug
schedule. Each design also carries an expectancy weight, one during
open-label periods and one half during blinded periods, scaling the
placebo-belief component.

\textbf{Crossover (CO).} A traditional two-period within-patient
crossover, blinded throughout, occasions spaced 2.5 weeks apart from
2.5 to 20 weeks. One path is active for the first four occasions
then comparator, and the other reverses that order and so never
discontinues, contributing no post-discontinuation observations.

\textbf{Open-label followed by blinded discontinuation (OL+BDC).}
Open-label titration at 4, 8, 12, and 16 weeks, then weekly follow-up
to week 20 with a blinded late switch to placebo. The two paths
discontinue after week 17 or 18, so only two or three occasions
follow.

\textbf{Hybrid.} The Hendrickson design and this program's reference.
Open-label titration at weeks 4 and 8 is followed by blinded
discontinuation weekly from week 9 to 12, then a brief two-occasion
crossover at weeks 16 and 20. The four paths vary discontinuation
timing and crossover-arm order. It is the richest of the three in
on-off transitions and the only one allowing more than one
discontinuation per patient. It is the design for which this
manuscript's recommendation is made; CO and OL+BDC are carried
through Stages 1 to 4 to show where the recommendation does and does
not transfer.

One consequence bears directly on the results. The first
post-discontinuation occasion falls one week after switching under
OL+BDC and Hybrid, but 2.5 weeks under CO. At the half-lives
examined here, roughly half the pharmacological effect therefore
persists into the first off-drug occasion under the two
non-crossover designs, against less than a fifth under CO. The
designs therefore differ substantially in how much carryover
contamination they can accumulate at all.

\subsection{Data-generating mechanism}\label{data-generating-mechanism}

Under covariance moderation, the biomarker and the biological
response are drawn jointly from a multivariate normal distribution
with a treatment-state-dependent correlation, \(\text{Cor}(B_i,
BR_{it}) = c_{bm} \cdot \phi(t_{sd})\), where \(\phi\) is the decay
form and \(t_{sd}\) the time since drug discontinuation. The
data-generating process is the refined process of the companion
study: graded coupling (zero before first exposure), separable AR(1)
within-component correlation at \(\rho = 0.7\) with a single
cross-component constant \(c_1 = 0.2\) scaled by \(\rho^{|w_t - w_s|}\)
at every lag, anchored carryover on the drug-response mean, and a
stop on non-positive-definite covariance matrices (Section 2.8).
Mean moderation appears only as one scenario cell in Stage 4; the
full cross-architecture comparison is in the archived full-scope
drafts (\texttt{archive/report.Rmd}, \texttt{archive/report-slim.Rmd}).

The decay function \(\phi : \mathbb{R}_+ \to [0, 1]\) maps \(t_{sd}\) to
a residual-effect multiplier. Two forms are used, each matched to a
common half-life \(t_{1/2}\). Exponential decay,

\[
\phi_{\text{exp}}(t_{sd}) =
  \exp\left(-\lambda t_{sd}\right), \qquad
  \lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{t_{1/2}},
\]

is classical first-order elimination, the default pharmacokinetic
assumption and the form every analysis specification assumes.
Weibull decay,

\[
\phi_{\text{weib}}(t_{sd}) =
  \exp\left(-\left(\lambda_w t_{sd}\right)^{k}\right), \qquad
  \lambda_w = \frac{(\ln 2)^{1/k}}{t_{1/2}},
\]

has a shape parameter \(k\) governing whether the elimination tail is
heavier than exponential (\(k < 1\)) or lighter (\(k > 1\)), recovering
the exponential form at \(k = 1\). The Stage 1 factorial uses
\(k \in \{0.7, 1.5\}\) alongside exponential; the Stage 4 decay-shape
misspecification cell uses \(k = 2\). All forms pass through the same
half-life-defining point, \(\phi = 0.5\) at \(t_{sd} = t_{1/2}\), so they
are comparable at a fixed nominal half-life even though their shapes
diverge on either side of it.

\subsection{Estimand and analysis specifications}\label{estimand-and-analysis-specifications}

The interaction estimand is the on-drug biomarker-response
correlation \(c_{bm}\), a dimensionless quantity. Bias and empirical SE
are reported on a calibrated effect-size scale, namely the expected
difference in on-drug response per SD of the biomarker at
\(t_{1/2}=0\). Power and Type I error are on the native test-statistic
scale, with the null estimand \(c_{bm} = 0\). The base analysis model
is
\texttt{nlme::lme(Sx\ \textasciitilde{}\ bm\ *\ X\ +\ t,\ random\ =\ \textasciitilde{}1\ \textbar{}\ ptID,\ correlation\ =\ corCAR1(form\ =\ \textasciitilde{}\ t\ \textbar{}\ ptID))}.
The specifications differ in how \(X\) is constructed, whether a
nuisance term is added, and how the standard error is obtained.
Internally, and in the code and data files described in the
Reproducibility section, each specification is stored under an
\texttt{E}-prefixed code; this manuscript refers to them by the compact
\texttt{G1}-\texttt{G9} code of Table \ref{tab:tab-spec-summary}, or by name.
Six of the nine carry the argument: G1, G2 and G3 (Stage 1), G4
(Stage 2), and G8 and G9 (Stages 3 to 5). G5, G6 and G7 appear in the
nine-specification heatmaps of Stage 3 for completeness and are
otherwise not discussed.

\subsubsection{Unadjusted (G1): binary on-drug indicator}\label{unadjusted-g1-binary-on-drug-indicator}

Here \(X = D_{it}\), the binary on-drug indicator (one on drug, zero
off). Carryover is not represented at all. An off-drug occasion
still carrying most of the pharmacological effect is therefore
coded identically to one carrying none. This is the default an
analyst obtains without considering carryover, and we treat it as
the benchmark the other specifications must justify their
additional structure against. It consumes no half-life, so it is
invariant to the analyst's pharmacokinetic assumptions.

\subsubsection{Lag-adjusted (G2): binary indicator plus estimated carryover term}\label{lag-adjusted-g2-binary-indicator-plus-estimated-carryover-term}

We retain \(X = D_{it}\) and augment the model with a lagged-treatment
indicator, \(L_{it} = D_{i,t-1}(1 - D_{it})\), marking off-drug
timepoints that immediately follow an on-drug one. This is the
classical crossover-trial device of Jones and Kenward\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-jones2014crossover}{4}}. Carryover magnitude is estimated from the
data rather than assumed, so, like Unadjusted, this specification is
invariant to the assumed half-life. \(L_{it}\) enters only as a
nuisance main effect. There is no \(\text{bm:}L_{it}\) product, so the
interaction actually tested remains \(\text{bm:}D_{it}\). Lag-adjusted
can absorb mean-level carryover, but it cannot repair the
interaction's attenuation.

\subsubsection{Exposure-weighted (G3): continuous decayed predictor}\label{exposure-weighted-g3-continuous-decayed-predictor}

Here \(X = D_{bc,it}\), a continuous exposure-decayed predictor
matched to the analyst's assumed decay form and half-life. At
on-drug timepoints \(D_{bc,it} = 1\). Off drug after first exposure it
decays as \(D_{bc,it} = (1/2)^{t_{sd,it}/\hat{t}_{1/2}}\), losing half
its value every \(\hat{t}_{1/2}\) weeks after discontinuation. Before
any exposure \(D_{bc,it} = 0\); this pre-exposure guard was absent in
earlier drafts (Section 2.8). The half-life assumption enters only
through the predictor values handed to \texttt{lme}, so Exposure-weighted
is, in effect, a different model for each assumed half-life. As
\(\hat{t}_{1/2} \rightarrow 0\), \(D_{bc,it} \rightarrow D_{it}\), so
Unadjusted is the boundary member of the Exposure-weighted family at
zero assumed half-life. In Stage 1 the assumed half-life is set
equal to the data-generating one, an oracle no real analyst has;
Stage 2 removes it.

\subsubsection{AIC-selected half-life (G4)}\label{aic-selected-half-life-g4}

This specification uses Exposure-weighted's formula but chooses
\(\hat{t}_{1/2}\) from the data. Writing \(M(t_{1/2})\) for the \texttt{lme} fit
with \(D_{bc,it}\) constructed at a candidate half-life, the selected
half-life is

\[
\hat{t}_{1/2} = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{t_{1/2} \,\in\, \mathcal{T}}
  \text{AIC}\big(M(t_{1/2})\big), \qquad
  \mathcal{T} = \{0.25,\ 0.5,\ 1.0,\ 2.0\}\ \text{weeks},
\]

with \(\text{AIC}(M) = -2\log L(M) + 2p\)\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-akaike1974new}{6}}. The
candidate fits are compared by maximum likelihood, since the
candidates differ in a fixed-effects column and REML likelihoods are
not comparable across fixed-effects designs; the selected model is
then refitted by REML and the interaction coefficient, its standard
error and \(p\)-value are read from that single refit, not pooled
across \(\mathcal{T}\). This mirrors \texttt{characterize\_carryover()}
(\texttt{R/carryover\_analysis.R}), the package's own real-data
half-life-selection routine. The cost is a post-selection inference
risk\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-berk2013valid}{7}}: the reported standard error treats
\(\hat t_{1/2}\) as fixed in advance when the same data chose it.
Stage 3 checks whether this manifests as Type I error inflation.

\subsubsection{CR2 cluster-robust variants (G8, G9)}\label{cr2-cluster-robust-variants-g8-g9}

The standard error of \(\beta_{bm:X}\) read from the fitted \texttt{lme}
object is valid only if the assumed residual covariance is correct.
The working covariance combines a patient random intercept with a
\texttt{corCAR1} AR(1) residual correlation, and the companion calibration
study\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats_calibration2026}{8}} shows this does not match the
covariance the data-generating mechanism induces. We quantify the
consequence with the calibration ratio

\[
\kappa = \frac{\overline{\widehat{SE}}}{SD\big(\hat\beta_{bm:X}\big)},
\]

the mean model-reported standard error across replicates divided by
the empirical standard deviation of the replicate point estimates;
\(\kappa = 1\) is correctly calibrated and \(\kappa > 1\) a conservative
test. The remedy is a cluster-robust sandwich variance, which derives
the variance of \(\hat\beta_{bm:X}\) from observed between-patient
variation rather than the assumed covariance. With only \(N = 35\) or
\(70\) clusters the naive Liang-Zeger form is itself downward-biased,
so we use the CR2 bias-reduced estimator of Bell and McCaffrey\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-bell2002bias}{5}} with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom, via
\texttt{clubSandwich}, replacing only the standard error, reference
distribution and \(p\)-value and leaving point estimates untouched. G8
is G3 with CR2 inference and G9 is G4 with CR2 inference; G6 and G7
are the corresponding variants of G1 and G2.

\subsubsection{External comparators: the published analysis (E0) and the F-family}\label{external-comparators-the-published-analysis-e0-and-the-f-family}

Two further analyses are fitted alongside the G-family to the same
simulated trials in Stages 3 to 5.

\textbf{E0} is the analysis actually published with the data-generating
process\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{2}}, read from the original repository
(commit \texttt{58b32a9}, \texttt{R/lme\_analysis.R}) rather than from this
project's vendored copy, which had acquired a residual correlation
structure at some later point. It fits \texttt{lme4::lmer} with a random
intercept and no residual correlation structure, uses the binary
on-drug indicator, and takes Satterthwaite \(p\)-values from
\texttt{lmerTest}. Every G-family specification therefore differs from it
in carrying \texttt{corCAR1}, which is a project addition.

\textbf{The F-family} adds the design-expectancy covariate \(D_e\)
(\(\eta_t\), one during open-label periods and one half during blinded
periods) and its product with the biomarker to G3's formula,
\(\text{Sx} \sim bm + t + D_{bc} + bm{:}D_{bc} + D_e + bm{:}D_e\),
with the same random intercept, \texttt{corCAR1} and CR2 inference as G8
and G9. The expectancy terms were introduced in the companion study
to protect the interaction test against a biomarker that is
prognostic rather than predictive. The members differ only in how
the analysis half-life is set: \textbf{F1} fixes it at a policy value of
0.2 week; \textbf{F1A} selects it by AIC with the interaction in the
selection model; \textbf{F1A0} selects it on the null model and refits
with the interaction, as G4 does; \textbf{F1A0B} calibrates F1A0's test
by a parametric bootstrap that repeats the selection on every
resample. F1 and F1A0B are the two reported here. Because \(D_e\) is
constant within every path of the crossover design, the F-family is
not identifiable there and is reported for Hybrid and OL+BDC only.

\subsubsection{Prognostic biomarker pathways}\label{prognostic-biomarker-pathways}

A biomarker may be associated with the outcome through channels
other than the drug response, and an interaction test that is
fooled by such an association is not usable in practice. Four
pathways are implemented in the data-generating process, each
setting one parameter with all others at their defaults: a
correlation between the biomarker and baseline severity (prog-BL,
\(c_{bl}\)); a correlation with the natural-history component at every
occasion (prog-TV-const, \(c_{tv}\)); an additive shift of the
natural-history mean proportional to the standardized biomarker
(prog-TV-grow, \(b_{tv}\)); and a correlation with the placebo-response
component at every occasion (prog-PB, \(c_{pb}\)). Three of the four
are entries in the biomarker row of the joint correlation matrix,
and their feasible strengths are bounded; Stage 5 derives the bound.

\subsection{Choice of analysis specifications}\label{choice-of-analysis-specifications}

The specifications were chosen to represent distinct, established
positions an analyst can take toward an unmeasured decay, and then
to answer the specific questions each stage raised, not to survey
the wider space of N-of-1 analysis models. Bayesian hierarchical
models, GEE as a primary model, randomization tests, flexible
spline decay curves, state-space treatments of drug concentration
and two-stage meta-analytic combination are all established in the
N-of-1 or crossover literature and none is evaluated here; Section
4.5 returns to three of them. An earlier draft also tested carryover
terms interacted with the biomarker (\texttt{bm:L} and \texttt{bm:t\_\{sd\}}); both
underperformed G1 at every cell, which we attribute to the
data-generating process having a single true decay parameter, so
that a second interacted coefficient adds estimation noise with no
signal to recover. They were dropped in favor of G4, which addresses
the same unknown, the decay half-life, by estimating it.

(\texttt{32-gap-fill-protocol.md}), then closed the items the stages left
open. All of its cells are on Hybrid with every specification fit to
the same trial, at 500 replicates unless stated. It replaces the
size diagnostic with four corrected-code null cells at 2,000
replicates (\(t_{1/2} \in \{0.5, 1\}\) weeks, \(N \in \{35, 70\}\)).
It extends four Stage 4 scenarios from one cell to a grid crossing
\(N \in \{35, 70\}\) with \(c_{bm} \in \{0, 0.25, 0.45\}\): Weibull
\(k \in \{0.5, 2\}\) data analyzed as exponential; a true half-life of
0.2, 2 or 4 weeks (the first below G4's candidate grid, the last
above it, and 2 at its boundary; \(c_{bm} \in \{0, 0.25\}\)); and the
mean-moderation architecture. It adds a fifth scenario, the
within-component autocorrelation \(\rho \in \{0.5, 0.9\}\), at
\(c_{bm} \in \{0, 0.30\}\), because the \(c_{bm}\) ceiling falls with
\(\rho\) and 0.45 is infeasible at \(\rho = 0.9\) (ceiling 0.391). And it
runs a prognostic ladder at \(N = 70\) chosen inside the ceilings of
Section 3.5: \(c_{pb}\) and \(c_{tv}\) at 0.40 and 0.45 and \(c_{bl}\) at
0.6 and 0.8, each at \(c_{bm} \in \{0, 0.30\}\), plus \(c_{bl} = 0.45\)
at \(c_{bm} \in \{0, 0.45\}\); and prog-PB at \(c_{pb} \in \{0.3,
0.45\}\) with \(c_{bm} = 0\) at \(N \in \{140, 280\}\) (1,000
replicates). It also imposes monotone dropout, completely at random
(MCAR) or with a hazard that rises with the baseline severity rank
(MAR, scaled by twice the rank so that the average hazard is
unchanged), at rates of 0.1, 0.2 and 0.3, with \(c_{bm} \in \{0,
0.45\}\) and \(N \in \{35, 70\}\). The rate is the fraction of patients
whose series is incomplete: the first occasion is always observed and
the per-occasion hazard over the remaining seven is \(1 -
(1 - \text{rate})^{1/7}\) (0.015, 0.031 and 0.050), which keeps about
95, 90 and 84 percent of the observations. A first run used the
earlier convention of the driver, in which the rate is the hazard at
each of the eight occasions including the first. That convention
keeps 64, 42 and 27 percent of the observations and leaves 10, 20
and 30 percent of patients with none, so it is not a 10 to 30
percent dropout scenario; it is reported separately as a
heavy-missingness stress case. Every cell passed a pre-flight check
that the smallest eigenvalue of its covariance matrix is positive
before any simulation ran.

\subsection{Performance measures and computing environment}\label{performance-measures-and-computing-environment}

For each cell we report Power, Type I error, Bias, Empirical SE
(calibrated scale), Coverage of the nominal 95\% Wald CI, MSE,
non-convergence rate, and the MCSE of each, following Morris et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morris2019simulation}{1}, Tables 5-6}. We chose \(n_{sim} = 500\) so
that the MCSE on power at \(p = 0.5\) would not exceed 0.022; at
\(n_{sim} = 500\) the two-MCSE band around a nominal 0.05 rejection
rate is approximately \([0.031, 0.069]\). No simulation runs while
this manuscript renders. All specifications are fit to the same
simulated dataset within each cell (common random numbers), so every
pairwise contrast carries reduced Monte Carlo variance relative to
independent draws. Exact file paths and versions are given in the
Reproducibility section.

\subsection{Corrections to the simulation code during the study}\label{sec-corrections}

Two defects in the simulation driver were found and corrected while
this study was in progress, and a third change in policy followed
from Stage 5. Each is stated here so that the reader can tell which
results it affects.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Pre-exposure coding of the exposure-weighted predictor
  (corrected 2026-10-06).} Before any exposure, \(D_{bc,it}\) was
  coded as \(\phi(0) = 1\) rather than 0, so a patient on the
  placebo-first path of the crossover design was treated as fully
  exposed at every visit. The same correction replaced G4's REML
  AIC comparison with the ML comparison and REML refit of Section
  2.4. A paired check fitting each simulated trial under the old and
  new coding (\texttt{26-co-dbc-fix-paired.R}) showed that the entire
  collapse of the exposure-weighted family under the crossover
  design reported in earlier drafts was this defect: with the
  correction, G3 and G4 match G1 under CO. Hybrid cells, where no
  patient starts on placebo, were unaffected.
\item
  \textbf{Cross-factor correlation constant (corrected 2026-10-21).} The
  documented data-generating process uses a single cross-component
  correlation \(c_1 = 0.2\) scaled by \(\rho^{|w_t - w_s|}\) at every
  lag; the driver had used 0.1 at lags greater than zero. The Stage
  1 factorial was rerun in full under the corrected constant, and
  every Stage 2 to 5 comparison was run after it. No result from
  before this correction is reported: the 3,000-replicate size
  diagnostic of earlier drafts was replaced by the 2,000-replicate
  corrected-code diagnostic of Section 3.3, which reproduces its
  conclusion.
\item
  \textbf{Non-positive-definite covariance matrices are refused, not
  repaired (policy change, 2026-10-26).} The published code repairs
  a non-positive-definite covariance matrix with
  \texttt{corpcor::make.positive.definite} and continues; the refined
  process recommends stopping. Stage 5 found that a prognostic
  confound at strength 0.6 produced such a matrix and that the repair
  had removed part of the interaction signal, so the driver now
  refuses such cells by default. Section 3.5 gives the evidence and
  the closed-form feasibility bound that replaces trial and error.
\end{enumerate}

The exploratory sensitivity blocks of earlier drafts (within-factor
autocorrelation, analyst-truth half-life mismatch, dropout,
effect-size curve, and single-cell comparisons of G4, G5 and the CR2
variants) all predate both corrections and are no longer part of
this manuscript. Their material remains in the supplement and the
archived drafts; the autocorrelation, half-life and dropout scenarios
were rerun under the corrected code in the gap-fill grids of Section
3.4. The dropout block of those drafts used the per-occasion
convention described in Section 2.6, under which a stated rate of
0.1 to 0.3 removed 36 to 73 percent of the observations, so its
rates are not comparable with the patient-level rates reported here.

\section{Results}\label{results}

Results are reported in the order the model was built. Each stage
states the question it answers, the data it uses, and what it passes
to the next stage. The Monte Carlo standard error on power does not
exceed 0.022 at \(p = 0.5\), and differences smaller than this should
not be over-interpreted.

\subsection{Stage 2: removing the analyst's half-life}\label{sec-stage2}

Stage 2 asks whether choosing the half-life from the data by AIC

\subsubsection{The strength-0.6 loss is a data-generating artifact}\label{the-strength-0.6-loss-is-a-data-generating-artifact}

A direct check of the covariance matrix (Table \ref{tab:stage5-pd})
shows why. Each strength-0.6 cell on prog-PB and prog-TV-const, and
prog-BL at \(c_{bm} = 0.45\), has a joint correlation matrix that is
not positive definite on any of Hybrid's four paths. Because the
driver then ran with silent repair, \texttt{corpcor::make.positive.definite}
replaced the matrix by a nearby positive-definite one before any data
were drawn. The repair shrinks the population on-drug minus off-drug
slope of the outcome on the biomarker, the quantity the interaction
term estimates, from about \(-3.3\) to \(-2.4\) (prog-PB) and \(-1.8\)
(prog-TV-const), and the fitted G9 estimates at those cells match the
repaired values (\(-2.5\) and \(-2.0\) at \(N = 35\)) while their empirical
standard errors fall rather than rise. The power loss is the repair
removing a third to a half of the interaction from the generated
data; there was nothing for any analysis model to recover. Every
strength-0.3 cell is positive definite, which is why none shows a
loss.

\subsubsection{The feasibility ceiling for a prognostic coupling}\label{the-feasibility-ceiling-for-a-prognostic-coupling}

The condition that failed is computable in closed form. Write \(R_0\)
for the joint correlation matrix with the biomarker removed, which
depends on no biomarker coupling, and \(r\) for the biomarker row,
which is a sum of fixed patterns scaled by the couplings,
\(r = c_{bm}\, g + c_{pb}\, p + c_{tv}\, q + c_{bl}\, b\). By the
Schur complement on the biomarker row, the joint matrix is positive
definite if and only if \(R_0\) is and

\[
r^{\top} R_0^{-1} r = c^{\top} A\, c < 1, \qquad
A_{jk} = x_j^{\top} R_0^{-1} x_k, \quad x \in \{g, p, q, b\},
\]

the biomarker's squared multiple correlation on everything else. The
feasible region is the interior of an ellipsoid, and for fixed values
of the other couplings the largest feasible value of any one is the
positive root of a quadratic. The design's ceiling is the minimum
over its paths. Table \ref{tab:stage5-ceiling} gives the ceilings
for the configuration used here, each verified by the smallest
eigenvalue of the full matrix just below and just above it.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

\subsection{What each stage established}\label{what-each-stage-established}

The sequence of stages was not planned in advance; each was built to
answer the question the previous one raised, and the recommendation
rests on the chain rather than on any single comparison.

Stage 1 established that the choice of exposure representation is a
question of power, not validity, and that only the representation
that changes the regressor carrying the interaction recovers any of
it. The lagged-treatment term, the classical remedy, changed nothing
measurable across 216 cells, and the reason is structural: it enters
as a main effect and leaves the attenuated interaction untouched. A
practitioner should not report a lagged term as an adjustment for
carryover. Exposure weighting recovered most of the loss under the
two designs with a blinded-discontinuation phase and was never worse
than the binary indicator; the earlier finding that it failed under
a crossover design was a coding defect, and we withdraw it.

Stage 2 established that the oracle half-life Stage 1 granted the
analyst is not needed. AIC selection over four candidates matches the
oracle within Monte Carlo error at every cell of the corrected grid,
including under the crossover design once the selection compares ML
fits and the predictor is coded correctly before first exposure.

Stage 3 established that the model-based test is conservative by
about a quarter of its nominal size, that the conservatism comes from
a working covariance that does not match the data, and that a CR2
cluster-robust standard error with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom
restores nominal size for every specification while recovering two
to five points of power. It also established that the post-selection
risk of data-chosen half-life does not materialize as Type I error
inflation under CR2.

Stage 4 established two things about analyses outside the family.
The published analysis, which lacks a residual correlation structure,
is liberal at every null scenario examined, by a factor of two to
three under the crossover and OL+BDC designs; adding \texttt{corCAR1} to it,
which every G-family specification does, is consequential and should
be stated as such. And the expectancy-term family, built to protect
against a prognostic biomarker, buys no size that G9 lacks and costs
power where the half-life is misspecified, because its fixed-half-life
member cannot adapt and its bootstrap-calibrated member pays for a
correction G9 with CR2 does not need. The scenario grids confirmed
this across sample size and effect size, with one exception worth
stating: under mean moderation at \(N = 35\) the CR2 variants trail
the model-based test by five to six points of power while matching
it in size. Dropout of 10 to 30 percent of patients changed neither
the size of G9 nor its ordering against G1 and F1; only the heavier
per-occasion convention, which removes up to 73 percent of the
observations, degraded its standard error.

Stage 5 established that G9 holds size under all four prognostic
pathways at every feasible strength, loses no power and no part of
its estimate to a confound anywhere inside the feasible region, up
to within 0.05 of the ceiling, and gains nothing from random slopes.
A placebo-tracking biomarker does not inflate its Type I error
beyond that of the expectancy-term family at \(N\) up to 280. Its
methodological contribution is the identification of a silently
repaired non-positive-definite covariance matrix as the source of an
apparent robustness failure, and the closed-form feasibility ceiling
that lets a stress grid be designed inside the valid region.

\subsection{The recommended analysis for a Hybrid trial}\label{the-recommended-analysis-for-a-hybrid-trial}

For a Hybrid aggregated N-of-1 trial under covariance moderation we
recommend G9:

\[
\text{Sx}_{it} \sim bm_i + t + D_{bc,it}(\hat t_{1/2}) +
  bm_i{:}D_{bc,it}(\hat t_{1/2}),
\]

with a patient random intercept and \texttt{corCAR1} residual correlation;
\(D_{bc,it}\) equal to one on drug, \(2^{-t_{sd}/\hat t_{1/2}}\) off drug
after first exposure and zero before it; \(\hat t_{1/2}\) chosen by
ML-AIC over \(\{0.25, 0.5, 1, 2\}\) weeks on the null model and the
selected model refitted by REML; and the interaction tested with a
CR2 cluster-robust standard error and Satterthwaite degrees of
freedom. Three properties recommend it over the alternatives
considered. It requires no pharmacokinetic input from the analyst and
matches the power of the oracle-half-life predictor. It holds nominal
size at every null scenario examined, including after half-life
selection and under four prognostic pathways, without an expectancy
term. And it is at least as powerful as the richer expectancy-term
family at every alternative scenario, with a clear advantage when the
true half-life is long.

The recommendation transfers to OL+BDC on the evidence of Stages 1
to 4, where G9 and G8 lead in the same near-tie. Under the crossover
design the specifications are within a few points of one another and
G9 is not worse than any; the choice there is immaterial on this
evidence, and the expectancy-term family is not identifiable at all.
Under the mean-moderation architecture G9 holds size and matches G1
at \(N = 70\) but trails it by six points at \(N = 35\); the
recommendation is stated for covariance moderation, and the archived
full-scope drafts remain the authority on the other architecture.

\subsection{What the expectancy terms are for}\label{what-the-expectancy-terms-are-for}

The G-family omits the design-expectancy covariate that the F-family
carries, and Stages 4 and 5 show that G9 does not need it for size
under any pathway at the strengths and sample sizes examined. One
mechanism deserves a note. The placebo-response amplitude scales with
expectancy, which differs between on-drug and off-drug visits in the
Hybrid design, so a biomarker correlated with the placebo response
acquires a small on/off slope difference by construction: the
population contrast under prog-PB is \(-0.14\) at strength 0.3 and
\(-0.27\) at 0.6 even with no true interaction, against \(-3.3\) at the
alternative. This is precisely what \(D_e\) and \(bm{:}D_e\) absorb. At
\(N\) up to 280 and \(c_{pb}\) up to 0.45 it produced no inflation in G9
beyond that of F1, which carries the terms (Section 3.5), and F1
matched G9's power at every prog-PB alternative. A trial with a
biomarker known to track placebo responsiveness therefore loses
nothing by adding the two terms, but the evidence here does not
require them.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{limitations}

The numbers come from a single parameter set calibrated to a
prazosin and PTSD trajectory, so absolute power values are
illustrative and the ranking, conditional on design, is the more
portable result. Dropout was examined at 10 to 30 percent of
patients, monotone, with the MAR mechanism tied to baseline severity
only; informative dropout related to the outcome or to treatment
response was not simulated, and the block has no alternative cell
without dropout under the same code. Under the heavier per-occasion
convention power collapsed for every specification and G9's
standard error became optimistic, so the recommendation is for
trials that retain most of their observations. The scenario grids use one alternative effect size per
scenario and the prognostic ladder one sample size. The feasibility
ceilings are specific to the Hybrid design at \(t_{1/2} = 0.5\) week,
\(\rho = 0.7\) and \(c_1 = 0.2\), and must be recomputed for any other
configuration; the closed form makes that immediate. Size at the
strength-0.6 prognostic cells was measured under a repaired matrix
and is a result about a modified data-generating process. The
large-sample prog-PB cells have no confound-free counterpart at the
same \(N\), so the small excess over nominal seen there for the CR2
and F-family tests at \(N = 280\) is not attributed. The effect-size
curve of earlier drafts was not rerun under the corrected code. And
the sensitivity checks vary one factor at a time, so they can miss
interactions between factors.

\subsection{Related approaches not evaluated}\label{related-approaches-not-evaluated}

Three N-of-1 analysis approaches sit outside the family examined and
would each require substantial independent development to enter the
comparison. Tang and Landes\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-tang2020ttests}{9}} propose formula-based
\(t\)-tests that correct a single-subject paired-difference test for an
estimated serial correlation; they have no biomarker-moderation term.
Schmid and Yang\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-schmid2022bayesian}{10}} describe a Bayesian
hierarchical alternative with partial pooling of individual-level
treatment, trend and autocorrelation parameters, noting that
carryover is tractable only in aggregated analyses like ours. Daza\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-daza2018causal}{11}} frames N-of-1 inference as a counterfactual
time-varying-treatment problem in which carryover is confounding to
be controlled rather than a predictor to be modeled. Senn, Julious
and Araujo's\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-senn2014four}{12}} finding that a simple paired analysis
can match carryover-adjusted mixed models motivated the
paired-difference specification G5 in earlier drafts; on the
corrected grid it trails the mixed-model family under every design
and is not pursued.

\section{Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Represent residual exposure in the regressor, not as a nuisance
  term.} The classical lagged-treatment term never improved on
  ignoring carryover, on any measure, in any design; an
  exposure-weighted predictor recovers most of the power carryover
  removes under the Hybrid and OL+BDC designs and is never worse than
  the binary indicator.
\item
  \textbf{Let the data choose the half-life.} AIC selection over four
  candidates, comparing ML fits and refitting the winner by REML,
  matches the oracle-half-life predictor at every cell and removes
  the one input the analyst cannot supply.
\item
  \textbf{Use a CR2 cluster-robust standard error.} The model-based test
  rejects at about 0.037 under the null; CR2 with Satterthwaite
  degrees of freedom restores nominal size for every specification
  and recovers two to five points of power, and it absorbs the
  post-selection risk of the half-life choice.
\item
  \textbf{Do not use the published analysis as is.} Without a residual
  correlation structure it is liberal at every null scenario, by two
  to three times nominal under the crossover and OL+BDC designs.
\item
  \textbf{Expectancy terms are not needed for size, and cost power when
  the half-life is fixed.} G9 holds nominal size under all four
  prognostic pathways at every feasible strength examined, up to
  within 0.05 of the ceiling, and is at least as powerful as the
  bootstrap-calibrated expectancy-term specification at every
  alternative.
\item
  \textbf{Check feasibility before stressing the data-generating process.}
  A prognostic coupling that exceeds the closed-form ceiling yields a
  non-positive-definite matrix; silently repairing it removes part of
  the interaction and produces a spurious robustness failure in every
  analysis model at once. The ceiling is a quadratic root and should
  be computed first.
\item
  \textbf{Recommendation.} For a Hybrid aggregated N-of-1 trial under
  covariance moderation, use G9: the exposure-weighted predictor at
  the AIC-selected half-life with CR2 inference. Under dropout of
  10 to 30 percent of patients it keeps its size and its power
  advantage, but it should be accompanied by an effort to retain
  patients, since heavier missingness costs every analysis power.
\end{enumerate}
