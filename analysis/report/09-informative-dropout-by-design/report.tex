\section{Abstract}\label{abstract}

\textbf{Background.} Aggregated N-of-1 trial designs vary substantially
in their robustness to informative dropout, yet the published
methodological literature has documented dropout as a generic problem
without adequately engaging the coupling between the trial-design family
(open-label (OL), open-label plus blinded discontinuation (OL+BDC),
traditional crossover, hybrid) and the inferential consequences of
informative dropout for the biomarker-treatment interaction estimand.
The unaddressed coupling is not a minor sensitivity issue: the bias and
power consequences of dropout differ by an order of magnitude across
design families even at a fixed overall dropout rate, and the most
powerful design under no dropout is not necessarily the most powerful
design under dropout.

\textbf{Methods.} We formalize the informative-dropout model introduced
in\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{1}} (hazard depending
on cumulative symptom worsening since baseline), place it within the
classical missing-data taxonomy, and then reproduce and extend
Hendrickson's four-design dropout-robustness analysis with wider
parameter ranges, sensitivity to dropout-pattern misspecification at the
analysis stage, and an explicit randomization-path decomposition. The
simulation employs the existing pmsimstats framework with extensions to
the dropout-pattern grid, and reporting follows
the\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morris2019}{2}} ADEMP framework.

\textbf{Results.} {[}Forthcoming.{]} Across all four design families,
informative dropout that selectively retains treatment-non-responders
appears to inflate the apparent biomarker-treatment interaction by
underestimating the on-drug response of the responder stratum. The OL
design is the most dropout-robust on power but contributes no useful
biomarker-prediction information; the OL+BDC and hybrid N-of-1 designs
are the most dropout-vulnerable on raw power but, paradoxically, recover
power through the so-called `happy accident' selection effect
(informative dropout preferentially preserves the most-informative
crossover blocks). The traditional crossover design is most robust on
combined power-and-bias under symptom-worsening-driven dropout but most
vulnerable under symptom-improvement-driven dropout, where
treatment-responders drop early and the crossover signal is degraded.

\textbf{Conclusions.} Trial designers should evaluate proposed N-of-1
designs against the anticipated dropout-pattern mechanism, not merely
against a generic dropout rate. We provide a design-by-dropout-pattern
recommendation table that maps three classes of dropout mechanism
(balanced, worsening-driven, improvement-driven) to preferred design
choices.

\subsection{Why the question matters}\label{why-the-question-matters}

\begin{bullets}

\textbf{Three reasons motivate the design-by-dropout coupling specifically for the aggregated N-of-1 design class.}

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item Informative dropout can selectively destroy within-participant contrasts central to the N-of-1 estimand.
\item Patient self-selection is correlated with latent biomarker-response components that are partly signal, not nuisance.
\item The four canonical designs differ in their phase-specific vulnerability windows.
\end{itemize}

\end{bullets}

\begin{bullets}

\textbf{The first reason is that informative dropout selectively destroys within-participant contrasts, not merely sample size.}

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item Unlike parallel-group RCTs, N-of-1 dropout removes specific within-participant contrasts central to the estimand.
\item A participant lost before the blinded discontinuation phase contributes nothing to the BR-versus-PB separation.
\item A 30-percent phase-selective dropout can degrade interaction power more than 50-percent uniform dropout.
\end{itemize}

\end{bullets}

\begin{orig}
The first reason is that the N-of-1 estimand depends on within-
participant contrasts that informative dropout can selectively destroy.
In a parallel-group RCT, dropout reduces the effective sample size but
does not, under MAR, systematically distort the per-arm mean response
that the analysis estimates. In an aggregated N-of-1 trial, dropout
removes specific within-participant contrasts: a participant who drops
out after the open-label phase but before the blinded discontinuation
phase contributes nothing to the BR-versus-PB separation that the design
is constructed to deliver. The information loss is not merely in the
sample size but in the combinatorial structure of the design. A
30-percent overall dropout rate that selectively removes participants
from the post-discontinuation phase can degrade the biomarker-treatment
interaction power more than a 50-percent dropout rate that drops
uniformly across phases.

\end{orig}

\begin{bullets}

\textbf{The second reason is that patient self-selection is correlated with latent components that are partly signal, not nuisance.}

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item High-BR patients tend to remain on drug; high-PB patients may drop at discontinuation; negative-TV patients may drop from worsening.
\item Each mechanism produces a different dropout pattern that interacts differently with the design family.
\item The worsening- and improvement-driven patterns operationalize the responder-versus-non-responder distinction the biomarker literature is validating.
\end{itemize}

\end{bullets}

\begin{orig}
The second reason is that patient self-selection into and out of the
trial is correlated with the latent BR-PB-TV components in ways that are
not nuisance but partly signal. Patients with strong pharmacological
response (high \(BR\)) are likely to remain on drug and therefore to
remain in the trial; patients with strong placebo response (high \(PB\))
may drop out at the blinded-discontinuation phase when their symptoms
return on the silently-switched placebo, mistaking the return for trial
failure; patients with adverse natural-history trajectories (negative
\(TV\)) may drop out from worsening regardless of treatment. Each of
these mechanisms produces a different dropout pattern, and each
interacts with the design family in a different way. The
\texttt{symptom-worsening-driven\textquotesingle{}\ and}symptom-improvement-driven'
patterns of\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{1}} are
operationalizations of the responder-versus- non-responder distinction
that the predictive-biomarker literature is endeavoring to validate; the
dropout pattern itself is therefore not incidental.

\end{orig}

\begin{bullets}

\textbf{The third reason is that each design family has distinct phase-specific vulnerability windows whose trade-offs this paper analyzes.}

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item OL is most dropout-robust but cannot identify the BR-versus-PB split required for the predictive-biomarker question.
\item OL+BDC introduces a discontinuation-phase window where responders may drop from symptom return.
\item The hybrid design has the largest vulnerability surface; the trade-off between identifiability and dropout vulnerability is the central design choice.
\end{itemize}

\end{bullets}

\end{rgt}

\begin{orig}
The third reason is that the four canonical designs differ in their
vulnerability windows. The open-label design has no discontinuation-
driven dropout and is therefore most robust to dropout overall, but it
cannot identify the \(BR\)-versus-\(PB\) split and is the wrong design
for the predictive-biomarker question. The OL+BDC design supplies
identifiability of the split but introduces a discontinuation-phase
vulnerability window where treatment-responders may drop out from
symptom return. The traditional crossover design has two discontinuation
windows (one per crossover) and is more exposed to
symptom-improvement-driven dropout. The hybrid N-of-1 design has the
largest number of identifying contrasts at risk and therefore the
largest set of vulnerability windows. The trade-off between
identifiability and dropout vulnerability is the central design choice
this paper analyzes.

\end{orig}

\section{Informative-dropout model}\label{informative-dropout-model}

\begin{bullets}

\textbf{The censoring hazard combines a time-only ignorable component with a symptom-trajectory-biased component, made absorbing by a monotone carry-forward pass.}

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item The per-timepoint censoring probability is the union of two independent draws: a flat component scaling with elapsed time and a biased component scaling with the convex-transformed symptom change since baseline.
\item Two hazard parameters govern the split: $\beta_0$ sets the overall dropout fraction and $\beta_1$ the proportion of that fraction routed through the symptom-biased component, with the convexity exponent $e_{b1}$ fixed at two.
\item A second carry-forward pass renders dropout absorbing: once a participant is censored at a timepoint, all later timepoints for that participant are censored.
\end{itemize}

\end{bullets}

\begin{rgt}
Lorem ipsum dolor sit amet, consectetur adipiscing elit, sed do eiusmod
tempor incididunt ut labore et dolore magna aliqua.

\end{rgt}

\begin{orig}
We adopt the hazard-based informative-dropout model implemented in the
pmsimstats \texttt{R/censordata.R} infrastructure, which operationalizes
the mechanism of\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{1}}. For
participant \(i\) at timepoint \(t\), the marginal censoring probability
is the union (logical OR) of two independent Bernoulli draws,
\[\Pr(\text{drop}_{it}) = 1 - (1 - p^{\text{flat}}_{it})(1 -
p^{\text{bias}}_{it}),\] where the flat component
\(p^{\text{flat}}_{it} \propto t\) depends on elapsed time alone and is
calibrated to a marginal dropout fraction of \(\beta_0(1 - \beta_1)\),
and the biased component \(p^{\text{bias}}_{it}
\propto t\,(\Delta_{Sx}(t) + 100)^{e_{b1}}\) depends on the symptom
change \(\Delta_{Sx}(t)\) since baseline and is calibrated to \(\beta_0
\beta_1\). The additive shift of \(100\) keeps the convex argument
positive across the realised range of symptom scores, so that a large
improvement lowers the dropout probability while a small change or a
worsening raises it. A second pass enforces monotone dropout: once a
participant is censored at a timepoint, every later timepoint for that
participant is also set missing. The hazard is thus governed by three
quantities: the overall rate \(\beta_0\), the biased proportion
\(\beta_1\), and the convexity exponent \(e_{b1}\), held at two
throughout.

\end{orig}

\begin{bullets}

\textbf{Whenever the biased component is active the mechanism is missing-not-at-random, because the censoring indicator at a timepoint depends on the symptom value that the same indicator renders missing.}

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item The flat component depends only on elapsed time, a fully observed design variable, and is therefore ignorable with respect to the latent response.
\item The biased component makes dropout at time $t$ depend on $\Delta_{Sx}(t)$, and because that observation is itself censored at the triggering timepoint the mechanism is MNAR in the Little-Rubin sense.
\item The departure from ignorability is controlled by $\beta_1$: at $\beta_1 = 0$ the mechanism reduces to the ignorable limit, and the MNAR departure increases monotonically with $\beta_1$.
\end{itemize}

\end{bullets}

\begin{rgt}
Lorem ipsum dolor sit amet, consectetur adipiscing elit, sed do eiusmod
tempor incididunt ut labore et dolore magna aliqua.

\end{rgt}

\begin{orig}
The two components occupy different rungs of the Little-Rubin ladder.
The flat component depends only on elapsed time, which is a fully
observed design variable rather than a function of the response, so on
its own it induces missingness that is ignorable for likelihood-based
inference. The biased component is the consequential one. Because the
censoring indicator for the observation at time \(t\) is a function of
\(\Delta_{Sx}(t)\), the symptom change realised at that same timepoint,
and because the triggering observation is itself rendered missing in the
carry-forward pass, the missingness of \(Y_{it}\) depends on the value
of \(Y_{it}\). This is the defining condition for missing-not-at-random
data\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-littlerubin2019}{3},\citeproc{ref-molenberghskenward2007}{4}}.
The mechanism is therefore MNAR for every pattern with \(\beta_1 > 0\),
and the standard MMRM appeal to a MAR
justification\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-mallinckrodt2001dropout}{6}}
does not hold. The extent of the violation scales with \(\beta_1\),
which makes the biased proportion, not the overall rate, the
methodologically load-bearing parameter.

\end{orig}

\begin{bullets}

\textbf{Five dropout patterns are fixed as cells in the $(\beta_0,\beta_1)$ hazard-parameter space, spanning a no-dropout reference through balanced, flat-weighted, bias-weighted, and high-rate conditions.}

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item The no-dropout reference sets $\beta_0 = 0$ and supplies the full-data gold standard against which the bias estimands of Study 2 are measured.
\item Balanced $(\beta_0 = 0.05, \beta_1 = 0.5)$, more-of-flat $(\beta_1 = 0.2)$, and more-of-biased $(\beta_1 = 0.8)$ hold the overall rate fixed while varying informativeness from mostly-ignorable to strongly MNAR.
\item High-dropout $(\beta_0 = 0.15, \beta_1 = 0.5)$ raises the overall rate at the balanced split, isolating the rate axis from the informativeness axis.
\end{itemize}

\end{bullets}

\begin{rgt}
Lorem ipsum dolor sit amet, consectetur adipiscing elit, sed do eiusmod
tempor incididunt ut labore et dolore magna aliqua.

\end{rgt}

\begin{orig}
The five dropout patterns are defined operationally as cells in the
\((\beta_0, \beta_1)\) parameter space of the hazard, with
\(e_{b1} = 2\) held throughout. The no-dropout reference
\((\beta_0 = 0)\) retains the complete data and furnishes the
gold-standard estimate of \(\beta_{bm:D}\) used in the second
bias-reference mode of Study 2. The balanced pattern
\((\beta_0 = 0.05, \beta_1 = 0.5)\) routes half of a modest five-percent
dropout fraction through the symptom-biased component. Holding
\(\beta_0\) fixed, the more-of-flat \((\beta_1 = 0.2)\) and
more-of-biased \((\beta_1 = 0.8)\) patterns move the same overall rate
toward the ignorable and the strongly MNAR ends of the informativeness
axis respectively, so that any difference in operating characteristics
between them isolates the effect of the mechanism from the effect of the
rate. The high-dropout pattern \((\beta_0 = 0.15, \beta_1 = 0.5)\)
triples the overall rate at the balanced split, isolating the
complementary rate axis. The sign convention of the biased component is
worsening-driven, in that a worsening relative to baseline raises the
dropout probability; the symptom-improvement-driven variant examined in
the design-recommendation of Section 6 is obtained by reflecting the
symptom-change argument, so that improvement rather than worsening
raises the hazard.

\end{orig}

\section{The four design families and their vulnerability
windows}\label{the-four-design-families-and-their-vulnerability-windows}

\begin{bullets}

\textbf{The four design families are distinguished by their phase structure, which determines both the within-participant contrasts that identify the biomarker-treatment interaction and the windows in which informative dropout can remove them.}

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item Each design is a set of randomization paths through an ordered sequence of on-drug and off-drug phases, encoded by the \texttt{ondrug} path vectors of \texttt{buildtrialdesign}.
\item The estimand-defining contrasts are within-participant differences across phases; informative dropout is damaging in proportion to how directly it removes those contrasts rather than merely reducing sample size.
\item The four designs are ordered by an increasing number of identifying contrasts at risk, and therefore by an increasing number of vulnerability windows, from open-label through the hybrid N-of-1 design.
\end{itemize}

\end{bullets}

\begin{orig}
Each candidate design is represented as a set of randomization paths
through an ordered sequence of on-drug and off-drug phases, encoded by
the \texttt{ondrug} path vectors that \texttt{buildtrialdesign} accepts.
The biomarker-treatment interaction is identified by within-participant
contrasts across those phases, so the consequence of informative dropout
for a given design is determined by which phase a dropout removes and
which contrast that phase carries. We take the four designs in order of
increasing number of identifying contrasts at risk.

\end{orig}

\begin{bullets}

\textbf{The open-label design has a single all-on-drug path, no discontinuation window, and is the most dropout-robust but cannot identify the pharmacological-versus-placebo split.}

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item The single path carries only an on-drug-versus-baseline contrast, which is confounded with natural history and cannot separate the $BR$ and $PB$ components.
\item With no off-drug phase there is no discontinuation-driven vulnerability window; dropout reduces only the precision of the on-drug slope.
\item The design is the robustness benchmark but is the wrong design for the predictive-biomarker question.
\end{itemize}

\end{bullets}

\begin{rgt}

\end{rgt}

\begin{orig}
The open-label design comprises a single path on which every timepoint
is on-drug. The only within-participant contrast available is the
on-drug response against baseline, which is confounded with the
natural-history trajectory \(TV\) and cannot separate the
pharmacological response \(BR\) from the placebo response \(PB\).
Because there is no off-drug phase, there is no discontinuation-driven
vulnerability window, and informative dropout can do no more than reduce
the precision with which the on-drug slope is estimated. The design is
consequently the most robust to dropout of the four and serves as the
robustness benchmark, but its inability to identify the
\(BR\)-versus-\(PB\) split makes it the wrong instrument for the
predictive-biomarker question that motivates the program.

\end{orig}

\begin{bullets}

\textbf{The open-label-plus-blinded-discontinuation design adds a silent-placebo phase that identifies the pharmacological-versus-placebo split but opens a discontinuation window in which worsening-driven dropout removes exactly that contrast.}

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item The estimand-defining contrast is the on-drug-versus-post-discontinuation response, which separates $BR$ from $PB$.
\item Treatment-responders whose symptoms return on the silently switched placebo may drop, mistaking the return for trial failure, which is a worsening-driven dropout located in the discontinuation phase.
\item Because the lost phase carries the identifying contrast, dropout here biases $\hat{\beta}_{bm:D}$ rather than merely inflating its variance.
\end{itemize}

\end{bullets}

\begin{rgt}

\end{rgt}

\begin{orig}
The open-label-plus-blinded-discontinuation design retains the
open-label phase and appends a blinded discontinuation phase in which
the active drug is silently replaced by placebo. The discontinuation
phase supplies the identifiability that the bare open-label design
lacks: the contrast between the on-drug response and the
post-discontinuation response separates the pharmacological component
\(BR\) from the placebo component \(PB\). That same phase is the
design's vulnerability window. A treatment-responder whose symptoms
return on the silently switched placebo may interpret the return as
trial failure and drop out, a worsening-driven dropout concentrated in
the discontinuation phase. The damage is more than a loss of precision:
because the removed phase carries the very contrast that defines the
estimand, dropout here produces a systematic negative bias in
\(\hat{\beta}_{bm:D}\), the effect quantified
in\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{1}} Section 3.4 and
reproduced in Study 2.

\end{orig}

\begin{bullets}

\textbf{The traditional crossover design has two randomization paths and two transition windows, and is robust to worsening-driven dropout but exposed to improvement-driven dropout at each crossover.}

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item The drug-first and placebo-first paths each carry a drug-block-versus-placebo-block contrast that identifies the within-participant treatment effect.
\item A responder who feels well on the drug block and worsens after crossing to placebo may drop, an improvement-driven dropout that removes the second block and one crossover contrast.
\item The two transition windows make the design more exposed than the single-window OL+BDC under improvement-driven dropout, though comparably robust under worsening-driven dropout.
\end{itemize}

\end{bullets}

\begin{orig}
The traditional crossover design has two randomization paths, one
beginning on drug and crossing to placebo and the other the reverse,
each composed of two blocks separated by a transition. The identifying
contrasts are the drug-block-versus-placebo-block differences carried
within each path. The design has two transition windows, one per
crossover, and its exposure is asymmetric in the dropout mechanism.
Under worsening-driven dropout it is comparatively robust, because
worsening tends to occur on the placebo block and a dropout there still
leaves the drug-block observations that anchor the contrast. Under
improvement-driven dropout it is exposed: a responder who feels well on
the drug block and then worsens after crossing to placebo may drop,
removing the second block and with it one of the two crossover
contrasts. The two transition windows therefore make the crossover
design more vulnerable than the single-window OL+BDC design when the
operative mechanism is improvement-driven.

\end{orig}

\begin{bullets}

\textbf{The hybrid N-of-1 design has the largest number of identifying contrasts at risk and the most vulnerability windows, but recovers power through the happy-accident selection effect that preferentially preserves the most informative crossover blocks.}

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item An open-label run-in followed by a randomized multi-block crossover gives four randomization paths and the widest set of vulnerability windows.
\item Participants with a clear open-label response are resolved early, so those continuing to the later randomized blocks are enriched for inconclusive open-label response.
\item Biased dropout therefore preferentially preserves the most informative crossover blocks, partially offsetting the raw power loss from the larger vulnerability surface.
\end{itemize}

\end{bullets}

\begin{orig}
The hybrid N-of-1 design
of\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{1}} combines an
open-label run-in, which establishes each participant's apparent
response, with a subsequent randomized multi-block crossover. With two
randomization points it admits four paths and the largest number of
identifying contrasts at risk of the four families, and therefore the
widest set of phase-specific vulnerability windows: the run-in phase,
each crossover block, and each transition between them. On raw power
this makes the hybrid design the most dropout-exposed. The exposure is
partly offset, however, by a selection effect
that\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{1}} terms a `happy
accident'. Participants whose open-label response is unambiguous,
whether clear responders or clear non-responders, are the ones most
readily resolved and most prone to leave early; those who continue into
the later randomized blocks are consequently enriched for inconclusive
open-label response, which is precisely the subgroup whose crossover
contrasts are most informative for the biomarker-treatment interaction.
Biased dropout thus preferentially preserves the most informative
crossover blocks, partially recovering the power that the larger
vulnerability surface would otherwise forfeit. The magnitude of this
recovery is the object of the randomization-path decomposition reported
in Study 3.

\end{orig}

\section{Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

\begin{bullets}

\textbf{The conclusions distil three findings: design-by-dropout differentiation, rate dominance over mechanism, and SE inflation as the power-loss mechanism.}

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item The four N-of-1 design families differ materially in dropout robustness in ways the published literature has not characterized.
\item Dropout rate dominates mechanism as the determinant of power loss; mechanism becomes load-bearing only when rates are comparable.
\item Estimand stability under informative dropout is reassuring but also a warning: coverage degrades as SEs widen without the model acknowledging it.
\end{itemize}

\end{bullets}

\begin{bullets}

\textbf{First conclusion: the hybrid design is the most dropout-robust of the four, losing less than half a percentage point under 40 percent biased dropout at the reference parameters.}

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item At $N=35$, $\beta_{bm}=0.45$, Architecture A: hybrid loses $<0.5$ pp, OL+BDC loses 5.6 pp, crossover loses 8.6 pp under \texttt{biased\_40}.
\item OL lacks the within-subject $D_{bc}$ contrast under Architecture A and is not viable for the biomarker-interaction question.
\item The hybrid's robustness extends the Hendrickson happy-accident argument without requiring path-stratified analysis.
\end{itemize}

\begin{orig}
First, the four candidate aggregated N-of-1 trial designs differ in
their robustness to informative dropout in ways that the published
methodological literature has not adequately characterized. At the
prazosin/PTSD reference parameters with \(N = 35\),
\(\beta_{bm} = 0.45\), and Architecture A, the hybrid Hendrickson design
is the most dropout- robust of the four, losing less than half a
percentage point of power under 40 percent biased dropout. The OL+BDC
design loses approximately 5.6 percentage points under the same
condition, the traditional crossover approximately 8.6 percentage
points, and the OL design lacks the within-subject \(D_{bc}\) contrast
required for the biomarker- treatment interaction under Architecture A.
The hybrid's robustness extends and corroborates
the\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{1}} happy-accident
randomization-path argument without requiring path-stratified analysis
to support the substantive claim.

\end{orig}

\begin{bullets}

\textbf{Second conclusion: dropout rate is the dominant power determinant; mechanism is load-bearing only when rates are comparable across competing design candidates.}

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item The \texttt{biased\_25} vs.\ \texttt{MCAR\_25} contrast at fixed 25 percent dropout is below MCSE detection at 500 replicates.
\item Moving to 40 percent biased dropout produces clear losses in OL+BDC and crossover; hybrid is unaffected.
\item Trial designers should evaluate designs against anticipated dropout rate first, and mechanism only secondarily.
\end{itemize}

\end{bullets}

\begin{orig}
Second, the dominant determinant of the design-by-dropout interaction,
in this prototype's evaluation, is the dropout rate rather than the
dropout mechanism. The \texttt{biased\_25} versus \texttt{MCAR\_25}
contrast at fixed 25 percent dropout produces within-design differences
below MCSE detection at 500 replicates per cell; moving to 40 percent
biased dropout, by contrast, produces clear power losses in every design
except the hybrid. Trial designers should therefore evaluate proposed
N-of-1 designs against the anticipated dropout rate in their target
population first, and only secondarily against the bias mechanism. The
mechanism becomes the load-bearing factor in the regime where the rate
is comparable across competing trial-population candidates.

\end{orig}

\begin{bullets}

\textbf{Third conclusion: estimand stability is reassuring but also a coverage warning, since the $\hat\beta_{bm:D_{bc}}$ distribution widens without the model acknowledging it.}

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item The largest within-design shift in $\hat\beta_{bm:D_{bc}}$ is below 0.04 across the no-dropout to \texttt{biased\_40} range.
\item Power degradation traces to SE inflation; the inferential target is preserved under informative dropout in this regime.
\item Nominal CI coverage may understate true uncertainty: the coefficient distribution widens but the model does not acknowledge it.
\end{itemize}

\end{bullets}

\begin{orig}
Third, the mean estimated coefficient \(\hat\beta_{bm:D_{bc}}\) is
stable across dropout cells, with the largest within-design shift below
0.04 across the no-dropout to \texttt{biased\_40} range. The power
degradation under informative dropout therefore traces to standard-error
inflation rather than systematic bias in the estimand. This finding is
reassuring for the validity of the aggregated N-of-1 framework in the
presence of informative dropout, in the sense that the inferential
target is preserved, but it is also a warning to trialists that nominal
coverage of confidence intervals may understate the true uncertainty in
the dropout regime, since the \(\hat\beta_{bm:D_{bc}}\) distribution
widens without the model acknowledging it.

\end{orig}

\begin{bullets}

\textbf{Three production-grade extensions are scoped: path tracking, Architecture B coverage, and higher replicate counts to resolve the biased-versus-MCAR distinguishability question.}

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item Per-path-id tracking is needed for the randomization-path decomposition of the happy-accident effect.
\item Architecture B coverage is needed to interpret the OL row as a power estimate rather than a structural null check.
\item Resolving the \texttt{biased\_25} vs.\ \texttt{MCAR\_25} question requires 2,000 or more replicates per cell.
\end{itemize}

Docker container (image hash recorded in \texttt{Dockerfile}; package
set captured in \texttt{renv.lock}). Principal packages used by the
simulation pipeline are \texttt{nlme} (continuous-time linear-mixed
analysis with \texttt{corCAR1} residual correlation), \texttt{parallel}
(8-core \texttt{mclapply} for parameter-grid sweeps),
\texttt{data.table} (the \texttt{original-extended} simulation
collection), \texttt{furrr} and \texttt{purrr} (the \texttt{tidyverse}
simulation collection), and \texttt{rmarkdown} plus \texttt{bookdown}
for the manuscript build. The informative-dropout module is at
\texttt{R/censordata.R}.

Each simulation driver sets \texttt{set.seed(20260507)} at the top of
the replicate loop and uses \texttt{parallel::mc.set.seed} inside
\texttt{mclapply}. Per-replicate seeds derive from the master seed by
\texttt{+\ rep\_idx}.

Per-replicate simulation outputs are checkpointed at
\texttt{analysis/data/quick-sim/09-dropout-replicates.rds}; Morris ADEMP
cell-level summaries are at the companion
\texttt{09-dropout-summary.txt}. Driver scripts are under
\texttt{analysis/scripts/informative-dropout-by-design/} and
\texttt{analysis/scripts/quick-sim/09-dropout-prototype-10min.R}. The
pre-registered ADEMP plan is at
\texttt{analysis/scripts/informative-dropout-by-design/00-ademp-pre-registration.md}.
