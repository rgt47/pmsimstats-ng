\section*{Abstract}\label{abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

\textbf{Background.} A predictive biomarker is validated by an interaction
test. In an aggregated N-of-1 design every participant is observed on
and off drug, and the power of that test is estimated by simulation,
which can only show what its data-generating process (DGP) allows. We set out three requirements for such a DGP: a valid
(positive-definite) joint distribution, an interaction that survives
carryover, and flexibility to impose stress conditions. We compare
candidate choices for each of its elements against them. The DGP is a multivariate normal
model in which the predictive biomarker is a correlation with the
drug response (Hendrickson et al.~2020).

\textbf{Methods.} We derive the closed-form power of a paired-difference
interaction statistic and a Schur-complement bound on the largest
feasible interaction. We check both by Monte Carlo simulation. We
assume a biomarker that is predictive only: it is correlated with the
drug response and not with baseline severity, the placebo response or
the natural history. The Hybrid design is the primary case.

\textbf{Results.} The first element is the biomarker-response correlation
at off-drug visits. If it stays constant after first exposure, power
falls nearly to the test size under any carryover. If it decays at the
carryover half-life, power returns to its no-carryover value at
half-lives of 0.1 to 0.2 week and declines at longer ones. Second, the
serial correlation structure bounds the
largest interaction: 0.256 under compound symmetry and 0.474 under
separable AR(1) at a serial correlation of 0.7. A PTSD symptom series
suggests 0.9, where the bound falls to 0.34 and the Hybrid design needs
about half the sample it needs at 0.7.

\textbf{Conclusions.} We recommend a DGP with a biomarker-response
correlation that decays after discontinuation, a separable AR(1)
structure, and a check that refuses infeasible effect sizes. We give
closed-form results, code and planning sample sizes. Its parameter
values are not yet calibrated, so it is a step toward an optimal DGP.

\textbf{Trial registration.} Not applicable.

\textbf{Keywords:} N-of-1 trials; predictive biomarker; simulation;
carryover; data-generating process; power; prazosin.

\section{Background}\label{background}

Validating a predictive biomarker requires a biomarker-by-treatment
interaction test (\citeproc{ref-sargent2005}{1}). In an aggregated N-of-1 design every
participant is observed both on and off drug, so the test can be made
within participants. Its power must be estimated by simulation.

A simulation can show only what its data-generating process (DGP)
allows. The DGP decides how the biomarker acts, how the drug effect
builds and wanes, and how one period carries into the next. Yet there
is little guidance on what a DGP for these trials should be. In this
paper we explore that question. We set out three requirements a DGP
should meet. We then take its elements one at a time. For each element
we compare candidate choices against the requirements and keep the
better one. The result is a recommended DGP. We do not claim it is optimal.
We say which of its parts remain uncalibrated.

The DGP builds on the model of Hendrickson et al. (\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{2}),
the only DGP we found that encodes a predictive biomarker for these
designs. It draws the baseline biomarker and three latent response components at
every visit from one multivariate normal distribution. The trial
design assembles the correlation matrix from each participant's
exposure state. The biomarker enters as a correlation with the drug
response. The primary case is the Hybrid design. It combines
open-label titration, blinded discontinuation and a brief crossover,
with carryover between phases.

\subsection{Aggregated N-of-1 trials and predictive biomarkers}\label{aggregated-n-of-1-trials-and-predictive-biomarkers}

In an aggregated N-of-1 trial each participant moves between drug and
placebo, or between drug and no drug, several times. The individual
series are analyzed together (\citeproc{ref-nikles2011}{3}--\citeproc{ref-punja2016}{5}).
Every participant contributes both an on-drug and an off-drug
response. The design can therefore estimate how the drug effect varies
between people. It can then test whether a baseline measurement
predicts who benefits (\citeproc{ref-senn2016mastering}{6},\citeproc{ref-schork2015}{7}). The motivating
example is prazosin for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).
Prazosin has been tested in several randomized trials
(\citeproc{ref-raskind2003}{8}--\citeproc{ref-raskind2018}{10}). The largest, the VA
Cooperative Studies Program trial CSP 563 (304 veterans), found no
benefit on its primary outcomes (\citeproc{ref-raskind2018}{10}). In a secondary
analysis of an earlier trial in 67 soldiers, higher pretreatment
standing systolic blood pressure was associated with greater symptom
reduction on prazosin but not on placebo (\citeproc{ref-raskind2016bp}{11}). The
difference was about 14 points on the Clinician-Administered PTSD
Scale per 10 mm Hg.
Blood pressure was therefore proposed as a candidate predictive
biomarker. That association did not replicate in our reanalysis of
CSP 563 (Additional file 1; participant-level data not public). In 279 participants the arm-by-blood-pressure interaction was
+0.25 points per 10 mm Hg (95\% CI \(-2.72\) to 3.21), which excludes
the published value. Prazosin and blood pressure therefore serve here
as a worked example for the units of the planning section, not as an
established predictive biomarker.

Recent reviews cover the design and analysis of N-of-1 trials,
including Bayesian models for series of trials
(\citeproc{ref-duan2022personalized}{12},\citeproc{ref-schmid2022bayesian}{13}). Closed-form power
calculations exist only for simplified versions of these designs
(\citeproc{ref-wang2019}{14},\citeproc{ref-senn2002}{15}). Power is therefore estimated by
simulating trials from a data-generating process (DGP) and analyzing
each simulated trial (\citeproc{ref-morris2019simulation}{16}). Several simulators of
N-of-1 and aggregated N-of-1 data exist. Percha et al. (\citeproc{ref-percha2019}{17})
simulate single series with exponential wash-in and wash-out to choose
block lengths and sampling rates. Chen and Chen (\citeproc{ref-chen2014}{18}) generate
cycles under compound symmetry or AR(1) with a carryover effect to
compare analyses. Blackston et al. (\citeproc{ref-blackston2019}{19}) and Gärtner et al.
(\citeproc{ref-gartner2023}{20}) simulate aggregated series with carryover to compare
designs and estimators. None of these four represents a baseline
biomarker that modifies the drug response. Tueller et al.
(\citeproc{ref-tueller2022}{21}) estimate power for idiographic within-subject trials by
simulation. Grenet et al. (\citeproc{ref-grenet2020}{22}) simulate
crossover and parallel-group trials with a binary predictive biomarker.
They encode it as a mean-term interaction. In single-case research,
multilevel models with moderators of the intervention effect are
established (\citeproc{ref-vandennoortgate2003}{23},\citeproc{ref-moeyaert2014}{24}), and Moeyaert et al.
(\citeproc{ref-moeyaert2022power}{25}) estimate by simulation the power to detect such a
moderator. There, too, the moderator enters the mean model.

\subsection{The correlation encoding}\label{the-correlation-encoding}

The DGP has three parts. The first is a component
decomposition of the symptom score. The score is the baseline level
minus three responses. Each response follows a modified Gompertz
curve. The drug-response component (BR) is driven by time since the
current on-drug period began. The placebo-response component (PB) is
driven by time in the trial and scaled by the participant's
expectancy, the probability of receiving drug given the design. The
natural-history component (TV) is driven by time in the trial. The
second part is the design specification for open-label, crossover,
blinded-discontinuation and Hybrid designs. It tracks time on drug,
time since discontinuation and expectancy at each visit. The third
part is open software (the \texttt{pmsimstats} R package).

The DGP also encodes the predictive biomarker in a distinctive way. Statistical models and sample-size methods for N-of-1
and crossover designs usually represent treatment-effect heterogeneity
as random participant-by-treatment variation
(\citeproc{ref-senn2016mastering}{6},\citeproc{ref-senn2004individual}{26}--\citeproc{ref-zucker1997}{30}).
The DGP instead places the biomarker in the same multivariate
normal distribution as the response components. The biomarker has a
nonzero correlation \(c_{bm}\) with the drug response at on-drug visits.
Without carryover it has zero correlation elsewhere. We call \(c_{bm}\)
the biomarker-response correlation. A predictive biomarker, a
moderator of the treatment effect (\citeproc{ref-kraemer2002moderators}{31}), is then
one whose association with the outcome differs between on-drug and
off-drug visits. The parameterization keeps the marginal distribution
of the drug response fixed. It expresses the strength of the
interaction as a correlation. We call this the correlation encoding of
the interaction.

The statement that the encoding has no precedent rests on three
database searches. On 2026-10-27 we searched PubMed for (N-of-1 OR
single patient OR aggregated N-of-1) AND simulation AND (biomarker OR
effect modifier OR treatment-by-covariate) AND (power OR sample size).
It returned one record, the study that proposed the encoding
(\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{2}). On 2026-11-03 we searched
PubMed titles and abstracts for (``N-of-1'' OR ``single-patient'' OR
``single-case'' OR ``aggregated N-of-1'' OR crossover) AND (simulation OR
simulated OR ``Monte Carlo'') AND (biomarker OR moderator OR ``effect
modifier'' OR ``treatment-by-covariate'') AND (power OR ``sample size'').
We ran the same terms, without ``Monte Carlo'', against abstracts in
Europe PMC. Both returned two records: that study and a simulation of moderator power in single-case multilevel models
(\citeproc{ref-moeyaert2022power}{25}). Neither search retrieved Grenet et al.
(\citeproc{ref-grenet2020}{22}), which we identified otherwise, so the searches are not
exhaustive. Web of Science and Scopus were not searched.
The searches cover the N-of-1, crossover and single-case literature
indexed in these two databases. They do not cover the wider
literature on joint simulation of covariates and outcomes, such as
copula methods. In that literature, generating variables jointly from
a specified correlation matrix, and checking that the matrix is
feasible, is standard (\citeproc{ref-ghosh2003norta}{32},\citeproc{ref-joe2006generating}{33}). What we
found no precedent for is a biomarker-by-treatment interaction encoded
as a correlation whose value depends on the design and the exposure
state. The claim is therefore limited to the N-of-1 literature we
searched.

\subsection{Why the joint distribution is built from the design}\label{why-the-joint-distribution-is-built-from-the-design}

The simplest DGP that could serve would generate one symptom score per
visit. Its mean would carry the design effects and an interaction term
\(\beta\, B_i D_{it}\). Its residual would be compound symmetric or
AR(1). Most simulators of crossover and N-of-1 data use that form
(\citeproc{ref-wang2019}{14},\citeproc{ref-chen2014}{18}), and the analysis model
assumes it. It is adequate when the question is the main effect. For
the questions this series asks, the joint-distribution form differs
from it in three ways. The simple form stays
usable: a mean model with a random participant-by-treatment slope
(\citeproc{ref-senn2016mastering}{6},\citeproc{ref-araujo2016}{27}) is the conditional equivalent of the
joint-distribution form (Discussion).

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{How the moderation persists after discontinuation is stated
  separately from the mean carryover.} Carryover in crossover trials
  is usually modeled as a mean effect (\citeproc{ref-jones2014crossover}{34}), a model
  whose usefulness has been questioned (\citeproc{ref-senn1992carryover}{35},\citeproc{ref-senn1998robust}{36}). In the
  simple form the
  analyst adds it as a term such as \(\beta B_i D_{bc,it}\). In the
  joint-distribution form it is the correlation profile \(g_t\). Either
  is a modeling choice. The joint-distribution form makes the choice
  visible in the same quantity that sets the effect size.
\item
  \textbf{The interaction is expressed as a share of between-person
  variation.} With the marginal variance of the drug response held
  fixed, \(c_{bm}^2\) is the share of the on-drug drug-response
  variance that the biomarker explains linearly. Appendix A.6 relates
  it to a responder-class model. A mean model with a random
  participant-by-treatment slope implies the same kind of quantity
  through the ratio of the fixed slope to the slope variance. Neither
  form makes the residual variance depend on the biomarker.
\item
  \textbf{Existence becomes a question with an answer.} In the simple form
  every parameter combination yields a valid distribution. In the
  joint-distribution form, with the marginal variances fixed, it does
  not. Whether the simulated effect sizes are jointly possible, and
  how much of the interaction the design can see, then have
  closed-form answers.
\end{enumerate}

In the joint-distribution form, the biomarker and the three response
components at every visit form one multivariate normal vector. The
design assembles its correlation matrix. The biomarker is correlated
with the drug response at on-drug visits, and at a value set by the
exposure state at off-drug visits. The components are serially
correlated in calendar weeks. The components are cross-correlated
through a common block. Three results follow. The largest feasible
interaction has a closed form. It depends
on the design and on two modeling choices: the marginal drug-response
variance is held fixed, and the drug-response component keeps its
full variance and serial correlation at off-drug and pre-exposure
visits. We call it the feasibility bound. The power of a
paired-difference statistic has a closed form. The design enters it
through the on/off correlation difference, the on-drug correlation
minus the mean off-drug correlation. The residual structure the
analysis model should assume can be derived from the DGP. Three
requirements come with this form. The marginal
covariance of the outcome is implied rather than chosen. The joint
distribution must exist. Every entry must be calibrated or declared
uncalibrated. The Discussion shows that, conditional on the biomarker,
the correlation encoding reduces to the mean-term form with a smaller
residual variance, so the standard form remains available within it.
We do not claim the correlation encoding is preferable to a mean-term
or random-coefficient form.

The DGP is not new in its parts. Simulating repeated measures from a
multivariate normal with compound symmetry or AR(1) and a carryover
term is routine (\citeproc{ref-wang2019}{14},\citeproc{ref-chen2014}{18}). Decomposing a clinical
trajectory into disease progression, placebo response and drug action
is the pharmacometric convention (\citeproc{ref-holford2015}{37}). Separable (Kronecker)
covariances for several response variables measured repeatedly are an
established model (\citeproc{ref-galecki1994}{38},\citeproc{ref-naik2001kronecker}{39}). Hendrickson et
al. (\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{2}) combined these parts. It placed a baseline variable in the joint distribution of the
latent response components, with a correlation indexed by each visit's
exposure state. Designs for heterogeneity analyses in cluster
randomized trials also use correlations of an effect modifier
(\citeproc{ref-li2023}{40}). These are intraclass correlations of the modifier and of
the outcome, and the modification itself is a term in the mean model.
This paper refines that combination. We found no other instance of it
in the N-of-1 literature we searched (above).

\subsection{Requirements for the data-generating process}\label{requirements-for-the-data-generating-process}

We judge candidate choices against three requirements. Each stage of
the Results takes one element of the DGP, compares the candidates,
and ends with the choice and the requirement it rests on.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{A valid joint distribution.} The biomarker, baseline and the
  three response components at every visit are drawn from one
  multivariate normal distribution. Their correlation matrix must
  therefore be positive definite: every eigenvalue must be positive.
  We meet this by construction in two steps. The response block uses
  a separable AR(1) structure, the Kronecker product of a
  cross-component matrix and an AR(1) matrix in calendar weeks. It is
  positive definite for any visit schedule (Appendix A.5). The
  biomarker row is then constrained. By the Schur complement, the full
  matrix is positive definite if and only if \(c_{bm}\) lies below a
  bound set by the design and the correlation profile, the
  feasibility bound (Appendix A.4). A matrix outside the bound is
  refused, not repaired.
\item
  \textbf{An interaction that survives carryover.} The
  biomarker-by-treatment interaction is a contrast between on-drug and
  off-drug visits, and under carryover it must remain one. The
  biomarker-response correlation off drug must therefore fall as the
  drug effect fades. We measure this by the on/off correlation
  difference, which governs power in closed form (Methods). The
  carryover half-life should mean the same thing for the
  drug-response mean and for the correlation.
\item
  \textbf{Flexibility for stress conditions.} The DGP should be able to
  impose the conditions under which analyses are compared, such as
  dropout, measurement error and a different decay form. These are
  options, off by default, so that their effect on size and power can
  be measured.
\end{enumerate}

Where the requirements leave a choice open, we use pharmacological
plausibility for a short-half-life, titrated drug such as prazosin,
and say so where it decides a stage. Every parameter is labeled as a
default value, a choice made here, or uncalibrated (Table
\ref{tab:spec}). Calibration is reported, not required: the values
have not been re-estimated for this paper, and a reanalysis of CSP 563
(planning section) estimates some of them on the total symptom score
and shows that others cannot be identified from a parallel-group
trial. A DGP meeting the three requirements is what we call the
recommended DGP. Its label in the repository's code and data is
\texttt{refined}.

\textbf{The reference drug.} Prazosin's plasma half-life is about 2.3 hours
(\citeproc{ref-hobbs1978}{41}), against weekly visits. The plasma concentration
therefore falls by orders of magnitude within a day of the last dose.
We assume a symptom-level half-life of 0.1 to 0.2 week (17 to 34
hours), or 7 to 15 plasma half-lives, as the pharmacological range.
No symptom-response half-life for prazosin in PTSD has been reported,
so the range is an assumption. A drug's effect need not decline with
its plasma concentration (\citeproc{ref-holford1981dose}{42}), so the plasma half-life
is only a first guide. The Results
therefore cover 0 to 2 weeks and label half-lives of 0.5 week and
above as behavioral scenarios. The drug is re-titrated after an
interruption, which bears on how the DGP treats re-exposure (Stage 3).

\subsection{Aims}\label{aims}

We have three aims. First, we judge the elements of a DGP for these
trials against the three requirements, one element at a time. The elements
are the handling of infeasible matrices, the biomarker-response
correlation off drug, carryover on the
mean, the serial correlation, the cross-component structure, and the
optional features of measurement error, decay form and dropout. Second, we
derive in closed form what each candidate does to feasibility and to
power. Third, we collect the choices as a recommended DGP and give the
sample sizes a trialist needs to plan under it. The Hybrid design is
the primary case. The open-label, blinded-discontinuation and
crossover designs are comparators.

\textbf{The biomarker is assumed not to be prognostic.} Throughout, the
biomarker is predictive only. It is correlated with the drug response
and with nothing else: its correlations with baseline severity, the
placebo response and the natural history are zero. A biomarker that is also prognostic can bias the interaction test
when the analysis model omits or misrepresents its prognostic effect
(\citeproc{ref-haller2018}{43},\citeproc{ref-kent2020path}{44}). That case, the pathways through which it
acts, their joint feasibility with the biomarker-response correlation,
and the analyses that remain valid under it, are the subject of a
companion paper (\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper15}{45}). In the motivating trial
the assumption is partly supported: in CSP 563 the biomarker is not
associated with baseline severity (planning section). The data cannot
show whether it is associated with the placebo response or the
natural history.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

The Methods describe the parts of the DGP that are fixed, the elements
left open, and the tools used to compare candidate choices: the
estimands, a statistic with a closed-form distribution, the
feasibility bound and the simulation studies. Every choice about an
open element is made in the Results.

\subsection{The data-generating process}\label{the-data-generating-process}

Participant \(i\) is observed at a baseline visit \(t = 0\) and at
post-baseline visits \(t = 1, \ldots, T\), at calendar weeks \(w_t\) (4,
8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 16 and 20 in the Hybrid design). The symptom score
is

\[Y_{it} = \mathrm{BL}_i - \left(TV_{it} + PB_{it} + BR_{it}\right),\]

so a larger response lowers the score. Each component has a mean given
by the modified Gompertz curve

\[G(x;\, m, d, r) = m\,\frac{\exp(-d e^{-rx}) - e^{-d}}{1 - e^{-d}},\]

so that \(G(0) = 0\) and \(G(\infty) = m\). It is evaluated at a
component-specific clock:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The drug-response component (BR) at time on drug \(t_{od}\), the time
  since the current on-drug period began, with \(m = 10.99\), \(d = 5\),
  \(r = 0.42\) and SD 8. The curve reaches its inflection at 3.8 weeks,
  half its maximum at 4.7 weeks and 90\% at 9.2 weeks. When the drug is
  resumed, time on drug restarts at zero.
\item
  The placebo-response component (PB) at time in the trial, scaled by
  the expectancy \(\eta_t\) (1 in the open-label phase, 0.5 when
  blinded), with \(m = 6.51\), \(d = 5\), \(r = 0.35\) and SD \(10\eta_t\).
\item
  The natural-history component (TV) at time in the trial, with the
  same curve as PB and SD 10.
\end{itemize}

The biomarker has mean 124.3 and SD 15.4, and baseline severity has
mean 83.1 and SD 18.5. We call these the default values. They are the
values of Hendrickson et al. (\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{2}) (their code at commit
58b32a9, stored in the package as \texttt{extracted\_rp} and
\texttt{extracted\_bp}). Every simulation and closed-form calculation in this
paper uses them, including the simulations of the analysis G10 in the
planning section and the paired rerun of Appendix B.4. In those two,
the biomarker enters the analysis standardized to mean 0 and SD 1, so
their interaction coefficients are per SD of biomarker. The
standardization is linear and does not change the test.

The vector (biomarker, baseline, \(TV_{i\cdot}\), \(PB_{i\cdot}\),
\(BR_{i\cdot}\)) is multivariate normal. The biomarker's correlation
with \(BR_{it}\) is \(c_{bm}\,g_t\), where \(g_t\) is the correlation
profile, the fraction of \(c_{bm}\) present at visit \(t\). Its
correlations with baseline, \(TV\) and \(PB\) are zero (Aims). Baseline is
uncorrelated with the response components. Participants are assigned
to the design's paths in fixed numbers (18, 18, 17 and 17 in the Hybrid
design at \(N = 70\)).

\textbf{The open elements.} Five elements are decided in the Results, each
by comparing candidates:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Infeasible matrices} (Stage 1): when the correlation matrix is not
  positive definite, replace it with a nearby valid matrix, or refuse
  it.
\item
  \emph{The correlation profile off drug} (Stage 2): the constant rule sets
  \(g_t = 1\) wherever the drug-response mean is nonzero, so the
  correlation is constant after first exposure; the decaying rule sets
  \(g_t = 2^{-t_{sd}/t_{1/2}}\) off drug after exposure, with \(t_{sd}\)
  the time since discontinuation and \(t_{1/2}\) the carryover
  half-life.
\item
  \emph{The drug-response mean off drug} (Stage 3): the compounding rule
  decays each off-drug mean from the previous visit's,
  \(\mu_{BR,t} = \mu_{BR,t-1}\, 2^{-t_{sd,t}/t_{1/2}}\); the anchored
  rule decays it from the last on-drug mean.
\item
  \emph{The serial correlation within a component} (Stage 4): compound
  symmetry, the same correlation \(\rho_{cs}\) between any two visits,
  or AR(1) in calendar weeks, \(\rho^{\lvert w_t - w_s\rvert}\). We write
  \(\rho_{cs}\) to keep \(\rho\) for the AR(1) correlation per week.
\item
  \emph{The correlation between components} (Stage 5): \(c_1\) at the same
  visit and a constant \(c_\times\) otherwise; \(c_1\) at the same visit
  and \(c_\times\rho^{\lvert w_t - w_s\rvert}\) otherwise; or the
  separable form \(c_1\rho^{\lvert w_t - w_s\rvert}\) at every lag.
\end{enumerate}

The binary-indicator analysis is the linear mixed model

\[Y_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_B B_i + \beta_D D_{it} + \beta_T w_t
  + \beta_{bm:D} B_i D_{it} + u_i + \varepsilon_{it},\]

with a random intercept \(u_i\), the binary drug indicator \(D_{it}\), and
baseline included as a row with \(D_{i0} = 0\). The interaction
\(\beta_{bm:D}\) is tested with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom
(\citeproc{ref-kuznetsova2017}{46}).

\textbf{Two standard errors.} Every mixed model in this paper assumes a
covariance for each participant's outcomes, its working covariance. The
\emph{working-covariance standard error} is the model-based one, computed as
if that covariance were the true covariance of the outcomes. It is
correct when the working covariance is right and can be too small or
too large when it is not. The \emph{cluster-robust standard error} treats
each participant as a cluster and estimates the variance of the fixed
effects from the residuals (the sandwich estimator), so it remains
valid when the working covariance is wrong. We use its bias-reduced
form, CR2, with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom
(\citeproc{ref-bell2002}{47},\citeproc{ref-pustejovsky2018}{48}). The binary-indicator analysis uses the
working-covariance standard error.

\subsection{Estimands}\label{estimands}

The target is the on-drug biomarker moderation of the drug response.
This is the change in the drug effect per unit of biomarker while the
drug is fully present. Without carryover it equals
\(\beta_{bm:D} = -c_{bm}\sigma_{BR}/\sigma_{bm}\).

Analyses that contrast on-drug with off-drug visits through a binary
drug indicator estimate this quantity only when no moderation remains
off drug. Under carryover they estimate an attenuated on-minus-off
contrast. Analyses with an exposure-decayed indicator \(D_{bc,it}\) at
the true half-life recover the on-drug moderation, because the
decaying rule and \(D_{bc,it}\) share one exponential decay. Under
the Weibull decay option, or with a correlation half-life that differs
from the carryover half-life, they do not. At another analysis
half-life, fixed or chosen from the data, they recover it only
approximately. Table
\ref{tab:estimands} states which analysis in this paper targets which
quantity. The three sets of results are compared only where their
targets coincide (no carryover) or where the attenuation is the object
of study (Stage 2).

\subsection{The paired-difference statistic}\label{the-paired-difference-statistic}

To isolate how carryover acts, we use a summary statistic whose
distribution can be derived exactly (Appendix A). For each participant,

\[\Delta_i = \bar Y_i^{\text{on}} - \bar Y_i^{\text{off}}
  = \sum_t a_t Y_{it},\]

the difference between the mean on-drug and mean off-drug scores. It
is regressed on \(B_i\) by least squares. We call it E9 when it uses
post-baseline visits only; E9 is the statistic used throughout. A
variant, E9b, counts baseline as one more off-drug visit, as the
binary-indicator analysis does. It is used only to explain the
results of the binary-indicator analysis (section ``Differences from
the DGP of Hendrickson et al.''). In the Hybrid
design, Monte Carlo simulation of the binary-indicator mixed model shows that
E9b reproduces its mean estimates to within 0.004. It reproduces its
power closely (0.654 against 0.632 without carryover). In the
crossover design the closed form understates the mixed model's power
(0.645 against 0.708 without carryover, and 0.279 against 0.31 to 0.35
under the constant rule). The mixed model's time term uses the
between-path contrast that the paired difference discards. The
derivation gives three results.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Proposition 1.} The variance of \(\Delta\) does not depend on the
  carryover half-life. The half-life enters only through the response
  means and the correlation profile. Neither appears in the variance.
\item
  \textbf{Proposition 2.} The population slope is
  \[\beta_{bm:D}^{\text{E9}}
    = -c_{bm}\frac{\sigma_{BR}}{\sigma_{bm}}\left(1 - \bar g_{\text{off}}\right),
  \qquad
  \beta_{bm:D}^{\text{E9b}}
    = -c_{bm}\frac{\sigma_{BR}}{\sigma_{bm}}
      \left(1 - \frac{n_{\text{off}}}{n_{\text{off}} + 1}\,
      \bar g_{\text{off}}\right),\]
  where \(\bar g_{\text{off}}\) is the mean of \(g_t\) over the
  \(n_{\text{off}}\) post-baseline off-drug visits. The slopes are
  specific to each path. In the Hybrid design \(n_{\text{off}}\) is 3 in
  two paths and 4 in the other two. The pooled slope is their
  allocation-weighted average.
\item
  \textbf{Power} is a noncentral-\(t\) probability governed by the correlation
  \(r_{\Delta B}\) between \(\Delta\) and \(B\). For E9 that correlation is
  proportional to the on/off correlation difference
  \(\mathcal G = 1 - \bar g_{\text{off}}\).
\end{itemize}

So carryover can affect power only through the on/off correlation
difference. The propositions reduce the question of why power falls
to the question of what the correlation rule does to
\(\bar g_{\text{off}}\).

\subsection{The feasibility bound}\label{the-feasibility-bound}

Write the response block of the correlation matrix as \(M\) and the
biomarker's correlations with the response components as
\(c_{bm}\tilde g\), with \(\tilde g\) the correlation profile padded with
zeros at the \(TV\) and \(PB\) entries. The full matrix is positive
definite if and only if \(M\) is, and

\[c_{bm}^2\, \tilde g^\top M^{-1} \tilde g < 1 .\]

The left side is the squared multiple correlation of the biomarker on
the whole response trajectory. The largest feasible \(c_{bm}\), the
feasibility bound, is therefore
\(c_{bm}^{\ast} = (\tilde g^\top M^{-1} \tilde g)^{-1/2}\) (Appendix A.4).
It depends on the correlation structure, the design and the
correlation profile. It is a bound on the DGP, not on any analysis.

\textbf{Number of replicates.} Replicate counts were set by the precision
each comparison needs. In study 2, 1,000 null replicates give a
two-MCSE band of 0.036 to 0.064 for a single cell. Pooling the 11
null cells gives the band of 0.046 to 0.054 used in Stage 6. In
study 1, a cell is flagged when the simulated and closed-form values
differ by more than 2 MCSE. Chance alone produces this in about 4.6\%
of cells, or about 26 of the 566 cell-variants (three
designs, three correlation structures, two statistics);
16 were flagged. The cell-variants are not independent,
since each uses its own draws but the same designs.

\textbf{Random numbers.} Every cell, or chunk of a cell, sets its own seed
from a fixed rule. In study 1 the seed is a function of the cell's
parameters. Study 2 uses
\(20261007 + 1000 \times \text{cell} + \text{chunk}\). Results therefore
do not depend on the number of cores or the order in which chunks run.
All analyses in study 2 are fitted to the same trials. The comparisons
are therefore paired. R's default Mersenne-Twister generator was used
throughout.

\textbf{Failed fits.} In study 2, a fit that returned an error was recorded
as missing. In the reported run, failures numbered 0 to 10 per

first and second runs they numbered 0 to 12 and 0 to 7, and at most
0.6\% and 0.4\% in any cell. Study 1 does not fit models for the
paired-difference statistic, so it has no failures.

\textbf{Which form of the DGP each study used.} Study 1 and the closed-form
calculations build the cross-component correlation in one of two ways.
Where the separable structure is named, it is
\(c_1\rho^{\lvert w_t - w_s\rvert}\). Where compound symmetry is named,
it is \(\rho_{cs} = 0.8\) within components, with \(c_1 = 0.2\) at the same
visit and \(c_\times = 0.1\) otherwise between components. Study 2 was
run three times, with the separable form in every cell except the
compound-symmetry curve and an analysis half-life of 0.5 week. The
first run used fixed path allocation (18, 18, 17, 17) and the
compounding carryover rule on the mean. The second run used the
anchored carryover rule, the rule of the recommended DGP, and also
assigned each participant to a path at random in every trial.
Randomized allocation is a driver option, not part of the recommended
DGP as defined (Table \ref{tab:spec}). The third run is the
recommended DGP as defined: the anchored carryover rule with fixed
allocation. It differs from the first run only in the carryover rule
and from the second only in the allocation, so the two effects can be
separated. The tables in Stage 6 are from the third run.

\textbf{Software.} R 4.6.1, with lme4 2.0.1 and lmerTest 3.2.1 for the
binary-indicator mixed model of study 1, nlme 3.1-169, mmrm 0.3.17,
clubSandwich 0.7.0 and MASS 7.3-65. In study 2 the binary-indicator
analysis is fitted with \texttt{mmrm} and a compound-symmetry covariance with
Satterthwaite inference, not with \texttt{lmer}. The two coincide when the
within-person correlation is non-negative.

\textbf{Exploratory status.} The stress test is exploratory. Its cells and
analyses were written down as a working protocol (Additional file 2).
No analysis plan was fixed in advance, and the protocol was revised as
results came in. Cells and three analyses were added after the first
results. Failed fits were first counted as non-rejections and are now
excluded from the denominator, a change of at most 0.0025 in any rate.
The study also compared the analyses' power and selected one by a
decision rule. That selection was revised after results, is post hoc,
and is reported in Additional file 3, not here. Such post hoc changes
can favor one method in comparative simulation studies
(\citeproc{ref-pawel2024pitfalls}{51}), which is a further reason not to rank the
analyses here. This paper uses the stress test only for what it shows
about the DGP: what the optional features do to size and power.

\section{Results}\label{results}

The Results are organized as stages. Each stage takes one element of
the DGP. It states the candidate choices and the evidence. It ends
with the choice made and the requirement it rests on. Table \ref{tab:spec}, at
the end, collects the choices.

\subsection{Stage 1. Feasibility of the joint distribution}\label{stage-1.-feasibility-of-the-joint-distribution}

The first question is whether the joint distribution exists for the
effect sizes a study simulates. Under compound symmetry the
correlation matrix is positive definite only for \(c_{bm}\) below a
bound. Without carryover the bound is 0.256 in the Hybrid design. It
is 0.256 in the crossover and 0.262 in the
open-label-plus-blinded-discontinuation design. These values follow
from the feasibility bound with \(M\) the compound-symmetry response
block (\(\rho_{cs} = 0.8\), \(c_1 = 0.2\), \(c_\times = 0.1\)).

But why is the bound so low? It is the condition that the biomarker's
squared multiple correlation on the whole response trajectory stays
below one. Under compound symmetry every visit's drug response shares
the same person-level factor. The biomarker therefore cannot be
correlated with the on-drug visits alone. Through that factor it is
correlated with the off-drug visits as well. The bound is low for two
reasons. The correlation profile switches between visits that the
structure ties together. The drug-response component also keeps its
full variance at off-drug visits. Stages 4 and 5 show how far a
serial correlation that decays with lag raises the bound. Even then,
an effect size of 0.6 is infeasible in 75\% of the grid of Table
\ref{tab:nonpd} and in every Hybrid cell.

When a matrix is infeasible, a DGP can either repair it or refuse it.
A repair replaces it with a nearby valid matrix, for example Higham's
nearest positive semidefinite matrix (\citeproc{ref-higham1988}{52}) (Appendix B.2). A
repaired matrix simulates a different DGP from the one stated, and it
does not report which correlation changed. The recommended DGP
therefore stops and reports the cell as infeasible. The package
function \texttt{buildSigma()} does so by default (Appendix B.5). The choice
rests on requirement 1, a valid joint distribution. What the bound means in outcome
units, and what to do above it, are taken up in the planning section
and the Discussion.

\subsection{Stage 2. The decaying biomarker-response correlation}\label{stage-2.-the-decaying-biomarker-response-correlation}

This stage takes the biomarker-response correlation at off-drug
visits. There are two candidates: the constant rule and the decaying
rule (Methods).

\textbf{The constant rule.} Under the constant rule every off-drug visit in the Hybrid design
follows an exposure. With any positive half-life its drug-response
mean is positive, so \(g_t = 1\). Hence \(\bar g_{\text{off}} = 1\) and,
by Proposition 2, \(\beta_{bm:D}^{\text{E9}} = 0\) for every
\(t_{1/2} > 0\).

Power is a step function of the half-life (Table
\ref{tab:closedform}, Figure \ref{fig:powerfig}). It takes its
no-carryover value at \(t_{1/2} = 0\). It equals the test size, 0.050,
at every positive half-life of practical interest, whether 0.1 week
or 10. At 0.1 week only 0.1\% of the drug effect remains a week after
stopping, yet the off-drug correlation is the full on-drug value.
(Below about 0.007 week the computed drug-response mean underflows to
zero in double-precision arithmetic, and the rule then behaves as if
there were no carryover. This is an artifact of the computation, not a
property of the DGP.) With a constant correlation, the biomarker
moderates the on-drug and post-baseline off-drug responses equally. No
interaction remains among the post-baseline visits, and E9 power is
the test size. The loss of power under small carryover is therefore a
property of the correlation rule, not of carryover or of the designs.

\textbf{The decaying rule.} The alternative is a correlation that decays
after discontinuation at the carryover half-life: \(g_t = 1\) on drug, \(2^{-t_{sd,t}/t_{1/2}}\)
off drug after exposure, and 0 before any exposure. This is the
pharmacological reading of a predictive biomarker. The biomarker
predicts the size of the drug effect, so it cannot predict a drug
effect that is no longer present. Under the decaying rule the mean of
\(g_t\) over the off-drug visits is

\[\bar g_{\text{off}}(t_{1/2}) = \frac{1}{n_{\text{off}}}
  \sum_{t\,\text{off}} 2^{-t_{sd,t}/t_{1/2}},\]

which is strictly increasing in \(t_{1/2}\), because every off-drug visit
has \(t_{sd} \ge 1\) week. Two consequences follow.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Power is strictly decreasing in the half-life} for the
  path-stratified paired-difference statistic. This holds on every
  path with at least one off-drug visit after an exposure (Proposition
  3, Appendix A.3). The placebo-first crossover path, whose off-drug
  visits all precede exposure, has constant power. For the pooled
  statistic power is non-increasing at every step of a 120-point grid
  from 0.03 to 4 weeks.
\item
  \textbf{The loss vanishes faster than any power of the half-life as
  \(t_{1/2} \to 0\).} At 0.1 week, \(\bar g_{\text{off}} \le 2^{-10}\)
  and power is unchanged to within 0.001.
\end{itemize}

The constant rule is the limit of the decaying rule as
\(t_{1/2} \to \infty\), applied at every positive half-life.

Power depends on the correlation profile only through the on/off
correlation difference \(\mathcal G = 1 - \bar g_{\text{off}}\) (Figure
\ref{fig:gapfig}). The difference is 1 without carryover. Under the
decaying rule in the Hybrid design it is 0.9997 at 0.1 week, 0.991 at
0.2, 0.906 at 0.5, 0.754 at 1 and 0.551 at 2. Under the constant rule
it is 0 at every positive half-life. The noncentrality is proportional
to \(r_{\Delta B}/\sqrt{1 - r_{\Delta B}^2}\), and \(r_{\Delta B}\) is
proportional to the difference. For small \(r_{\Delta B}\) the sample
size needed for a given power therefore scales approximately as
\(1/\mathcal G^2\). At 1 week this is about 1.8 times the no-carryover
sample. At 2 weeks it is about 3.3 times.

The recommended DGP uses the decaying biomarker-response correlation.
The choice rests on
requirement 2, an interaction that survives carryover. Pharmacological
plausibility supports it: a biomarker that predicts the size of the
drug effect cannot predict an effect that is no longer present.
Two limitations of the choice are stated here. First, at the
pharmacological half-lives of 0.1 to 0.2 week the on/off correlation
difference is within 0.01 of 1. The decaying rule therefore returns the
no-carryover result. The recommended DGP at its default half-life is not
a test bed for carryover. The smooth decline is a statement about
half-lives of 0.5 week and above, which we label behavioral. Second,
the decaying rule is asymmetric. Off drug the moderation fades
with the drug effect. On drug it is the full \(c_{bm}\sigma_{BR}b_i\) at
every visit, including the first weeks of titration when the Gompertz
mean is a small fraction of its maximum. A correlation proportional to
the current drug-response mean at every visit, or a correlation
half-life separate from the mean's, would remove the asymmetry.
Neither was tried. The sensitivity model of the Discussion is the form
that addresses it. A companion paper takes up the mixed-model power of
the recommended DGP at half-lives of 0.5 to 2 weeks
(\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper02}{50}). There the analysis half-life is either fixed or
chosen from the data.

\subsection{Stage 3. Carryover on the mean}\label{stage-3.-carryover-on-the-mean}

The compounding carryover rule decays the previous visit's mean by the
total time since discontinuation. At the second and later off-drug
visits that mean has already been decayed, so the decay compounds.
Consider two off-drug visits at weeks 11 and 12 following
discontinuation at week 10. The compounding rule decays the week-10 value
by \(2^{-1/t_{1/2}}\) and then by \(2^{-2/t_{1/2}}\), a total exponent of
3 weeks for 2 weeks elapsed. We treat the two forms as two
specifications.

The anchored rule decays the mean from the last on-drug value,
\(\mu_{BR,\text{last}}\,2^{-t_{sd}/t_{1/2}}\). The mean at any off-drug
visit then depends only on the elapsed time, and the half-life means
what it says (Appendix B.3).

The choice changes no slope, variance or feasibility bound. It changes
only the path means. It therefore changes the closed-form power of
study 1 by at most 0.002 in the Hybrid design. With the
binary-indicator analysis it changes simulated power by at most 0.006.
Figure \ref{fig:trajfig} shows the drug-response mean by visit for
the four Hybrid paths under both forms. Two features are visible. At
\(t_{1/2} = 1\) week the compounding rule lies below the anchored rule
at the second off-drug visit, as expected. At 0.2 week both are indistinguishable from zero
one week after discontinuation. At re-exposure (week 16 in paths 1 and
3) time on drug restarts at zero. The mean then rises along a fresh
Gompertz curve to 4.3 points at the week-16 visit, four weeks into the
restarted curve. The residual from the earlier exposure has decayed to
0.16 points by then even at a 1-week half-life. No downward step
therefore appears on the Hybrid schedule. A step would appear only if
a visit fell within about a week of re-exposure at a half-life of a
week or more.

Filled points: on-drug visits; open points: off-drug visits. D: drug; P: placebo.}\label{fig:trajfig}

The recommended DGP decays carryover from the last on-drug mean. That
choice rests on requirement 2: under the anchored rule the carryover
half-life means the same thing for the mean as for the correlation,
whereas the compounding rule decays faster than its stated half-life.
Time on drug restarts at zero at re-exposure, which for prazosin
matches re-titration after an interruption. That choice rests on
pharmacological plausibility, as a judgment. For drugs without
titration the response should instead rebuild from its residual level.
That is an option, not the default. The numerical consequence of
either choice is at most 0.006 in power.

\subsection{Stage 4. Serial (AR(1)) correlation}\label{stage-4.-serial-ar1-correlation}

Under compound symmetry, visits one week apart and sixteen weeks apart
have the same correlation, \(\rho_{cs}\). The one PTSD
symptom series available to us, the total CAPS score in CSP 563
(planning section), has a correlation that decays slowly with lag.
Matching its 4- and 20-week correlations within this DGP's structure
would need \(\rho\) of about 0.87 to 0.91 per week (Additional file 1). That
series is a total score, so it constrains the component-level \(\rho\)
without determining it. Both compound symmetry
and AR(1) are used in N-of-1 simulation (\citeproc{ref-chen2014}{18}). Serial
correlation has been discussed as a design consideration for
personalized studies (\citeproc{ref-schork2022}{53}). Two elements are open: the form of
the within-component correlation, and its value.

The recommended DGP uses AR(1) in calendar weeks,
\(\rho^{\lvert w_t - w_s\rvert}\), with one \(\rho\) for all three
components. The value of \(\rho\) is a parameter. We take \(\rho = 0.7\)
as a convention only, not an estimate. Under the adopted separable
form an effect size of 0.3 is already feasible at
\(\rho = 0.8\) (bound 0.440), so feasibility at 0.3 does not determine
the value. Of the four values in Table \ref{tab:rhostage}, 0.7 has
the largest separable feasibility bound (0.474). That keeps the
planning effect sizes feasible but says nothing about whether 0.7 is
right, and the value lies below the range the CSP 563 series suggests. Above 0.7, a larger \(\rho\) lowers the feasibility bound and
raises E9 power (Table \ref{tab:rhostage}, 0.7 against 0.8). If that
continues into the range CSP 563 suggests, the planning results at
\(\rho = 0.7\) overstate the largest feasible effect and the sample size
needed. Table \ref{tab:rhobound} extends the separable form to
\(\rho\) from 0.4 to 0.95. The bound is largest at \(\rho = 0.7\). It
falls slowly below that value, to 0.437 at 0.4, and
steeply above it, to 0.340 at 0.9 and
0.247 at 0.95, where the Hybrid design is the
binding one. Above 0.7 the E9 sample size in the Hybrid design falls
as well.
The planning section repeats its calculations at \(\rho = 0.5\) and 0.9.

Table \ref{tab:rhostage} shows what \(\rho\) does under compound
symmetry and under the structure adopted in Stage 5, for
\(\rho \in \{0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8\}\). Under compound symmetry alone,
lowering \(\rho_{cs}\) from 0.8 to 0.7 raises the feasibility bound from
0.256 to 0.346. An effect size of 0.3 then becomes feasible
by the change of \(\rho\) alone, with no change of structure. The same
change lowers closed-form power at \(c_{bm} = 0.25\) from 0.721 to
0.628. A lower serial correlation leaves more within-person noise in
the on-minus-off contrast. Feasibility and power pull in opposite
directions. The feasible range widens as \(\rho\) falls, and the power
at a matched \(c_{bm}\) falls.

\subsection{Stage 5. The separable cross-component structure}\label{stage-5.-the-separable-cross-component-structure}

Under compound symmetry the cross-component correlation is
\(c_1 = 0.2\) at the same visit and \(c_\times = 0.1\) at every other lag.
With AR(1) within components, the direct translation keeps \(c_1\)
at lag zero and lets the other-visit correlation decay,
\(c_\times\rho^{\lvert w_t - w_s\rvert}\). This two-constant form is what
the package's \texttt{buildSigma()} builds when its two constants differ
(Appendix B.5). It is not the only choice, and it has a validity
condition.

The recommended DGP uses the separable form,
\(c_1\rho^{\lvert w_t - w_s\rvert}\) at every lag including zero. The
response block is then a Kronecker product \(M = K \otimes A\) of a
\(3 \times 3\) component correlation \(K\) (1 on the diagonal, \(c_1\) off
it) and one AR(1) kernel \(A_{ts} = \rho^{\lvert w_t - w_s\rvert}\). In
words, every component has the same first-order autoregressive
correlation, and the between-component correlation is constant at
every lag. It is the two-constant form with \(c_\times = c_1\), with the
default \(c_1 = 0.2\). Four properties follow
(Appendix A.5).

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Validity for every schedule.} The eigenvalues of \(M\) are products
  of those of \(K\) and \(A\), so \(M\) is positive definite for any visit
  times. The two-constant form adds a same-visit cross-component term
  that carries correlation without variance. It is positive definite
  only when the smallest eigenvalue of \(A\) exceeds
  \((c_1 - c_\times)/(1 - c_\times) = 0.111\). On the paper's schedules
  the condition holds (smallest eigenvalue 0.122 in the Hybrid and
  OL+BDC designs at \(\rho = 0.8\), 0.193 at 0.7; 0.28 and 0.43 in the
  crossover design). On 20 weekly visits at \(\rho = 0.8\) it holds by
  less than 0.001. On daily measurement over eight weeks it fails
  (0.016 at \(\rho = 0.8\), 0.026 at 0.7). The two-constant form
  therefore cannot be used for the dense schedules that wearable or
  diary outcomes produce.
\item
  \textbf{The compound-symmetry structure is already of this kind in
  pieces.} It is the sum of a person-level and an occasion-level
  separable term, each valid. This is why its matrices fail only
  through the biomarker row, never in the response block.
\item
  \textbf{An exact, interpretable feasibility bound.} The bound factors
  into a component term and a schedule term,
  \[c_{bm}^{*} = \sqrt{1 - R^2}\;\bigl(g^\top A^{-1} g\bigr)^{-1/2},
  \qquad R^2 = \frac{2c_1^2}{1 + c_1},\]
  where \(R^2\) is the squared multiple correlation of the drug response
  on the other two components at one visit. The schedule term is a
  sum of per-step terms of the correlation profile. A profile that
  switches abruptly between closely spaced visits has a large
  per-step term. This is why designs with many on-off switches can
  carry only small effects.
\item
  \textbf{It nearly matches the analysis model.} Under separability the
  summed response has covariance \((s_t^\top K s_s)\,\rho^{\lvert w_t
  - w_s\rvert}\), with \(s_t\) the component SDs at visit \(t\) (Appendix
  A.5). With constant SDs this is exactly AR(1), the residual
  structure \texttt{corCAR1} assumes. In the Hybrid design the
  placebo-response SD is \(10\eta_t\), so it halves at blinding. The
  summed-response variance is then 368 at open-label visits and 257
  at blinded visits. The correlation between an open-label and a
  blinded visit is the AR(1) value times 0.976. The summed response is
  therefore AR(1) only within a phase. Under the two-constant form it
  is, in addition, AR(1) plus a white-noise term the analysis model
  omits. These statements follow from the algebra. Their effect on
  test calibration has not been measured separately.
\end{enumerate}

Table \ref{tab:ceilings} gives the feasibility bounds. At
\(\rho = 0.8\) the two-constant form raises the bound only to
0.291. A single same-visit correlation repeated across visits that
share little demands more of the biomarker row. In the open-label
design, where every visit is on drug, it lowers the bound. The
separable form raises the bound to 0.440. At \(\rho = 0.7\) the two
forms are close, 0.452 against 0.474. At the recommended \(\rho\) the
separable form's gain in feasible range is therefore small, and its
reasons are properties 1 and 4. The bound of 0.474 is attained in the
OL+BDC design (0.480 in the Hybrid design). A correlation of 0.45 is
feasible in every design without carryover. Effects as large as
0.6 exceed every feasibility bound in the Hybrid design.

The wider feasible range has a consequence that does not depend on
the form. At the same \(c_{bm}\), AR(1) data carry less information
about the interaction than compound-symmetric data. Take the
closed-form power of the paired-difference statistic in the Hybrid
design at \(c_{bm} = 0.25\). It is 0.721 under compound symmetry,
0.335 under the separable form at \(\rho = 0.8\) and 0.290 at
\(\rho = 0.7\). A
larger feasible correlation does not by itself mean more power. Effect
sizes under different structures should therefore be compared on the
scale of the implied slope, not of \(c_{bm}\).

\subsection{Stage 6. Optional features}\label{stage-6.-optional-features}

The recommended DGP has three optional features: phase-specific
measurement error, a Weibull decay form with the same half-life, and
monotone dropout. Study 2 fitted ten reported analyses
to trials with and without each feature. This stage reports what the
features did to size and power. The biomarker is not prognostic in
any cell reported here (Aims). The study's cells with a prognostic
biomarker are reported in the companion paper (\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper15}{45}).

\textbf{Size.} We pooled the 11 null cells
(11,000 replicates). The nominal band is 0.05 plus or minus two Monte
Carlo SE, \(2\sqrt{0.05 \times 0.95 / 11{,}000} = 0.004\), that is
0.046 to 0.054. The binary-indicator
analysis rejected 0.069, and
0.077 at \(N = 35\); both use the
working-covariance standard error. The unstructured model, with the
working-covariance standard error and Kenward-Roger inference
(\citeproc{ref-kenward1997}{54}), rejected
0.055, with 0.084 at
\(N = 35\). Random-intercept models with continuous-time AR(1) residuals
and the cluster-robust standard error (CR2) (\citeproc{ref-bell2002}{47},\citeproc{ref-pustejovsky2018}{48}), at the fixed analysis half-life, rejected
0.048 to 0.049. No single
cell of these models exceeded 0.067. With the
half-life chosen by AIC, the CAR(1) model rejected
0.053 and the expectancy-term model
0.056. The latter's largest single cell was
0.071, under
\(t_{1/2} = 1\) week. Of the analyses in this
paragraph, the binary-indicator analysis, the unstructured model and the expectancy-term model with AIC-chosen half-life
lie above the pooled band. Single-cell flags should be judged against
the number of comparisons. With 10 reported analyses and 11
null cells, about
5 analysis-cell pairs would
fall outside a two-MCSE band by chance at nominal size. The pairs are
not independent, since all analyses share the same trials. A single
exceedance is therefore weak evidence on its own.

\textbf{The options.} Table \ref{tab:options} shows what each optional
feature did to size and power. It covers three analyses: the
binary-indicator analysis with the working-covariance standard error,
the random-intercept CAR(1) model with the cluster-robust standard

biomarker-by-expectancy term to the mean model. Measurement

20\% dependent on the last score lowered power by 0.04 to 0.06. A Weibull
decay with the same half-life lowered power by 0.09 to 0.10 at shape 0.5
(a steep early decay) and raised power by 0.05 to 0.09 at shape 2. The true
half-life itself moved power from 0.50 to 0.57 at
zero to 0.35 to 0.42 at one week. Halving \(N\) gave
power of 0.21 to 0.27, against
0.40 to 0.49 in the base cell. At \(N = 150\) the
CAR(1) model reached 0.79. In these
null cells the CAR(1) and expectancy-term models rejected at most
0.067, against a single-cell band of
0.036 to 0.064 (the maximum, for the expectancy-term model under the base cell, lies 0.003 above the upper edge).
The binary-indicator analysis rejected up to 0.078, under
dropout completely at random. The features change power, which the planning section
and the companion paper address.

\subsection{Using the recommended DGP to plan a trial}\label{using-the-recommended-dgp-to-plan-a-trial}

\textbf{What \(c_{bm}\) means.} Conditional on the biomarker, the correlation
encoding shifts the on-drug drug-response mean by \(c_{bm}\sigma_{BR}\)
per standard deviation of biomarker (Discussion). With the default
\(\sigma_{BR} = 8\), \(c_{bm} = 0.25\) is a slope of 2.0 symptom points
per SD of biomarker, 0.35 is 2.8 points, 0.45 is 3.6 points, and the
feasibility bound of 0.474 is 3.8 points. The biomarker's default SD is
15.4 mm Hg, so 0.25 is 0.13 points per mm Hg.
The association first reported for prazosin is 14 points on the
Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale per 10 mm Hg (\citeproc{ref-raskind2016bp}{11}), or
21.5 points per SD of biomarker. Representing that slope under the
correlation encoding would need \(\sigma_{BR} \ge 45\) at the bound.
That is larger than the SD of the symptom score itself (18.5 at
baseline at the default values). At the default values the bound is
therefore below the reported association. The comparison is
approximate for two reasons. The observed slope is on the total
symptom change, not the drug-response component. And
\(\sigma_{BR} = 8\) is a default value, not an estimate.

That association did not replicate in our reanalysis of CSP 563
(Additional file 1). The interaction was +0.25 points per 10 mm Hg (95\% CI
\(-2.72\) to 3.21). With \(\sigma_{BR} = 8\) and the trial's biomarker SD
of 14.2 mm Hg, this interval corresponds to \(c_{bm}\) from \(-0.57\) to
0.48. The values of \(c_{bm}\) used in this paper, 0.25 to 0.45, imply
1.4 to 2.5 points per 10 mm Hg. They lie inside that interval. CSP 563
therefore neither supports nor excludes them, and it gives no reason
to plan for a slope above the feasibility bound. A study that does
need a larger slope should use the sensitivity model of the
Discussion once it is implemented, or raise \(\sigma_{BR}\) with a
stated reason.

\textbf{What CSP 563 identifies.} The reanalysis estimates some quantities
of the DGP on the total symptom score, all as computed in Additional file 1
and not re-estimated here. The baseline SD is 16.0 (DGP: 18.5) and
the biomarker SD 14.2 mm Hg (DGP: 15.4). The biomarker is not
prognostic for baseline severity (\(r = 0.02\), 95\% CI \(-0.09\) to
0.13), which supports the assumption of a zero prognostic correlation
with baseline (Aims). The
placebo-arm improvement has the DGP's time course and is about 1.8
times its size. The SD of change from baseline grows from 17 to 24
points over 26 weeks, where the DGP holds it at 16.0. The total-score
correlation decays by about 0.975 per week with about 10 percent
measurement noise. The DGP's total-score correlation falls faster at
short lags and then flattens. The trial does not identify the split
between the natural-history and placebo-response components, the
expectancy weight, the component-level correlations or the carryover
half-life. All participants were blinded throughout, and no one
stopped drug. A sensitivity arm of the DGP calibrated to these
estimates is future work. A working protocol for it exists in the
repository, and it has not been run.

\textbf{Sample size.} Table \ref{tab:planning} gives the total \(N\) for 80\%
power under the recommended DGP, by design, \(c_{bm}\) and half-life: in
closed form for the paired-difference statistic E9, and by simulation
for the recommended analysis G10 in the Hybrid design. At
the pharmacological half-life the Hybrid design needs about 280
participants at \(c_{bm} = 0.25\) and 84 at 0.45. At a 1-week half-life
those rise to 480 and 146.

\textbf{The recommended analysis at the planned sample sizes.} The
companion paper (\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper02}{50}) recommends the analysis G10 for
the interaction. G10 is a linear mixed model with a random intercept
and continuous-time AR(1) residual correlation. Its drug indicator is
the exposure-decayed \(D_{bc}\) at an analysis half-life chosen by AIC
over 0.25, 0.5, 1 and 2 weeks. The selection uses maximum-likelihood
fits of the model without the interaction, so the choice of half-life
does not depend on the term under test. The model at the chosen
half-life is refitted by REML with the interaction, and the
interaction is tested with CR2 cluster-robust standard errors and
Satterthwaite degrees of freedom. The grid of half-lives does not
include 0 or the pharmacological values of 0.1 to 0.2 week, so at
those true values G10 uses an analysis half-life of at least 0.25
week. The AIC-chosen analyses of the stress test also consider 0
(Table \ref{tab:estimands}). We simulated G10 under the recommended
DGP with the default values in every cell of Table
\ref{tab:planning}, at \(N = 70\) and at the E9 sample size for 80
percent power, with 1,000 replicates per cell. We also
simulated the null (\(c_{bm} = 0\)) at \(N = 70\) for each design and
half-life with 2,000 replicates. Table
\ref{tab:planningg10} gives the rejection rates at the 0.2- and 1-week
half-lives. Where the E9 sample size exceeded 400, or was above 800 and
not computed, the cell ran at \(N = 400\) and is marked.

Three results follow. First, G10 rejected the null at
0.046 to 0.053 across the nine design-by-half-life cells, including
the half-life of 0 not shown in the table (Monte
Carlo SE at most 0.005). Second, at \(N = 70\) G10 has
power more than two Monte Carlo SEs above that of E9 in 23 of the 27 cells, within two SEs in 4 and below in none. In the Hybrid design at \(c_{bm} = 0.25\) and the
0.2-week half-life, G10 power is 0.357
(MCSE 0.015) against
0.285 for E9. In the crossover design at the
same cell it is 0.243 (MCSE
0.014) against
0.202. Third, at the E9 sample size for
80 percent power, G10 power is 0.844 to 0.936 in the 20
uncapped cells, and 0.601 to 0.915 in the 7 cells that
ran at \(N = 400\). Under the recommended DGP at \(\rho = 0.7\) with the default values, the closed-form E9 rule is therefore conservative for G10. A trial sized by Table \ref{tab:planning} and analyzed with G10 had power of at least 0.844 in every uncapped cell, with the smallest margin in the crossover design.
Fits failed to converge in at most 0.008 of replicates in
any cell, and the rejection rates are computed over converged fits.

The same simulated trials were also analyzed with G9, which chooses
the half-life by AIC with the interaction in the model. In every cell
the G9 and G10 rejection rates differ by at most 0.040.

\textbf{The sample size for G10.} In the nine Hybrid cells we simulated G10
at 6 multiples of the E9 sample size, from 0.35 to 1.25
times, with 500 replicates at each. In each cell we fitted a
probit regression of rejection on \(\log N\). The G10 columns of Table
\ref{tab:planning} give the \(N\) at which the fitted power is 0.80. That \(N\) lies inside the simulated range in all 9 cells, and it is 0.71 to 0.83 times the E9 value. The E9 values are
closed form, so this comparison is the measured difference between
the paired-difference approximation and the analysis a trial would
use.

\textbf{Serial correlation.} The planning results above assume
\(\rho = 0.7\), a convention (Stage 4). We repeated the closed-form
calculation and the G10 simulation at \(\rho = 0.5\) and 0.9, with the
same seeds and replicate counts. At 0.5 the grid of \(c_{bm}\) is
unchanged, and one cell lies above its bound (OL+BDC at 0.45 without
carryover). At 0.9 the Hybrid bound falls to 0.340,
so the grid moves to 0.20, 0.25 and 0.30. Table
\ref{tab:planningrho} gives the cells at the 0.2-week half-life.
Below, ranges over all half-lives cover 0, 0.2 and 1 week.

In the Hybrid design the required sample size depends strongly on
\(\rho\) above 0.7 and little below it. At \(c_{bm} = 0.25\) the E9 rule
gives 314, 278
and 154 participants at \(\rho = 0.5\), 0.7
and 0.9. The crossover needs 1.5 times the Hybrid sample
at 0.7 and 2.6 times at 0.9. The likely reason is visit
spacing. The Hybrid design compares on- and off-drug visits one week
apart, where a high serial correlation cancels most of the shared
noise. The crossover compares two blocks of four visits 2.5 weeks
apart, ten weeks apart on average. G10 rejected
the null at 0.047 to 0.056 in the 18 design-by-half-life cells at the
two new values. At the E9 sample size its power was
0.815 to 0.867 at \(\rho = 0.5\) and 0.938 to 0.999 at 0.9, in
the uncapped cells. The E9 rule therefore stays conservative for G10,
and at \(\rho = 0.9\) it is very conservative. There G10 had power
0.751 in the Hybrid design at
\(c_{bm} = 0.25\) with only 70 participants. We repeated the G10
sample-size search in the Hybrid design (Table
\ref{tab:planningrho}). Over all three half-lives it gives 0.80 to 0.91 times the E9 value at \(\rho = 0.5\) and
0.45 to 0.57 times at 0.9, against 0.71 to 0.83 at 0.7.
At both new values the sample size lies inside the simulated range in every cell.

In the crossover design at \(\rho = 0.9\), 8 to 12 percent of G10
fits failed in each cell at \(N = 70\), and 13 to 19 percent
at the E9 sample sizes below 400. In the cells that ran at \(N = 400\)
at most 0.1 percent failed. In every other cell at the
two new values at most 2.6 percent failed. In the failed replicates we
refitted, the model chosen by AIC put the serial correlation at its
upper limit of 1 and the random-intercept SD near zero. A correlation
that does not decay cannot be told apart from a random intercept, and
the CR2 step then failed because the fitted covariance was singular.
The rates in Table \ref{tab:planningrho} are over the fits that
succeeded. A trialist who expects slow decay and plans a crossover
analyzed with G10 should expect such failures and specify a fallback
model in advance.

\textbf{When these sample sizes apply.} The sample sizes assume, as the
whole paper does, that the biomarker is not prognostic (Aims). G10
does not hold its level under every prognostic pathway. A trial in
which such a pathway is plausible should be planned for an analysis
that does, and its sample size is larger; the companion paper gives
both (\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper15}{45}).

  \emph{Default values:} the Gompertz parameters of the three components

  standard deviations; \(c_1 = 0.2\); the path allocation; the
  expectancy values.
\item
  \emph{Chosen here:} the restart of time on drug at re-exposure; the
  decaying biomarker-response correlation and the stop on infeasible
  matrices; carryover decaying from the last on-drug
  mean; AR(1) in weeks with \(\rho = 0.7\); the separable
  cross-component form; the symptom half-life range of 0.1 to 0.2
  week; the option defaults used in the stress test.
\item
  \emph{Uncalibrated:} every value above. None was re-estimated for this
  paper. CSP 563 estimates the baseline and biomarker SDs, the total
  placebo-arm improvement and the total-score covariance, and it does
  not identify the component split, the expectancy weight or the
  half-life (above). The values to which the results are most
  sensitive are \(\rho\) (Stage 4), \(\sigma_{BR}\) (which sets the slope a
  given \(c_{bm}\) implies) and the half-life.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Choosing \(c_{bm}\).} Take the smallest feasibility bound over the
designs and half-lives in the study (Table \ref{tab:ceilings} and
Appendix A.5; 0.474 for the three designs at \(\rho = 0.7\) without
carryover, higher with carryover). State \(c_{bm}\) as a slope in
outcome points per SD of biomarker. Keep it below that bound. The
package reports an infeasible value by stopping.

\subsection{Their code, as published and with the decaying rule}\label{their-code-as-published-and-with-the-decaying-rule}

We reran their code in two versions: as published, and with only the
correlation lines replaced by the decaying rule (Appendix B.1). Both
versions used their analysis, the binary-indicator mixed model fitted
with \texttt{lmer} and tested with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom, and the
same seeds. We used \(c_{bm} = 0.25\), below the compound-symmetry
feasibility bound of 0.256, so that no matrix would be repaired and
the correlation rule would be the only difference. Every replicate
returned an estimate and no matrix was repaired. The rerun had two
parts: (a) the Hybrid design at \(N = 70\) without censoring, at
\(t_{1/2} \in \{0, 0.1, 0.2, 1\}\), with 500 replicates per cell (Monte
Carlo SE of power at most 0.022) and one seed per half-life; and (b)
the four designs at \(N \in \{35, 70\}\) and \(t_{1/2} \in \{0, 0.1,
0.2\}\) under their censoring patterns, with 250 replicates per cell (at
most 0.032) and one seed per design, sample size and half-life.

\textbf{As published.} Power fell from 0.668 without carryover to 0.126 at
a half-life of 0.1 week (Table \ref{tab:patch}), the loss that
Hendrickson et al.~reported. At that half-life only 0.1\% of the drug
effect remains a week after stopping. This is the constant rule of
Stage 2, under which no interaction remains among the post-baseline
visits.

In our own implementation of their DGP, the mean estimate falls from
\(-0.126\) to between \(-0.026\) and \(-0.032\) at every positive
half-life, and power from 0.63 to between 0.08 and 0.11 (500
replicates per cell). Their code gives 0.126 at 0.1 week against
0.080 for our implementation. The difference is 2.4 combined Monte
Carlo SE (two-sided \(p = 0.016\)). The two implementations share the
parameters, the path allocation and the analysis model. They differ
in the random-number stream and in the carryover rule on the mean. At
0.1 week the carryover rule leaves an off-drug drug-response mean
below 0.011 points under either rule, so it cannot account for a
difference of this size. We therefore reran their code at 0.1 week
with 4,000 new replicates (16 seeds not used before). Power was
0.090 (MCSE 0.0045), within
0.8
combined Monte Carlo SE of our implementation. Rerunning the stored
cell with its stored seed reproduces 0.126 exactly. The value of 0.126
is therefore consistent with a high draw of a 500-replicate run. We
found no difference between the two implementations that would
explain it.

\textbf{Why their analysis keeps a little power.} Under the constant rule
the paired-difference statistic E9 has power equal to the test size
(Stage 2), but the binary-indicator analysis does not. It counts
baseline as an off-drug visit. The paired-difference statistic coded
the same way, E9b, shows what that adds. Every post-baseline score is
the baseline score minus the summed responses, so baseline cancels
from the contrast. Counting it as off drug only shrinks the off-drug
mean of the responses by \(n_{\text{off}}/(n_{\text{off}} + 1)\) toward
their baseline value of zero. At baseline the correlation is zero, so
under the constant rule

\[\beta_{bm:D}^{\text{E9b}} = -c_{bm}\frac{\sigma_{BR}}{\sigma_{bm}}\,
  \frac{1}{n_{\text{off}} + 1}
\qquad \text{for every } t_{1/2} > 0 ,\]

a quarter or a fifth of the no-carryover slope, with power 0.081
instead of 0.050. E9b's pooled slope, \(-0.029\), matches the
binary-indicator analysis's mean estimates of \(-0.026\) to \(-0.032\).
This residual compares the on-drug visits with the pre-treatment
visit. It is not an interaction during the trial.

\textbf{With the decaying rule.} Replacing only the correlation
lines and running their pipeline unchanged gave the results in Table

must. At 0.1 and 0.2 week the decaying version keeps the full

\subsection{Repaired matrices in their grid}\label{repaired-matrices-in-their-grid}

The nonzero effect sizes of their grid were 0.3 and 0.6, in the
open-label, open-label-plus-blinded-discontinuation, crossover and
Hybrid designs at \(t_{1/2} \in \{0, 0.1, 0.2\}\) week. Counting one
matrix per design path, effect size and half-life, 20 of its 54
matrices at those effect sizes (37\%) were replaced by nearby valid
matrices. The counts from our calculation reproduce those of their
code exactly. At \(c_{bm} = 0.3\) the repair changed no correlation by
more than 0.007. Results at that effect size are therefore essentially
those of the stated DGP. At \(c_{bm} = 0.6\) the repair lowered the
on-drug correlation to 0.54 to 0.57 and moved other correlations by up
to 0.10. Those results describe a different DGP. The constant rule
passes more cells at positive half-lives, because a constant
correlation places a small demand on the structure. It also carries no
interaction (Stage 2). The cells that need no repair under the
constant rule are therefore the cells in which the interaction is
absent.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

\subsection{The correlation encoding of the interaction}\label{the-correlation-encoding-of-the-interaction}

\textbf{Conditional structure.} Under joint normality, conditioning on the
biomarker shows what the correlation encoding does to the drug
response. With \(b_i\) the standardized biomarker,

\[E(BR_{it} \mid B_i) = \mu_{BR,t} + c_{bm}\, g_t\, \sigma_{BR}\, b_i,
\qquad
\mathrm{Cov}(BR_{i\cdot} \mid B_i) = \Sigma_{BR} - c_{bm}^2\sigma_{BR}^2\, g g^\top .\]

The encoding therefore shifts the conditional drug-response mean in
proportion to the correlation profile \(g_t\). It reduces the residual
drug-response covariance by a rank-one term. Two consequences follow.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{The power loss under carryover is set by the profile.} The size
  of the loss depends on whether the interaction fades with the drug
  effect, which is the correlation rule.
\item
  \textbf{The feasibility bound is the condition} that the reduced
  residual covariance remains positive definite.
\end{itemize}

The correlation encoding is therefore a parameterization of the
interaction as a correlation. It keeps the marginal variance of the
drug response fixed. Beyond its first use (\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{2}) we found
no use of it in the N-of-1 literature we searched (Background). Its strengths and its
limits both follow from it.

The correlation encoding has four strengths.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{One interpretable parameter.} The interaction strength is a
  single correlation. Its square, \(c_{bm}^2\), is the share of on-drug
  drug-response variance the biomarker explains, the systematic part of
  treatment-effect variation in the sense of Ding et al.
  (\citeproc{ref-ding2019decomposing}{55}).
\item
  \textbf{A reduced form of responder heterogeneity.} Suppose participants
  fall into two latent responder classes, and both the biomarker and
  the drug response differ between classes. Then class membership
  carries the covariance between them. Matching second moments gives
  \(c_{bm}^2 = f_B f_{BR}\), where \(f_B\) and \(f_{BR}\) are the fractions
  of the biomarker's and the drug response's variance that lie between
  classes (Appendix A.6). The encoding therefore matches the second
  moments of a responder-class mechanism without requiring the mixture
  to be identified. Identifying the mixture typically needs larger
  samples than N-of-1 programs enroll
  (\citeproc{ref-bauercurran2003}{56}--\citeproc{ref-nylund2007}{58}). The match
  is limited to second moments. The mixture's conditional mean is
  nonlinear in the biomarker, and its higher moments differ.
\item
  \textbf{The marginal variance of the drug response is fixed.} The
  biomarker explains part of the existing variation rather than
  adding to it. The effect size can therefore be stated as a share of
  response variance without changing the response distribution.
\item
  \textbf{It fits a biomarker that marks individual sensitivity to the
  drug} better than one that sets a fixed dose-response shift.
\end{itemize}

It also has four structural limits.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{The joint distribution must exist,} which bounds the effect size.
  In outcome units the bound is \(c_{bm}^{*}\sigma_{BR}\) per SD of
  biomarker, 3.8 points under the recommended DGP at the default
  \(\sigma_{BR}\). The planning section shows this is below the
  association first reported for prazosin, which did not replicate in
  CSP 563. The bound depends on the correlation structure
  and the design.
\item
  \textbf{The correlation must be specified at off-drug visits.} A constant
  correlation after first exposure removes the interaction under
  carryover. The specification adopted here is itself asymmetric
  between titration and washout (Stage 2).
\item
  \textbf{The drug-response component keeps its full variance at off-drug
  visits,} with zero mean. This adds noise to every on-off contrast.
  It belongs to the component decomposition, not to the correlation
  encoding.
\item
  \textbf{A correlation is less intuitive than a slope for clinical
  readers.} The slope it implies, \(-c_{bm}\sigma_{BR}/\sigma_{bm}\),
  must be derived before it can be compared with an observed effect.
  The comparison depends on \(\sigma_{BR}\), which is not estimated.
\end{itemize}

A model that generates the drug response from a participant-level
sensitivity to exposure would address these limits:

\[BR_{it} = \omega_i f(t) + \varepsilon_{it}, \qquad
\omega_i = \bar\omega\,(1 + \zeta b_i) + u_i ,\]

where \(f(t)\) is the exposure-response time course, \(\zeta\) the
biomarker's effect on sensitivity and \(u_i\) the sensitivity it does
not explain. This is a random-coefficient model with a
covariate-by-exposure interaction, the form used to describe
individual response in replicated crossover and N-of-1 trials
(\citeproc{ref-senn2016mastering}{6},\citeproc{ref-araujo2016}{27}).

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  It encodes the biomarker through individual sensitivity, so it has
  no feasibility bound.
\item
  Its moderation is proportional to the response at every visit, so it
  fades during titration as well as after discontinuation.
\item
  Its off-drug variance shrinks with the response.
\end{itemize}

It is not yet implemented in the software. Its response half-life for
prazosin in PTSD is not known. Until then, the correlation encoding's
range of use is effect sizes below the feasibility bound, which the
planning section translates into outcome units.

We do not claim that the correlation encoding is preferable to the
random-coefficient form. Whether the two give the same power,
feasibility and design ranking at a matched implied slope has not been
tested.

\subsection{What remains uncalibrated}\label{what-remains-uncalibrated}

The recommended DGP meets requirements 1 and 2 by construction and
derivation, and requirement 3 through its options. Two of its choices
rest on pharmacological judgment: the restart of time on drug at
re-exposure and the assumed half-life range. The separable form
matches the analysis model's residual structure only within each
phase, and the effect of the departure across phases on test
calibration has not been measured. The DGP is not calibrated. Every
parameter is a default value or a choice made here. The values that matter most are the serial correlation, the
drug-response SD and the symptom half-life. The parallel-group trial
CSP 563 informs only the first, and only on the total score. Its CAPS
correlation decays slowly, and the DGP's total-score correlation
decays faster at short lags (planning section). In the reanalysis
(Additional file 1), compound symmetry fit it worst among the structures
compared. All three values could be
estimated from an aggregated N-of-1 trial of prazosin with weekly
symptom measurement. Such a trial would also show which correlation
form fits weekly series.

\subsection{Recommendations}\label{recommendations}

For simulation studies of these designs, we recommend the following.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Let the biomarker-response correlation decay after
  discontinuation,} and stop on matrices that are not positive
  definite.
\item
  \textbf{State the choices:} carryover decaying from the last on-drug
  mean, AR(1) in weeks at a reported \(\rho\), and the separable
  cross-component form. Repeat the analysis at neighboring \(\rho\)
  where the conclusion could depend on it.
\item
  \textbf{Choose \(c_{bm}\) below the feasibility bound} for every design in
  the study. Report it on two scales: as \(c_{bm}\), and as the implied
  slope in outcome points per SD of biomarker.
\item
  \textbf{Use the sensitivity model, once available, for effects above the
  feasibility bound.} Until then, state the bound in outcome units
  as a limitation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}\label{limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{The closed form covers one statistic.} It is exact for the
  paired-difference statistic, not for the binary-indicator mixed model. The
  two agree closely in the Hybrid design. Under compound symmetry, in
  the crossover design, the closed form understates the binary-indicator mixed
  model. The sample sizes of the planning table are E9 values. The
  simulated G10 columns show the direction and size of the difference
  under the recommended DGP: at the E9
  sample size G10 power was 0.844 to 0.936 in the uncapped cells. The
  G10 sample size for 80 percent power was searched for in the Hybrid
  design only, by simulation at 6 values of \(N\) per cell. It
  carries Monte Carlo error that Table \ref{tab:planning} does not
  show.
\item
  \textbf{The planning results depend on \(\rho\).} The main tables use
  \(\rho = 0.7\). The one available PTSD series suggests a slower decay
  (Stage 4). At \(\rho = 0.9\) the Hybrid design needs about half the
  sample, the largest feasible effect is lower, and G10 fits fail
  often in the crossover design (Table \ref{tab:planningrho}).
\item
  \textbf{The biomarker is assumed not to be prognostic.} Every result
  assumes a biomarker correlated with the drug response only (Aims).
  CSP 563 supports the assumption for baseline severity but cannot
  test it for the placebo response or the natural history. Under a
  prognostic biomarker some analyses, including G10, lose their
  nominal size, and the planning sample sizes do not apply
  (\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper15}{45}).
\item
  \textbf{At pharmacological half-lives the recommended DGP shows no carryover
  effect.} The on/off correlation difference is within 0.01 of 1 at
  0.2 week. The DGP therefore tests carryover only at half-lives of
  0.5 week and above, which we cannot ground pharmacologically.
\item
  \textbf{The correlation is asymmetric between titration and washout.}
  Full moderation during the first weeks on drug is a modeling choice,
  not a consequence of the pharmacology.
\item
  \textbf{Parameter values are not estimated.} The Gompertz parameters,
  correlations and \(\sigma_{BR}\) are default values or chosen
  values. A reanalysis of CSP 563 estimates the
  total-score variance and correlation, which differ from the DGP's,
  and cannot identify the component split, the expectancy weight or
  the half-life. The motivating blood-pressure association did not
  replicate there.
\item
  \textbf{The DGP is Gaussian with a single biomarker.}
\item
  \textbf{The pharmacological carryover range} for prazosin at weekly
  visits is inferred from its plasma half-life, not measured as a
  symptom-response half-life.
\item
  \textbf{The stress test is exploratory.} Cells and analyses were added
  after first results.
\item
  \textbf{The literature search is incomplete.} We searched PubMed and
  Europe PMC, not Web of Science or Scopus, and the searches missed one
  relevant study that we found by other means (Background). A prior
  use of the correlation encoding may therefore exist.
\item
  \textbf{The correlation encoding is not compared with the alternative.}
  The random-coefficient form of the Discussion has no feasibility bound
  and no off-drug drug-response noise. We have not compared the two at
  a matched implied slope.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

We set out to move toward an optimal DGP for simulating
predictive-biomarker interactions in aggregated N-of-1 trials. We took
its elements one at a time, and three choices matter most. First, the
biomarker-response correlation must decay after discontinuation. If it
stays constant after first exposure, the interaction is absent among
the post-baseline visits under any carryover. Power then falls nearly
to the test size. With a decaying biomarker-response correlation,
power returns to its
no-carryover value at pharmacological half-lives (the on/off
correlation difference is within 0.01 of 1 at 0.2 week). It declines
smoothly at half-lives of 0.5 week and above. The decline is proved
monotone for the path-stratified paired-difference statistic and was
checked on a grid for the pooled statistic. Second, the serial
correlation structure sets the largest interaction the DGP can hold. A
separable AR(1) structure at a serial correlation of 0.7 allows a
biomarker-response correlation of 0.47. That is 3.8 symptom points per
SD of biomarker at the default drug-response SD. The value 0.7 is a
convention, however, and the one available PTSD series suggests a
slower decay. At 0.9 the bound falls to 0.340, and
the Hybrid design needs 154 participants at
\(c_{bm} = 0.25\) instead of 278. Third, a
matrix outside the bound should be refused rather than repaired.

The planning table gives the power at matched strength. It does so for
the paired-difference statistic in closed form and for the recommended
mixed-model analysis G10 by simulation. At the closed-form sample
size, G10 power was 0.844 to 0.936 in the uncapped cells. In the
Hybrid design the G10 sample size for 80 percent power was
0.71 to 0.83 times the closed-form
value.
We propose the recommended DGP as a reference for simulation studies
of these designs when the biomarker is predictive only. It has options
for measurement error, the decay form and dropout; the prognostic case
is the subject of a companion paper. Every parameter is
labeled as a default value, a choice or uncalibrated. Calibrating the
serial correlation, the drug-response SD and the symptom half-life is
the next step toward an optimal DGP.

  and the optional features.
\item
  \textbf{Default values} (\texttt{published} in code and data labels): the
  parameter values of Hendrickson et al.~(2020), used throughout
  (Methods).
\item
  \textbf{Correlation encoding of the interaction} (\texttt{mvn}): the predictive
  biomarker is a correlation between the biomarker and the drug
  response that is present on drug and absent off drug.
\item
  \textbf{Biomarker-response correlation} (\(c_{bm}\), \texttt{c.bm}): the
  correlation between the baseline biomarker and the drug-response
  component at an on-drug visit, which sets the strength of the
  interaction.
\item
  \textbf{Correlation profile} (\(g_t\)): the fraction of \(c_{bm}\) present at
  visit \(t\), equal to 1 on drug.
\item
  \textbf{Constant rule} (\texttt{step} in code and data labels): the
  biomarker-response correlation is \(c_{bm}\) at every visit where the
  drug-response mean is nonzero, and so is constant after first
  exposure.
\item
  \textbf{Decaying rule} (the decaying biomarker-response correlation;
  \texttt{graded} in code and data labels): the correlation is \(c_{bm}\) on drug,
  \(c_{bm}2^{-t_{sd}/t_{1/2}}\) off drug after exposure, and 0 before
  exposure.
\item
  \textbf{On/off correlation difference} (\(\mathcal G\)): the on-drug value
  of \(g_t\) minus the mean of \(g_t\) over the post-baseline off-drug
  visits, \(1 - \bar g_{\text{off}}\), through which alone the
  correlation rule affects closed-form power.
\item
  \textbf{Feasibility bound} (\(c_{bm}^{*}\), ``Ceiling'' in tables): the
  largest \(c_{bm}\) for which the correlation matrix is positive
  definite under a given correlation structure, design and
  correlation profile.
\item
  \textbf{Carryover half-life} (\(t_{1/2}\), \texttt{carryover\_t1half}): the time
  after discontinuation for the drug-response mean, and under the
  decaying rule the biomarker-response correlation, to fall by half.
\item
  \textbf{Pharmacological and behavioral half-lives}: half-lives of 0.1 to
  0.2 week are the assumed pharmacological range for prazosin, and
  half-lives of 0.5 week and above are labeled behavioral scenarios.
\item
  \textbf{Compounding carryover rule} (\texttt{recursive}): the drug-response mean at
  an off-drug visit is the previous visit's mean decayed by the time
  since discontinuation, so the decay compounds across visits.
\item
  \textbf{Anchored carryover rule} (\texttt{anchored}): the drug-response mean at
  an off-drug visit is the last on-drug mean decayed by the elapsed
  time since discontinuation.
\item
  \textbf{Drug-response component} (BR): the latent response driven by time
  on drug, with mean on a modified Gompertz curve.
\item
  \textbf{Placebo-response component} (PB): the latent response driven by
  time in the trial and scaled by the expectancy.
\item
  \textbf{Natural-history component} (TV): the latent response driven by
  time in the trial that does not depend on treatment.
\item
  \textbf{Baseline severity} (BL): the participant's symptom level before
  any response, which enters every visit equally.
\item
  \textbf{Expectancy} (\(\eta_t\)): the participant's probability of being on
  drug at visit \(t\) given the design, 1 in the open-label phase and
  0.5 when blinded.
\item
  \textbf{Compound symmetry} (\(\rho_{cs}\)): the same correlation between
  any two visits of a component, whatever their separation.
\item
  \textbf{Separable AR(1) structure}: the same first-order autoregressive
  correlation \(\rho^{\lvert w_t - w_s\rvert}\) for every component,
  with a constant between-component correlation \(c_1\) at every lag,
  so that the response block is a Kronecker product.
\item
  \textbf{Two-constant form}: an AR(1) structure in which the same-visit
  cross-component correlation \(c_1\) and the other-visit correlation
  \(c_\times\rho^{\lvert w_t - w_s\rvert}\) use different constants.
\item
  \textbf{Hybrid design} (\texttt{Hybrid}): open-label titration, blinded
  discontinuation, then a brief crossover, with visits at weeks 4, 8,
  9, 10, 11, 12, 16 and 20.
\item
  \textbf{OL+BDC} (\texttt{OLBDC}): open label followed by blinded
  discontinuation.
\item
  \textbf{CO}: the crossover design, in which participants receive drug and
  placebo in one of two orders.
\item
  \textbf{OL}: the open-label design, in which every visit is on drug.
\item
  \textbf{Path}: one sequence of drug and placebo phases within a design,
  to which participants are allocated in fixed numbers.
\item
  \textbf{Paired-difference statistic} (E9, E9b): the per-participant
  difference between the mean on-drug and mean off-drug scores,
  regressed on the biomarker, using post-baseline visits only (E9) or
  counting baseline as one more off-drug visit (E9b).
\item
  \textbf{Binary-indicator analysis}: the linear mixed model with a random
  intercept, a binary drug indicator and baseline included as an
  off-drug row (Methods). The companion paper calls it E0, because its
  own analysis G1 also uses a binary indicator.
\item
  \textbf{G10} (\texttt{E7null\_cr2} in data labels): the analysis the companion
  paper recommends. It is a random-intercept CAR(1) mixed model with
  the exposure-decayed drug indicator. The half-life is chosen by AIC
  over 0.25, 0.5, 1 and 2 weeks from maximum-likelihood fits without
  the interaction. The model is then refitted by REML with the
  interaction and tested with CR2 cluster-robust standard errors and
  Satterthwaite degrees of freedom.
\item
  \textbf{G9} (\texttt{E7cr2} in data labels): as G10, except that the half-life is
  chosen with the interaction in the model.
\item
  \textbf{Working-covariance standard error} (model-based): the standard
  error computed as if the analysis model's assumed covariance of each
  participant's outcomes were the true one. Correct only when that
  covariance is right.
\item
  \textbf{Cluster-robust standard error} (CR2, sandwich): the standard
  error estimated from the residuals with each participant as a
  cluster, valid when the assumed covariance is wrong; used here in its
  bias-reduced CR2 form with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom.
\item
  \textbf{Capped cell} (\(\dagger\) in Table \ref{tab:planningg10}): a cell
  whose E9 sample size for 80 percent power exceeds 400, simulated at
  \(N = 400\).
\item
  \textbf{Exposure-decayed drug indicator} (\(D_{bc}\)): a drug indicator
  equal to 1 on drug, \(2^{-t_{sd}/t_{1/2}}\) off drug after exposure,
  and 0 before exposure.
\item
  \textbf{Prognostic biomarker}: a biomarker associated with the outcome
  through a channel other than the drug response, such as baseline
  severity, the placebo response or the natural history. This paper
  assumes the biomarker is not prognostic.
\item
  \textbf{Expectancy-term model}: the random-intercept CAR(1) analysis that
  adds the expectancy and a biomarker-by-expectancy term to the mean
  model.
\item
  \textbf{CAR(1)}: the continuous-time first-order autoregressive residual
  correlation \texttt{corCAR1} of the analysis model.
\item
  \textbf{Stress test} (\texttt{11-strawman-stress-test.R}): study 2, the
  simulation that fits fourteen analyses to trials from the recommended
  DGP, with and without its optional features. Ten analyses are
  reported. Its cells with a prognostic biomarker are reported in the
  companion paper.
\item
  \textbf{Runs of the stress test} (\texttt{summary-full*.csv},
  \texttt{summary-full*-anchored.csv} and
  \texttt{summary-full*-anchored-fixed-ha0.5.csv}): the first run used the
  compounding carryover rule (\texttt{recursive}) and fixed allocation. The
  second used carryover decaying from the last on-drug mean
  (\texttt{anchored}) and randomized allocation. The third, reported in
  Stage 6, used the anchored carryover rule and fixed allocation, the
  recommended DGP as defined.
\item
  \textbf{The three requirements}: (1) a valid joint distribution: the
  correlation matrix is positive definite, ensured by the separable
  AR(1) structure and a biomarker-response correlation below the
  feasibility bound; (2) an interaction that survives carryover: it
  remains an on-minus-off contrast; (3) flexibility for stress
  conditions: dropout, measurement error and decay form as options.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{A.1 Moments of the paired-difference statistic}\label{a.1-moments-of-the-paired-difference-statistic}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{A.1 Moments of the paired-difference statistic}

Let \(a_t = 1/n_{\text{on}}\) at on-drug visits and \(-1/n_{\text{off}}\) at
off-drug visits, so that \(\Delta_i = \sum_t a_t Y_{it}\). Because the
weights sum to zero, the baseline level cancels and
\(\Delta = -\sum_t a_t S_t\), with \(S_t = TV_t + PB_t + BR_t\). In general
\(\mathrm{Var}(\Delta) = a^\top \Sigma_S a\), where \(\Sigma_S\) is the
covariance of the summed response, whatever the structure.

With \(m_1 = \sum a_t\), \(m_2 = \sum a_t^2\), \(m_\eta = \sum a_t \eta_t\),
\(m_{\eta\eta} = \sum a_t^2 \eta_t^2\) and \(m_{2\eta} = \sum a_t^2 \eta_t\),
with \(\eta_t\) the design expectancy, the variance under compound
symmetry with common correlation \(\rho_{cs}\) is

\[\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Var}(\Delta) = {} & (\sigma_{TV}^2 + \sigma_{BR}^2)
  \big[\rho_{cs} m_1^2 + (1 - \rho_{cs}) m_2\big]
+ \sigma_{PB}^2 \big[\rho_{cs} m_\eta^2 + (1 - \rho_{cs}) m_{\eta\eta}\big] \\
& + 2\sigma_{TV}\sigma_{BR}\big[c_\times m_1^2 + (c_1 - c_\times) m_2\big]
+ 2(\sigma_{TV} + \sigma_{BR})\sigma_{PB}
  \big[c_\times m_1 m_\eta + (c_1 - c_\times) m_{2\eta}\big],
\end{aligned}\]

where \(c_1\) and \(c_\times\) are the same-visit and other-visit
cross-component correlations. Under the separable form of Stage 5,
\(\mathrm{Cov}(S_t, S_s) = (s_t^\top K s_s) A_{ts}\) (A.5), and
\(\mathrm{Var}(\Delta) = \sum_{t,s} a_t a_s (s_t^\top K s_s) A_{ts}\), with
\(s_t\) the vector of component standard deviations at visit \(t\).

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Proposition 1.} No term depends on \(t_{1/2}\) under either
  structure, so \(\mathrm{Var}(\Delta)\) is free of the half-life. The
  argument applies to any correlation matrix in which the half-life
  does not enter the response block. It therefore holds for unrepaired
  matrices only. The repair changes the response block, and its
  changes depend on the half-life through the correlation profile. For
  E9 the weights sum to zero, so \(m_1 = 0\). For E9b the post-baseline
  weights sum to \(1/(n_{\text{off}} + 1)\), and the baseline row, with
  \(S_0 = 0\), carries the remaining weight.
\item
  \textbf{Proposition 2.} Only \(BR_t\) is correlated with \(B\), with covariance
  \(c_{bm} g_t\sigma_{bm}\sigma_{BR}\), so
  \(\beta_{bm:D} = -c_{bm}(\sigma_{BR}/\sigma_{bm})\sum_t a_t g_t\). With
  \(g_t = 1\) on drug, this gives the expressions in the Methods.
\item
  \textbf{Residual variance.} Under the correlation encoding,
  \(\sigma_\Delta^2 = \mathrm{Var}(\Delta) - \beta_{bm:D}^2\sigma_{bm}^2\).
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{A.2 Power}\label{a.2-power}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{A.2 Power}

Within a path of \(n\) participants, the least-squares slope is exactly
normal given the biomarker values. Its \(t\)-statistic is exactly
noncentral \(t\) with \(n - 2\) degrees of freedom and noncentrality
\(\beta_{bm:D}\sqrt{\mathrm{SS}_B}/\sigma_\Delta\), where
\(\mathrm{SS}_B \sim \sigma_{bm}^2\chi^2_{n-1}\).

At \(\mathrm{SS}_B = \sigma_{bm}^2(n - 1)\) the noncentrality is
\(\Lambda = \sqrt{n - 1}\,r_{\Delta B}/\sqrt{1 - r_{\Delta B}^2}\),
where \(r_{\Delta B} = \beta_{bm:D}\sigma_{bm}/\mathrm{SD}(\Delta)\) is
proportional to the on/off correlation difference. For small
\(r_{\Delta B}\) the noncentrality is approximately
\(\sqrt{n - 1}\,r_{\Delta B}\). The sample size for a fixed
noncentrality therefore scales as \(1/r_{\Delta B}^2\) and hence
approximately as \(1/\mathcal G^2\). The exact scaling uses
\(r/\sqrt{1 - r^2}\).

Pooling paths with a common intercept adds the between-path variation
in mean contrast to the residual variance. In the Hybrid design it is
under 0.7\% of the total. Power is the noncentral-\(t\) tail probability
averaged over the \(\chi^2\) law of \(\mathrm{SS}_B\). For the pooled
statistic this is an approximation. It treats the between-path
differences in mean and slope as extra residual variance and ignores
the differences in residual variance between paths. The per-path
result is exact.

\subsection*{A.3 Monotonicity under the decaying rule}\label{a.3-monotonicity-under-the-decaying-rule}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{A.3 Monotonicity under the decaying rule}

\[\frac{d\bar g_{\text{off}}}{dt_{1/2}}
  = \frac{\ln 2}{n_{\text{off}}\,t_{1/2}^2}
    \sum_{t\,\text{off}} t_{sd,t}\,2^{-t_{sd,t}/t_{1/2}} > 0\]

on any path with at least one off-drug visit after an exposure.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Proposition 3.} On such a path, by Proposition 2 the slope's
  magnitude is strictly decreasing in \(t_{1/2}\). By A.1,
  \(\sigma_\Delta^2\) is strictly increasing, since \(\mathrm{Var}(\Delta)\)
  is fixed. So the noncentrality and the power of the path-stratified
  statistic are strictly decreasing. On a path whose off-drug visits
  all precede first exposure (the placebo-first crossover path),
  \(\bar g_{\text{off}} = 0\) at every half-life and the power is constant.
  The pooled statistic's residual variance also contains the
  between-path variation of mean contrasts, which depends on the
  half-life through the carryover means. Its monotonicity was checked
  on a grid (Stage 2), not proved.
\item
  \textbf{Small half-lives.} \(\bar g_{\text{off}} \le 2^{-1/t_{1/2}}\), which
  gives the behavior near zero stated in the Results.
\item
  \textbf{Large half-lives.} \(\bar g_{\text{off}} \to 1\) as
  \(t_{1/2} \to \infty\), which recovers the constant rule.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{A.4 The feasibility bound}\label{a.4-the-feasibility-bound}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{A.4 The feasibility bound}

\textbf{The feasibility bound.} Order the variables as biomarker, then
responses, and partition the correlation matrix as
\(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & c_{bm}\tilde g^\top \\ c_{bm}\tilde g & M\end{pmatrix}\).
By the Schur complement, the matrix is positive definite if and only if
\(M\) is positive definite and \(1 - c_{bm}^2 \tilde g^\top M^{-1}\tilde g > 0\).
Hence the bound is \(c_{bm}^* = (\tilde g^\top M^{-1}\tilde g)^{-1/2}\).

\subsection*{A.5 The separable structure}\label{a.5-the-separable-structure}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{A.5 The separable structure}

Let \(A_{ts} = \rho^{\lvert w_t - w_s\rvert}\) and
\(K = (1 - c_1)I_3 + c_1 J_3\), and set \(M = K \otimes A\), so that the
correlation between component \(c\) at visit \(t\) and component \(c'\) at
visit \(s\) is \(K_{cc'}A_{ts}\).

\textbf{Validity.} The eigenvalues of \(M\) are the products of those of \(K\)
and \(A\).

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(K\) has eigenvalues \(1 + 2c_1\) and \(1 - c_1\) (twice), so it is
  positive definite for \(-\tfrac12 < c_1 < 1\).
\item
  \(A\) is the correlation matrix of a stationary Ornstein-Uhlenbeck
  process at the visit times, and is positive definite for any
  distinct times.
\end{itemize}

Hence \(M\) is positive definite for every schedule. Generatively,
\(\mathbf X = L_A\mathbf Z L_K^\top\) with \(L_AL_A^\top = A\) and
\(L_KL_K^\top = K\): each column of \(L_A\mathbf Z\) is an independent AR(1)
path, and the components are fixed mixtures of them.

\textbf{The feasibility bound.} With the biomarker correlated with the
drug-response component only, its correlation vector is
\(c_{bm}\tilde g\), with \(\tilde g = e_3 \otimes g\) and \(g\) the
correlation profile. Since \(M^{-1} = K^{-1} \otimes A^{-1}\),

\[\tilde g^\top M^{-1} \tilde g = [K^{-1}]_{33}\, g^\top A^{-1} g,
\qquad
[K^{-1}]_{33} = \frac{1 + c_1}{(1 - c_1)(1 + 2c_1)} = \frac{1}{1 - R^2}.\]

\textbf{The schedule term.} The sampled process is Markov, with
step correlation \(\rho_t = \rho^{w_t - w_{t-1}}\), so

\[g^\top A^{-1} g = g_1^2 + \sum_{t=2}^{n}
  \frac{(g_t - \rho_t g_{t-1})^2}{1 - \rho_t^2}.\]

For a binary correlation profile, each step from visit \(t-1\) to visit
\(t\) contributes the following term:

{\def\LTcaptype{none} % do not increment counter
\begin{longtable}[]{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule\noalign{}
Step & Term \\
\midrule\noalign{}
\endhead
\bottomrule\noalign{}
\endlastfoot
on to on & \((1 - \rho_t)/(1 + \rho_t)\) \\
on to off & \(\rho_t^2/(1 - \rho_t^2)\) \\
off to on & \(1/(1 - \rho_t^2)\) \\
off to off & 0 \\
\end{longtable}
}

Under the decaying rule, the off-drug correlation decays
instead of switching, which lowers the on-to-off term. This is why the
feasibility bound under the decaying rule rises with the
half-life. Under compound symmetry it rises from 0.256 without
carryover to 0.307 at 1 week and 0.369 at 2 weeks, which makes
\(c_{bm} = 0.3\) feasible from 1 week (Figure \ref{fig:fig4b}).

\textbf{The two-constant form.} With a common \(\rho\), setting the
same-visit cross-component correlation to \(c_1\) and the different-visit
correlation to \(c_\times A_{ts}\) gives

\[M = K_a \otimes A + K_b \otimes I_n, \qquad
K_a = (1 - c_\times)I_3 + c_\times J_3, \qquad
K_b = (c_1 - c_\times)(J_3 - I_3).\]

\(K_b\) has zero diagonal and so is indefinite. The eigenvalues of \(M\)
are \(\omega_j(A)(1 + 2c_\times) + 2(c_1 - c_\times)\) and
\(\omega_j(A)(1 - c_\times) - (c_1 - c_\times)\), where \(\omega_j(A)\) are
the eigenvalues of \(A\). Hence \(M\) is positive definite if and only if
\(\omega_{\min}(A) > (c_1 - c_\times)/(1 - c_\times)\). For visits at a
common spacing of \(s\) weeks, \(\omega_{\min}(A)\) decreases toward
\((1 - \rho^s)/(1 + \rho^s)\) as visits accumulate, which is 0.111 at
weekly spacing and \(\rho = 0.8\) (the threshold itself) and 0.016 at
daily spacing. The values for the paper's schedules are in Stage 5.

\textbf{The compound-symmetry structure.} The compound-symmetry form is
\(M = K_o \otimes I_n + K_p \otimes J_n\), with a person-level factor
\(K_p = \rho_{cs} I_3 + c_\times(J_3 - I_3)\) and an occasion-level factor
\(K_o = (1 - \rho_{cs})I_3 + (c_1 - c_\times)(J_3 - I_3)\). It is positive
definite if and only if \((1 - \rho_{cs}) - (c_1 - c_\times) > 0\), whatever
the schedule (0.10 at \(\rho_{cs} = 0.8\), \(c_1 = 0.2\) and
\(c_\times = 0.1\), which is also the smallest eigenvalue of the
eight-visit response block under those values).

\textbf{The summed response.} With component standard deviations \(s_t\) and
\(S_t = \sum_c s_{c,t}X_{c,t}\), separability gives

\[\mathrm{Cov}(S_t, S_s) = (s_t^\top K s_s)\,A_{ts},\]

proportional to the AR(1) kernel when the standard deviations are
constant. The correlation of the summed response is
\[\mathrm{Corr}(S_t, S_s) = q_{ts}\,A_{ts}, \qquad
q_{ts} = \frac{s_t^\top K s_s}
  {\sqrt{(s_t^\top K s_t)(s_s^\top K s_s)}} \le 1,\]
by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in the inner product defined by the
positive definite \(K\). Equality holds if and only if \(s_s\) is a
multiple of \(s_t\). In the Hybrid design
\(s_t = (\sigma_{TV}, \sigma_{PB}\eta_t, \sigma_{BR}) = (10, 10\eta_t, 8)\).
Within a phase \(\eta_t\) is constant, so \(q_{ts} = 1\) and the
summed response is exactly AR(1) there. Between an open-label visit
(\(\eta_t = 1\)) and a blinded visit (\(\eta_s = 0.5\)), with \(c_1 = 0.2\),
\(s_t^\top K s_t = 368\), \(s_s^\top K s_s = 257\), \(s_t^\top K s_s = 300\)
and \(q_{ts} = 0.976\). The summed response therefore has a
variance that changes at blinding and a cross-phase correlation 2.4\%
below the AR(1) value. A constant-variance \texttt{corCAR1} model omits both.
The analyses with phase-specific residual variances in the stress test
represent the first and not the second. The two-constant form adds
\((s_t^\top K_b s_t)\,\mathbf 1[t = s] > 0\), a white-noise term.

These results are derived in full, and checked numerically, in the
pmsimstats-ng documentation (Additional file 6).

\subsection*{\texorpdfstring{A.6 The responder-class reading of \(c_{bm}\)}{A.6 The responder-class reading of c\_\{bm\}}}\label{a.6-the-responder-class-reading-of-c_bm}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{A.6 The responder-class reading of \(c_{bm}\)}

Let \(Z_i \in \{0, 1\}\) be a latent responder class with
\(\Pr(Z_i = 1) = p\), and let \(B_i = \mu_B(Z_i) + e_{B,i}\) and
\(BR_i = \mu_{BR}(Z_i) + e_{BR,i}\) for the on-drug drug response, with
\(e_B\) and \(e_{BR}\) independent of each other and of \(Z\). Then
\(\mathrm{Cov}(B, BR) = \mathrm{Cov}(\mu_B(Z), \mu_{BR}(Z))\). Both
\(\mu_B(Z)\) and \(\mu_{BR}(Z)\) are affine in the binary \(Z\), so their
correlation is \(\pm 1\) and
\(\lvert\mathrm{Cov}(B, BR)\rvert = \mathrm{SD}(\mu_B(Z))\,\mathrm{SD}(\mu_{BR}(Z))\).
Dividing by \(\sigma_B\sigma_{BR}\),

\[c_{bm}^2 = \frac{\mathrm{Var}(\mu_B(Z))}{\sigma_B^2}\cdot
  \frac{\mathrm{Var}(\mu_{BR}(Z))}{\sigma_{BR}^2} = f_B\, f_{BR},\]

the product of the between-class variance fractions. With more than
two classes the correlation of \(\mu_B(Z)\) and \(\mu_{BR}(Z)\) is at most
1 in magnitude, so \(c_{bm}^2 \le f_B f_{BR}\).

The constant rule tests whether the off-drug drug-response mean is
nonzero. In double precision that mean underflows to zero at late
off-drug visits when the half-life is below about 0.007 week, or below
about 0.012 week under the compounding carryover rule. The constant rule
then sets \(g_t = 0\) at those visits. This is a property of the
floating-point arithmetic, not of the DGP. It is why Stage 2 describes
power under the constant rule as constant at every positive half-life
of practical interest.

\subsection*{B.2 Stop instead of repairing}\label{b.2-stop-instead-of-repairing}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{B.2 Stop instead of repairing}

The code of Hendrickson et al.~replaces a non-positive-definite
covariance matrix with a nearby valid one when its
\texttt{makePositiveDefinite} option is on, as it is in their driver (lines
145 to 150). It calls
\texttt{corpcor::make.positive.definite} with tolerance \(10^{-3}\). That
function follows Higham's nearest symmetric positive semidefinite
matrix (\citeproc{ref-higham1988}{52}). It raises every eigenvalue below
\(2 \times 10^{-3}\) to that value, so it changes the variances as well
as the correlations. No warning is issued. The replacement below stops
instead, so that an infeasible cell is reported rather than changed:

\begin{Shaded}
\begin{Highlighting}[]
  \CommentTok{\# published:}
  \CommentTok{\# if(makePositiveDefinite)\{}
  \CommentTok{\#   if(!is.positive.definite(sigma))\{}
  \CommentTok{\#     sigma\textless{}{-}make.positive.definite(sigma, tol=1e{-}3)}
  \CommentTok{\#   \}}
  \CommentTok{\# \}}
  \ControlFlowTok{if}\NormalTok{(}\SpecialCharTok{!}\NormalTok{corpcor}\SpecialCharTok{::}\FunctionTok{is.positive.definite}\NormalTok{(sigma))\{}
    \FunctionTok{stop}\NormalTok{(}\FunctionTok{sprintf}\NormalTok{(}\StringTok{\textquotesingle{}c.bm = \%.3f exceeds the feasible range for this design\textquotesingle{}}\NormalTok{,}
\NormalTok{                 modelparam}\SpecialCharTok{$}\NormalTok{c.bm))}
\NormalTok{  \}}
\end{Highlighting}
\end{Shaded}

One case is exempt from the stop. The placebo-response component's
standard deviation is proportional to the expectancy. A visit with
expectancy zero (a run-in or a baseline visit coded with no
expectancy) has exactly zero placebo-response variance. It makes
\(\Sigma\) singular by construction rather than by an infeasible
correlation. The package's stop path checks and factors only the
sub-block of positive-variance components and draws the zero-variance
components at their means. No correlation is modified. The
repair set those variances to \(10^{-3}\) instead.

A repair need not be the unstructured nearest matrix. A shrinking
repair can keep the response block fixed and change only the
biomarker row (\citeproc{ref-higham2016shrinking}{59}). Such a repair simulates a
smaller \(c_{bm}\) than the one requested. The same feasibility
question arises for target correlations in NORTA generation
(\citeproc{ref-ghosh2003norta}{32}). Correlation matrices built from partial
correlations are positive definite by construction
(\citeproc{ref-joe2006generating}{33}), but they do not fix the target correlations a
design requires. The stop is the simpler choice. It reports the
infeasibility to the user.

The case that fixed the default came from the companion carryover
study (\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper02}{50}). Two runs combined \(c_{bm} = 0.45\) with a
second correlation in the biomarker row that made the matrix
infeasible at any \(c_{bm}\). The repair lowered the on-drug
correlation, and the runs reported the attenuated interaction as a
loss of power. A repair can therefore turn an infeasible input into a
plausible but wrong finding. The case is documented in the companion
paper on prognostic biomarkers (\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper15}{45}).

\subsection*{B.3 Carryover decaying from the last on-drug mean}\label{b.3-carryover-decaying-from-the-last-on-drug-mean}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{B.3 Carryover decaying from the last on-drug mean}

The compounding carryover rule (lines 83 to 92) decays the previous
visit's mean by the total time since discontinuation. At the second
and later off-drug visits that mean has already been decayed, so the
decay compounds. The replacement decays from the last on-drug value.
Study 1 uses it at every positive half-life. Replacing it with the
compounding rule changes no slope, variance or feasibility bound, only
the path means. It therefore changes the study 1 closed-form power by
at most 0.002 in the Hybrid design. With the binary-indicator analysis
the change alone moves power by at most 0.006 (Additional file 7,
Section 8.3).
Combined with the restart of the Gompertz clock at re-exposure, it can
produce a downward step in the mean at the first re-exposure visit
when the residual exceeds the restarted curve's value. On the Hybrid
schedule it does not (Stage 3).

\subsection*{B.4 The exposure-decayed drug indicator in the analysis}\label{b.4-the-exposure-decayed-drug-indicator-in-the-analysis}

Analyses that use an exposure-decayed drug indicator must code
off-drug visits before first exposure as unexposed. The time since
discontinuation is zero there, and the decay function returns 1 at

\(t_{1/2} = 1\) week and 500 replicates, tested with the
working-covariance standard error

\subsection*{B.5 The recommended DGP in pmsimstats-ng}\label{b.5-the-recommended-dgp-in-pmsimstats-ng}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{B.5 The recommended DGP in pmsimstats-ng}

The package function \texttt{buildSigma()} (\texttt{R/generateData.R}) implements
the decaying rule, with \texttt{c.bm} on drug,
\texttt{c.bm\ *\ exp(-lambda\_cor\ *\ tsd)} off drug after exposure and zero
before exposure. It implements AR(1) within each component in
calendar weeks. It decays carryover on the drug-response mean from the
last on-drug value (not the constant rule of B.3). It also implements
prognostic pathways (\texttt{c.bm.pb}, \texttt{c.bm.bl}, \texttt{c.bm.tv}, \texttt{b.bm.tv}). They
are zero by default, which is the assumption of this paper, and are
described in the companion paper (\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper15}{45}).

\textbf{The cross-component block.} \texttt{buildSigma()} builds the
cross-component correlation from two constants: \texttt{c.cf1t} at the same
visit and \texttt{c.cfct\ *\ rho\^{}tg} at a lag of \texttt{tg} weeks. With
\texttt{c.cf1t\ =\ c.cfct\ =\ c\_1} this is exactly the separable form of Table
\ref{tab:spec}, \(c_1\rho^{\lvert w_t - w_s\rvert}\) at every lag, since
\(\rho^0 = 1\). With the two constants unequal it is the two-constant
form of Stage 5. The recommended DGP is therefore the package with both
constants set to 0.2, which is how the project's carryover driver
calls it (\texttt{analysis/scripts/carryover-sensitivity/simulation-core.R}).
Study 2's driver builds the separable block directly
(\texttt{C1\ *\ A} in \texttt{11-strawman-stress-test.R}).

\textbf{Allocation and carryover in study 2.} The driver takes
\texttt{-\/-allocation\ fixed\textbar{}random} and \texttt{-\/-carryover\ anchored\textbar{}recursive}. The
first run used fixed allocation (18, 18, 17, 17) and the compounding
carryover rule (\texttt{recursive}). The second run used carryover decaying
from the last on-drug mean (\texttt{anchored}) and randomized allocation. The
third run used the decaying rule and fixed allocation, the recommended
DGP as defined. All three used the same seed rule and an analysis
half-life of 0.5 week. The third run changes one setting relative to
each earlier run, so the effects of the carryover rule and of the
allocation can be read separately. The tables in Stage 6 are from the
third run. Randomized allocation is a driver option that is not part
of the definition.

\textbf{Stop instead of repair.} \texttt{buildSigma()}, \texttt{generateData()} and
\texttt{generateSimulatedResults()} accept a \texttt{stopOnNonPD} argument, \texttt{TRUE}
by default. A non-positive-definite covariance matrix then raises an
error, exactly as in B.2, both at the explicit positive-definiteness
check and in the Cholesky-factorization fallback that previously
repaired regardless of the \texttt{makePositiveDefinite} setting. A repair is
reproduced only by passing
\texttt{stopOnNonPD\ =\ FALSE} and setting
\texttt{options(pmsimstats.allow\_repair\ =\ TRUE)} explicitly, as the scripts
that study the repair itself do. Without the option, the repair is
locked off: a draw from a matrix that is not positive definite stops
with an error whatever the other arguments are. The argument was first added with a \texttt{FALSE} default for
backward compatibility. The default was changed after two runs of the
companion carryover study repaired infeasible cells and reported the
attenuated interaction as a power loss.

\textbf{Not in the package.} The measurement-error, Weibull-decay and
dropout options of Table \ref{tab:spec} are implemented in the
stress-test driver (\texttt{11-strawman-stress-test.R}) and in the companion
study's driver core, and not yet in the package. The package's dropout
mechanisms (\texttt{censordata.R}) are censoring patterns in which the biased
term weights each participant by 100 minus the improvement from
baseline, floored at zero and raised to the power \texttt{eb1}, so that
dropout rises with worsening. Figure \ref{fig:fig4bg10} uses them.
