\section*{Abstract}\label{abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

\textbf{Background.} Aggregated N-of-1 trials pool repeated within-patient
on/off contrasts to validate predictive biomarkers. Drug carryover
attenuates the biomarker-by-treatment interaction. The analyst must
decide how to represent residual exposure, how to size the test, and
whether the half-life must be known.

\textbf{Methods.} In an exploratory simulation under the structure of a
companion paper's data-generating process, with a predictive-only
biomarker, we compared an analysis ladder in six scenario families.
Rung 0 is the starting analysis (A0);
rung 1 a binary drug indicator with \texttt{corCAR1} residual correlation
(A1); rung 2 adds CR2 cluster-robust inference (A2); rung 3 an
exposure-weighted predictor at the true half-life (A3, an oracle);
rung 4 chooses the half-life by AIC on the model without the
interaction (A4).

\textbf{Results.} Pooled over three designs in the base grid, A0 rejected
the null at 0.110 and A1 at
0.038. CR2 moved size to 0.055.
In the Hybrid design A4 exceeded A2,
the CR2 binary indicator, by 1.3
points on average over 40 alternative cells and by up
to 7.1 at long half-lives. It was below
A2 in 6 cells, by at most
1.0 point. In the run with independent
seeds, the size of A4 minus that of the oracle was
-0.0008 (90 percent interval
-0.0033 to +0.0018),
equivalent within \(\pm 0.01\).

\textbf{Conclusions.} For a Hybrid trial with a biomarker known to be
predictive only, under this process at \(N \le 70\), A4 is correctly
sized and needs no half-life. Its gain over the binary indicator is
modest except at long half-lives. Even the oracle recovers only part
of the power that carryover removes, so the rest has to be met by the
design. Whether a biomarker is also prognostic is rarely known before
a trial. In that case the analysis of the companion paper applies;
this paper's results bear on its choice of half-life and its CR2 test. The results
are conditional on the correlation encoding and one parameter set.

\textbf{Keywords:} N-of-1 trials; carryover; predictive biomarker;
cluster-robust inference; post-selection inference; simulation
study.

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

In a single N-of-1 trial, one patient is crossed repeatedly between
active treatment and a comparator. The within-patient contrast
between on-drug and off-drug periods estimates that patient's
individual treatment effect. An aggregated N-of-1 trial pools many
such series. Pooling allows population-level inference at sample
sizes smaller than a parallel-group trial would require
(\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{1}). The estimand here is the biomarker-treatment
interaction, that is, whether a baseline covariate \(B_i\) modifies
the treatment effect. We evaluate analysis approaches for this
estimand in the Hybrid N-of-1 design defined in Section \ref{sec-designs}.

The main inferential complication in N-of-1 trials is carryover. A
drug's pharmacological effect does not stop at discontinuation. It
decays as a function of the drug's half-life. Observations taken
after discontinuation therefore still carry part of the treatment
effect. An exposure regressor that ignores this decay is a
mismeasured version of what the patient received. The consequences
are twofold. First, the interaction contrast is attenuated.
Occasions that still carry a large fraction of the drug effect are
coded the same as occasions that carry none. This biases the
biomarker-by-exposure coefficient toward zero, as any covariate
measured with error would. Second, those occasions vary in a way the
model does not represent. The unexplained variation inflates the
standard error of the same coefficient. Power falls for both
reasons.

This is a loss of power, not of validity. Under the null,
\(c_{bm} = 0\), there is no biomarker-response association at any
level of residual exposure. A miscoded regressor then has no
interaction to distort, and test size does not depend on the
exposure coding. The conservatism reported in Section \ref{sec-rung2} has a
separate origin in the working covariance and applies equally to
every specification. Carryover misspecification is therefore a
question of efficiency. Rung 3 of the study is therefore framed in terms of recovered power.

A companion study (\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats_dgp_architectures2026}{2}) builds on the
data-generating process of Hendrickson et al. (\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{1}).
In that process the interaction is encoded as a correlation between
the biomarker and the drug response. The correlation is present on
drug and decays off drug: \(\text{Cor}(B_i, BR_{it}) = c_{bm} \cdot
\phi(t_{sd})\). We call this the correlation encoding of the
interaction. The companion study shows that carryover reduces power
under this encoding, and that the reduction depends on the design.
The loss under a given analysis is governed by the on/off correlation
difference, that is, the on-drug correlation minus the mean off-drug
correlation, which the analysis's exposure coding determines. At a
half-life of one week in this study's factorial grid, the binary
indicator loses a fifth to two thirds of its power under the Hybrid
and OL+BDC designs. It loses 7 to 16 percent under the crossover
design (Section \ref{sec-rung3}). We restrict attention to the correlation
encoding. The carryover problem is largest there, and the starting DGP
uses it. Earlier work under this encoding fixed the decay shape at
exponential and evaluated only one analysis specification. Simulation studies of aggregated N-of-1 analyses
under carryover exist (\citeproc{ref-chen2014}{3}--\citeproc{ref-gartner2023}{6}). One includes an exponentially decaying wash-in and
wash-out term (\citeproc{ref-gartner2023}{6}). All target the treatment effect rather
than the biomarker-by-treatment interaction, which is the estimand
here. An interaction test needs about four times the sample of a
main-effect test of the same size (\citeproc{ref-brookes2004}{7}). The correlation
encoding is a modeling assumption of this project, not a validated
description of any trial. Every recommendation below is conditional
on it.

This manuscript shows how a sequence of changes to the analysis model
improves both size and power. We call the sequence an analysis ladder.
Each rung changes one thing about the analysis. The rungs follow the
order in which the questions arise for an analyst. Section
\ref{sec-ladder-def} defines the rungs and Table
\ref{tab:tab-spec-summary} lists them.

Rung 0 is the starting analysis (A0), the analysis used with the
starting DGP (\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{1}). It fits a random intercept, uses a binary
on-drug indicator, and has no residual correlation structure. The
first question is whether its test is correctly sized. Rung 1 (A1)
keeps the binary indicator and uses an AR(1) residual correlation
through \texttt{corCAR1} with the working-covariance standard error. A0 and
A1 differ in three respects at once. Isolation analyses separate the three and
attribute the liberal size of A0 to its missing residual correlation
(Section \ref{sec-rung1}).

The second question concerns the size of the test. The working
covariance of the mixed model is a random intercept with an AR(1)
residual correlation. It does not match the covariance that the
data-generating process induces, and the working-covariance test is
conservative. Rung 2 (A2) replaces the working-covariance standard error by a
CR2 bias-reduced cluster-robust standard error with Satterthwaite
degrees of freedom (\citeproc{ref-bell2002bias}{8}). The rung changes only the
standard error, the reference distribution and the \(p\)-value.

The third question is how residual exposure should enter the linear
predictor. The analyst may ignore the decay and code exposure as on or
off, as the starting analysis does (A1 and A2). Or the analyst may
represent it by committing to a decay form and half-life in a
continuous exposure-weighted predictor. The predictor changes the
regressor that carries the interaction. Rung 3 (A3) is the
exposure-weighted predictor at the true half-life with CR2 inference.
We compare the two positions against exponential and Weibull decay
forms in a full factorial.

The fourth question follows from the third position. Rung 3 uses the
true half-life, which no real analyst knows, so A3 is an oracle
benchmark rather than a candidate analysis. Rung 4 (A4) lets the data
choose the half-life by AIC over a candidate grid, from fits of the
model without the interaction, and then tests the interaction at the
chosen value. The selection step adds a post-selection inference risk
(\citeproc{ref-berk2013valid}{9}--\citeproc{ref-kuchibhotla2022}{11}). Leaving the tested
term out of the selection limits that risk. We measure what the
selection costs against the oracle in size, power and standard-error
calibration.

We then stress the ladder in scenario families that a real trial may
present. These are a misspecified half-life and decay shape, serial
correlation, patient dropout and a patient-specific slope variance.

The biomarker in this study is predictive only: it changes the drug
response and nothing else. A biomarker that is also prognostic raises
a different question, namely whether the interaction test confuses an
association with the natural history or the placebo response with
moderation of the drug effect. A prognostic shift that is constant
over visits is absorbed exactly by the biomarker main effect of every
analysis here. It cannot move the interaction estimate, so it needs no
simulation. Prognostic associations that vary over time, and the design-expectancy terms that
protect against them, are the subject of a companion paper
(\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats_prognostic2026}{12}).

Two defects in the simulation code were found during the study and
corrected. The first was a pre-exposure coding error in the
exposure-weighted predictor that affected the crossover design. The
second was a cross-factor correlation constant that departed from the
documented data-generating process. Every result in this manuscript
was produced under the corrected code. We
follow the ADEMP reporting framework of Morris, White, and Crowther
(\citeproc{ref-morris2019simulation}{13}) throughout. We also follow later reporting
guidance on common random numbers and on comparative simulation
studies (\citeproc{ref-siepe2024}{14},\citeproc{ref-pawel2024}{15}). The study is exploratory. Its rungs
and scenarios were not fixed in advance, and the recommendation is the
end of a documented sequence of choices rather than a pre-specified
comparison. Section \ref{sec-design} describes the simulation design
in ADEMP order. Section \ref{sec-results} gives the results rung by
rung and then by scenario. Section \ref{sec-discussion} states the
recommendation and its limits.

\section{Simulation design}\label{sec-design}

The study simulates complete aggregated N-of-1 trials and analyzes
each one under every specification. A single replicate proceeds as
follows. Patients are allocated across the randomization paths of
the chosen trial design (Section \ref{sec-designs}). For each patient we draw a
pre-treatment biomarker together with three latent response
components. These are the drug-response component, the
natural-history component, and the placebo-response component. The
four quantities are drawn jointly across all eight measurement
occasions from a multivariate normal distribution. The means follow
modified Gompertz trajectories. The covariance matrix contains
within-component AR(1) autocorrelation, cross-component
correlations, and the biomarker-response correlation that encodes
the interaction and depends on treatment state (Section \ref{sec-dgm}). The
observed symptom score at each occasion is the patient's baseline
severity minus the sum of the three components. A beneficial effect
therefore lowers the score. Carryover enters through a decay
function of time since discontinuation. The decay keeps part of the
drug-response component present at nominally off-drug occasions.
Every analysis specification in a given cell is then fitted to the
same simulated dataset. We record the interaction coefficient, its
standard error, and its \(p\)-value from each. This is repeated 500
times for every parameter combination in the grid of Section
\ref{sec-grid}, 1,000 to 5,000 times in the Hybrid run with
independent seeds, and 2,000 times in the supplementary size
diagnostic.

Fitting all specifications to a common dataset is deliberate. It
makes every contrast among them paired. Paired differences have
substantially less Monte Carlo variance than differences between
independent draws. Throughout, \(N\) denotes the \textbf{total} number of
patients in a trial. Patients are allocated as evenly as possible
across the design's randomization paths, so a design with \(P\) paths
assigns about \(N/P\) patients to each. At \(N = 70\) this is 35 per
path under the two-path designs and 17 or 18 per path under the
four-path Hybrid design.

\subsection{Aims}\label{sec-aims}

The first aim is to show how each change to the analysis model alters
size and power in the biomarker-treatment interaction test of a Hybrid
aggregated N-of-1 trial under the correlation encoding. The changes
form a ladder of five rungs (Table \ref{tab:tab-spec-summary}). Rung 0
is the starting analysis. Rung 1 uses a binary drug indicator with a
residual AR(1) correlation. Rung 2 adds CR2 cluster-robust inference
to fix size. Rung 3 replaces the binary indicator by an
exposure-weighted predictor at the true half-life to recover part of
the power that carryover removes. Rung 4 chooses the half-life by AIC, on the
model without the interaction, to remove the need to know it. Each
rung is judged against its predecessor by a paired contrast on the
same simulated trials. A side analysis measures what it costs to
choose the half-life with the interaction in the model instead.

The second aim is to find where a rung does not help. The scenario
families of Table \ref{tab:tab-families} stress the ladder with a
misspecified half-life and decay shape, serial correlation, dropout
and a patient-specific slope variance. The
third aim is to arrive at a recommended analysis for the Hybrid design
and to state its conditions and limits. A fourth aim is to document
the evidence for each rung so that a reader can check it.

\subsection{Trial designs}\label{sec-designs}

The three designs differ in how on-drug and off-drug occasions are
arranged. They therefore differ in how much post-discontinuation
information each generates. All three schedule eight measurement
occasions per patient. Each randomization path is a fixed
on-drug/off-drug schedule. Each design also carries an expectancy
weight, defined as the participant's probability of being on drug
by design. The weight is one during open-label periods and one half
during blinded periods, and it scales the placebo-response
component.

\textbf{Crossover (CO).} A traditional two-period within-patient
crossover, blinded throughout. Occasions are spaced 2.5 weeks apart
from 2.5 to 20 weeks. One path is active for the first four
occasions and then on comparator. The other path reverses that
order, so it never discontinues and contributes no
post-discontinuation observations.

\textbf{Open-label followed by blinded discontinuation (OL+BDC).}
Open-label titration at 4, 8, 12, and 16 weeks, then weekly
follow-up to week 20 with a blinded late switch to placebo. The two
paths discontinue after week 17 or 18, so only two or three
occasions follow.

\textbf{Hybrid.} The reference design of this program, taken from the
starting DGP. It consists of open-label titration at weeks 4 and
8, blinded discontinuation weekly from week 9 to 12, and a brief
two-occasion crossover at weeks 16 and 20. The four paths vary
discontinuation timing and crossover-arm order. It has the most
on/off transitions of the three designs. It is the only one that
allows more than one discontinuation per patient. The recommendation
of this manuscript is made for this design. CO and OL+BDC are
carried through the factorial grid and the corrected grid to show where the recommendation does
and does not transfer.

One consequence bears directly on the results. The first
post-discontinuation occasion falls one week after switching under
OL+BDC and Hybrid, but 2.5 weeks after switching under CO. At the
half-lives examined here, roughly half the drug effect persists into
the first off-drug occasion under the two non-crossover designs. Less
than a fifth persists under CO. The designs therefore differ
substantially in how much carryover they can accumulate.

\subsection{Data-generating mechanism}\label{sec-dgm}

Under the correlation encoding, the biomarker and the drug-response
component are drawn jointly from a multivariate normal distribution.
Their correlation depends on treatment state: \(\text{Cor}(B_i,
BR_{it}) = c_{bm} \cdot \phi(t_{sd})\), where \(\phi\) is the decay
form and \(t_{sd}\) is the time since drug discontinuation. The
data-generating process has the structure of the recommended DGP of
the companion study. Its parameter values are not those of the
companion study (Table \ref{tab:tab-params}). The response SDs and
the biomarker and baseline scales are the simulation core's own set,
chosen for this program, not Hendrickson's values. The placebo-response
SD in particular is 2 here against 10 in the starting DGP, which makes
the placebo pathway much weaker. The structure differs from the
starting DGP in four respects. The biomarker-response correlation is zero before
first exposure and decays after discontinuation at the carryover
half-life. The within-component correlation is a separable AR(1)
structure at \(\rho = 0.7\). That is, every component has the same
first-order autoregressive correlation, and a single cross-component
constant \(c_1 = 0.2\) is scaled by \(\rho^{|w_t - w_s|}\) at every lag.
Carryover on the drug-response mean decays from the
last on-drug mean. A covariance matrix that is not positive definite
stops the simulation rather than being repaired (Section \ref{sec-corrections}).

The decay function \(\phi : \mathbb{R}_+ \to [0, 1]\) maps \(t_{sd}\) to
a residual-effect multiplier. Two forms are used. Each is matched to
a common half-life \(t_{1/2}\). Exponential decay,

\[\phi_{\text{exp}}(t_{sd}) =
  \exp\left(-\lambda t_{sd}\right), \qquad
  \lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{t_{1/2}},\]

is classical first-order elimination and the default pharmacokinetic
assumption. Weibull decay,

\[\phi_{\text{weib}}(t_{sd}) =
  \exp\left(-\left(\lambda_w t_{sd}\right)^{k}\right), \qquad
  \lambda_w = \frac{(\ln 2)^{1/k}}{t_{1/2}},\]

has a shape parameter \(k\). The elimination tail is heavier than
exponential when \(k < 1\) and lighter when \(k > 1\). The form reduces
to exponential at \(k = 1\). The factorial grid uses \(k \in \{0.7,
1.5\}\) alongside exponential. The decay-shape
misspecification cells (family 2) use \(k \in \{0.5, 2\}\). All forms pass through
the same half-life-defining point, \(\phi = 0.5\) at \(t_{sd} =
t_{1/2}\). They are therefore comparable at a fixed nominal half-life
even though their shapes differ on either side of it. The decay form
the analysis assumes differs by scenario. In the factorial grid the
exposure-weighted predictor is built with the data-generating form,
shape and half-life. This is the driver's default when no analysis
form is given. The Weibull cells of the factorial grid therefore test the
representation under a correctly specified decay, not its robustness
to the shape. Every other analysis assumes exponential
decay. The Weibull cells of family 2 are the shape-misspecification
check.

\subsection{Estimand and analysis specifications}\label{sec-spec}

The target of inference is the on-drug biomarker-response
correlation \(c_{bm}\), a dimensionless quantity. Every analysis tests
it through the coefficient of \(\text{bm} \times X\). Its target on the
calibrated scale is \(\theta = -c_{bm}\sigma_{BR}\), with
\(\sigma_{BR} = 8\) in this parameter set (Table \ref{tab:tab-params}):
the expected difference in on-drug response per SD of the biomarker.
Bias, empirical SE and coverage are reported on this scale. Type I
error is the rejection rate at \(c_{bm} = 0\) and power the rejection
rate at \(c_{bm} > 0\). What the coefficient estimates depends on \(X\).
Under A3, \(X\) is exposure-weighted at the true half-life and the
coefficient targets \(\theta\). Under A1 and A2, \(X\) is the binary
indicator, which codes residual exposure off drug as none, so under
carryover the coefficient differs from \(\theta\). Under A4 the
half-life is selected, and the coefficient targets \(\theta\) only when
the selected value is the true one. The base analysis model is
\texttt{nlme::lme(Sx\ \textasciitilde{}\ bm\ *\ X\ +\ t,\ random\ =\ \textasciitilde{}1\ \textbar{}\ ptID,\ correlation\ =\ corCAR1(form\ =\ \textasciitilde{}\ t\ \textbar{}\ ptID))}.
The analyses differ in how \(X\) is constructed and how the standard
error is obtained. This manuscript labels them A0 to A4 by rung (Table
\ref{tab:tab-spec-summary}). The table also gives the code each
analysis carries in the companion papers and earlier drafts, where A4
is G10, and the \texttt{E}-prefixed code under which it is stored in the
code and data files described in the Reproducibility section.

\subsubsection{The analysis ladder}\label{sec-ladder-def}

The ladder orders the analyses by what each one changes. Table
\ref{tab:tab-spec-summary} gives the formula, the inference and the
assumptions of each. Rung 0 is the starting analysis (A0). Rung 1 is
the binary-indicator analysis with \texttt{corCAR1} residual correlation and
the working-covariance standard error (A1). Rung 2 is A1 with CR2
cluster-robust inference (A2). Rung 3 replaces the binary indicator by the
exposure-weighted predictor at the true half-life, with CR2 (A3). Rung
4 chooses the half-life by AIC on the model without the interaction,
with CR2 (A4). Each rung changes one thing from its predecessor, with
one exception. The step from A0 to A1 changes three things at once,
and the isolation analyses E0n, E0nc and E1nc separate them (Section
\ref{sec-rung1}). A3 uses the true half-life. No analyst has it, so
A3 is an oracle benchmark, not a candidate analysis: it shows what the
selection step of rung 4 costs.

\subsubsection{Rung 0: the starting analysis (A0)}\label{sec-e0}

\textbf{A0} is the starting analysis, the analysis published with the
starting DGP (\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{1}). We read it from the published
repository (commit
\texttt{58b32a9}, \texttt{R/lme\_analysis.R}) rather than from this project's
vendored copy, which had acquired a residual correlation structure
at some later point. It fits \texttt{lme4::lmer} with a random intercept
and no residual correlation structure. It uses the binary on-drug
indicator and takes Satterthwaite \(p\)-values from \texttt{lmerTest}. As
published it also adds the expectancy main effect \(D_e\) whenever
expectancy varies within the design, and it drops patients who are
never on drug. Under the crossover design expectancy is constant, so
\(D_e\) drops out. A0 therefore differs from A1 in three respects at once: no
\texttt{corCAR1}, the \(D_e\) term, and the software and degrees-of-freedom
method. A separate simulation changes them one at a time (E0n, E0nc
and E1nc; Section \ref{sec-rung1}).

\subsubsection{Rung 1: binary indicator with residual correlation (A1)}\label{sec-g1}

Here \(X = D_{it}\), the binary on-drug indicator (one on drug, zero
off). Carryover is not represented. An off-drug occasion that still
carries most of the drug effect is coded the same as one that
carries none. This is the default an analyst obtains without
considering carryover. We treat it as the benchmark against which
the other specifications must justify their additional structure.
It uses no half-life, so it does not depend on the analyst's
pharmacokinetic assumptions.

\subsubsection{Rung 2: CR2 cluster-robust inference (A2 to A4)}\label{sec-cr2-method}

The standard error of \(\beta_{bm:X}\) read from the fitted \texttt{lme}
object is valid only if the assumed residual covariance is correct.
The working covariance combines a patient random intercept with a
\texttt{corCAR1} AR(1) residual correlation. The companion calibration
study (\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats_calibration2026}{16}) shows that this does not match
the covariance the data-generating process induces. We quantify the
consequence with the calibration ratio

\[\kappa = \frac{\overline{\widehat{SE}}}{SD\big(\hat\beta_{bm:X}\big)},\]

the mean model-reported standard error across replicates divided by
the empirical standard deviation of the replicate point estimates.
\(\kappa = 1\) is correctly calibrated and \(\kappa > 1\) is a
conservative test. The mean of the standard errors is below their
root mean square. \(\kappa\) as defined is therefore biased downward
when the standard errors vary across replicates. We keep the mean
because every stored driver output uses it. With the root mean square
instead, the family-level \(\kappa\) of the CR2 analyses rises by
0.01 to 0.03, slightly more
for A4, whose standard errors vary more with the selected
half-life. A2, A3 and A4 are close enough that their
order can change, and the alternative \(\kappa\) of A4 reaches
0.95 to
1.02 by family. The empirical SD in the denominator is also
sensitive to a heavy-tailed estimate distribution. Section \ref{sec-dropout} meets
that case under heavy missingness and reports a robust ratio beside
it. Coverage of the nominal 95\% interval is the complementary
measure. It is reported where the stored per-replicate output allows
it. The remedy for the mismatch is a cluster-robust sandwich
variance. It derives the variance of \(\hat\beta_{bm:X}\) from
observed between-patient variation rather than from the assumed
covariance. With only \(N = 35\) or \(70\) clusters the naive
Liang-Zeger form is itself biased downward. We therefore use the CR2
bias-reduced estimator of Bell and McCaffrey (\citeproc{ref-bell2002bias}{8}) with
Satterthwaite degrees of freedom, as implemented in \texttt{clubSandwich}
(\citeproc{ref-pustejovsky2018}{17}). It replaces only the standard error, the
reference distribution and the \(p\)-value. Point estimates are
unchanged. The Kenward-Roger correction (\citeproc{ref-kenward1997}{18}) is the
standard small-sample correction within the working covariance. It corrects the
degrees of freedom and the variance under the working covariance, so
it does not address a misspecified one. It is not evaluated here. A2
is A1 with CR2 inference. A3 and A4 also use CR2, so A2 and A3 are the
like-for-like comparators for the power of A4. A1 is conservative.
Part of any power difference against it is the increase in power from
correcting a conservative test.

\subsubsection{Rung 3: exposure-weighted predictor (A3)}\label{sec-g3}

Here \(X = D_{bc,it}\), a continuous exposure-decayed predictor. It
is built from the analyst's assumed decay form and analysis
half-life \(t^{a}_{1/2}\). At on-drug timepoints \(D_{bc,it} = 1\). Off
drug after first exposure it decays as \(D_{bc,it} =
(1/2)^{t_{sd,it}/t^{a}_{1/2}}\). It loses half its value every
\(t^{a}_{1/2}\) weeks after discontinuation. Before any exposure
\(D_{bc,it} = 0\). This pre-exposure rule was absent in earlier drafts
(Section \ref{sec-corrections}). The half-life assumption enters only through the
predictor values passed to \texttt{lme}. Exposure-weighted is therefore a
different model for each assumed half-life. As \(t^{a}_{1/2}
\rightarrow 0\), \(D_{bc,it} \rightarrow D_{it}\), so Unadjusted is the
boundary member of the Exposure-weighted family at zero assumed
half-life. At rung 3 the assumed half-life is set equal to the data-generating one. This is the true half-life, which no real
analyst knows. Rung 4 removes that assumption. Under the correlation
encoding, the regression of the response on the biomarker at an
occasion is linear in \(c_{bm}\,\phi(t_{sd})\). \(D_{bc,it}\) at the true
\(\phi\) is therefore the correctly specified regressor. The
factorial grid compares a correct model with two incorrect ones. The question it
answers is how much the incorrect ones lose.

\subsubsection{Rung 4: AIC-selected half-life (A4)}\label{sec-g4}

This specification uses the Exposure-weighted formula but chooses
the analysis half-life \(\hat{t}^{a}_{1/2}\) from the data. Write
\(M_0(t^{a}_{1/2})\) for the \texttt{lme} fit of the model without the
interaction, \(\text{Sx} \sim bm + t + D_{bc}\), with \(D_{bc,it}\)
constructed at a candidate half-life. The selected half-life is

\[\hat{t}^{a}_{1/2} = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{h \,\in\, \mathcal{T}}
  \text{AIC}\big(M_0(h)\big), \qquad
  \mathcal{T} = \{0.25,\ 0.5,\ 1.0,\ 2.0\}\ \text{weeks},\]

with \(\text{AIC}(M) = -2\log L(M) + 2p\) (\citeproc{ref-akaike1974new}{19}). The
candidate fits are compared by maximum likelihood. The candidates
differ in a fixed-effects column, and REML likelihoods are not
comparable across fixed-effects designs. The model with the
interaction is then fitted by REML at \(\hat{t}^{a}_{1/2}\), and the
interaction coefficient, its CR2 standard error and its \(p\)-value are
read from that single fit, not pooled across \(\mathcal{T}\). Because
the selection model leaves out the interaction, the selected
half-life does not depend on the coefficient under test. The test
still treats \(\hat t^{a}_{1/2}\) as fixed in advance when the same data
chose it, so a post-selection risk remains in principle
(\citeproc{ref-berk2013valid}{9}--\citeproc{ref-kuchibhotla2022}{11}). It is smaller than when
the selection uses the tested coefficient, and Section
\ref{sec-rung4} measures it against the same predictor at the true
half-life (A3). That section also reports the distribution of the
selected half-life.

\subsubsection{Side analysis: selecting on the interaction model}\label{sec-comparators}

The obvious alternative makes the same choice with the interaction
term in the selection model, \(\text{Sx} \sim bm + t + D_{bc} +
bm{:}D_{bc}\). It mirrors \texttt{characterize\_carryover()}
(\texttt{R/carryover\_analysis.R}), the package's own real-data
half-life-selection routine, and earlier drafts used it as the
recommended analysis. The selected half-life then depends on the
coefficient under test. Fitted with CR2 to the same simulated trials
as A4, it rejected the null more often than A4 in every scenario
family, by +0.001 to +0.007, and its standard error
was a few percent too small under the alternative. A4 leaves the
interaction out of the selection model for this reason. The variant is
not reported further.

\subsection{Choice of analysis specifications}\label{sec-choice}

The specifications were chosen to represent distinct, established
positions an analyst can take toward an unmeasured decay, and then
to answer the specific questions each rung raised. They were not
chosen to survey the wider space of N-of-1 analysis models. Bayesian
hierarchical models, GEE as a primary model, randomization tests,
flexible spline decay curves, state-space treatments of drug
concentration and two-stage meta-analytic combination are all
established in the N-of-1 or crossover literature. None is evaluated
here. Section \ref{sec-related} returns to three of them. An earlier draft also
tested carryover terms interacted with the biomarker (\texttt{bm:L} and
\texttt{bm:t\_\{sd\}}). Both had lower power than A1 at every cell. We
attribute this to the data-generating process having a single true
decay parameter, so that a second interacted coefficient adds
estimation noise with no signal to recover. They were dropped in
favor of choosing the decay half-life from the data (rung 4), which
addresses the same unknown.

reported separately as a heavy-missingness stress case. The study

(\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats_prognostic2026}{12}). Every cell

covariance matrix is positive before any simulation ran.

\subsection{Performance measures and computing environment}\label{sec-perf}

For each cell we report Power, Type I error, Bias, Empirical SE
(calibrated scale), Coverage of the nominal 95\% Wald CI, MSE,
non-convergence rate, and the MCSE of each, following Morris et al.
(\citeproc{ref-morris2019simulation}{13}, Tables 5-6). For the analyses that select
the half-life (A4), a fit can fail in the ML selection or
in the REML refit at the selected value. The stored output records
only whether the final fit succeeded, so the non-convergence rate
bounds the refit failure rate from above. Over the
55,000 A4
fits of the backfill, 132
failed
(0.24
percent). We chose \(n_{sim} = 500\) so
that the MCSE on power at \(p = 0.5\) would not exceed 0.022. At
\(n_{sim} = 500\) the two-MCSE band around a nominal 0.05 rejection
rate is approximately \([0.031, 0.069]\). That band is a check on
gross inflation, not a demonstration of nominal size. A single
500-replicate cell whose true size is 0.065 falls inside the band
with probability about 0.64. One whose true size is 0.075 falls
inside it with probability about 0.31 (normal approximation). Claims
about size therefore rest on the pooled 2,000-replicate cells of
Section \ref{sec-rung4} and on paired contrasts between specifications. Single-cell
band membership is reported as such. No simulation runs while this
manuscript renders. All specifications are fit to the same simulated
dataset within each cell (common random numbers). Every pairwise
contrast between specifications therefore has reduced Monte Carlo
variance relative to independent draws. Where a paired standard
error is quoted, it is computed from the per-replicate rejection
indicators. The drivers also seed replicate \(r\) of every cell from a
job-level base plus \(r\). Cells at the same design and \(N\) within a
job therefore share a random-number stream. Contrasts between such
cells are paired too. Counts of cells inside a band are not
independent confirmations and are not presented as such.
Comparisons across jobs, and across the two simulation hosts, are
unpaired and carry the full Monte Carlo error of both cells. The two
hosts do not produce identical draws. Exact file paths and versions
are given in the Reproducibility section.

\subsection{Corrections to the simulation code during the study}\label{sec-corrections}

Two defects in the simulation driver were found and corrected while
this study was in progress, and one policy was changed. Each is
stated here so that the reader can tell which results it affects.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Pre-exposure coding of the exposure-weighted predictor
  (corrected 2026-10-06).} Before any exposure, \(D_{bc,it}\) was
  coded as \(\phi(0) = 1\) rather than 0. A patient on the
  placebo-first path of the crossover design was therefore treated
  as fully exposed at every visit. The same correction replaced the
  REML AIC comparison of the half-life selection with the ML comparison and REML refit of
  Section \ref{sec-g4}. A paired check fitted each simulated trial under the
  old and new coding (\texttt{26-co-dbc-fix-paired.R}). It showed that the
  entire collapse of the exposure-weighted family under the
  crossover design reported in earlier drafts was this defect. With
  the correction, the exposure-weighted analyses match A1 under CO. Hybrid cells were
  unaffected, because no patient starts on placebo there.
\item
  \textbf{Cross-factor correlation constant (corrected 2026-10-21).} The
  documented data-generating process uses a single cross-component
  correlation \(c_1 = 0.2\) scaled by \(\rho^{|w_t - w_s|}\) at every
  lag. The driver had used 0.1 at lags greater than zero. The factorial
  grid was rerun in full under the corrected constant. Every
  other comparison was run after it. No result from before
  this correction is reported. The 3,000-replicate size diagnostic
  of earlier drafts was replaced by the 2,000-replicate
  corrected-code diagnostic of Section \ref{sec-rung2}, which reproduces its
  conclusion.
\item
  \textbf{Non-positive-definite covariance matrices are refused
  (2026-10-26).} As in the recommended DGP, the driver stops on such
  a cell rather than changing it
  (\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats_dgp_architectures2026}{2}). No cell reported here was
  affected.
\end{enumerate}

The exploratory sensitivity blocks of earlier drafts predate both
corrections and are no longer part of this manuscript. Those blocks
covered within-component autocorrelation, analyst-truth half-life
mismatch, dropout, the effect-size curve, and single-cell
comparisons of further specifications and of the CR2 variants. Their
material remains in the archived drafts. The autocorrelation,
half-life and dropout scenarios were rerun under the corrected code
in the supplementary grids of Section \ref{sec-scen-grids}. The dropout block of those
drafts used the per-occasion convention described in Section \ref{sec-grid}.
Under that convention a stated rate of 0.1 to 0.3 removed 36 to 73
percent of the observations. Its rates are therefore not comparable
with the patient-level rates reported here.

\section{Results}\label{sec-results}

Results follow the ladder. Section \ref{sec-glance} shows the whole
ladder at a glance. Section \ref{sec-rungs} gives the evidence for
each rung. Section \ref{sec-stress} stresses the ladder in scenario
families. Section \ref{sec-calibration} reports calibration and
coverage. Section \ref{sec-which} states which analysis to use. The
Monte Carlo standard error on power does not exceed 0.022 at \(p = 0.5\)
in a 500-replicate cell. Differences smaller than this should not be
over-interpreted. Pooled estimates and paired differences have smaller
standard errors and are quoted with them.

\subsection{The ladder at a glance}\label{sec-glance}

Table \ref{tab:tab-spec-summary} defines the analyses. Rungs 0 to 4
form the ladder. Each rung changes one thing from the one before,

\subsection{Evidence rung by rung}\label{sec-rungs}

Each subsection opens with what the rung changes and ends with what the
rung changed in size and in power. Paired contrasts use the same
simulated trials. The numbers in the closing sentences come from Table
\ref{tab:tab-ladder-inc} and the figures of Section \ref{sec-glance}.

\subsubsection{Rung 0 to 1: residual correlation}\label{sec-rung1}

Rung 1 replaces the starting analysis (A0) by A1. A0 fits \texttt{lme4::lmer}
with a random intercept and takes Satterthwaite \(p\)-values from
\texttt{lmerTest}. It has no residual correlation structure. As published it
adds the expectancy main effect \(D_e\) whenever expectancy varies within
the design, and it drops patients who are never on drug. A1 fits
\texttt{nlme::lme} with a random intercept and a \texttt{corCAR1} residual
correlation. It has no expectancy term, keeps every patient, and uses
the working-covariance standard error. A0 and A1 therefore differ in three
respects at once. These are the software and degrees-of-freedom method,
the residual correlation, and the expectancy term with the exclusion of
patients. Table S1 reports Type I error at the six
null cells of the corrected grid. The grid has three designs and two
sample sizes, all at \(t_{1/2} = 1\) week. It has no null cell at other
half-lives outside Hybrid.

A0 rejects at 0.060 to 0.150 in these six
cells. It is far outside the two-MCSE band under CO and OL+BDC and at
its edge under Hybrid. A1 rejects at 0.030 to
0.048, below nominal at every cell. The step from A0 to
A1 therefore moves the test from liberal to conservative.

\textbf{A0 is liberal under CO and OL+BDC, and at the edge of the band under
Hybrid.} Across the four Hybrid and OL+BDC null scenarios of
Table S7, A0 rejects at
0.060 to 0.100.
It reaches 0.100 under OL+BDC. In
the 2,000-replicate Hybrid cells of Table S3 it
is 0.062 to 0.064. Taken together, its inflation is clear under CO and
OL+BDC and marginal under Hybrid, where several single cells lie inside
the 500-replicate band. The power of A0 at the alternatives is not
comparable with that of the other analyses because the test is
liberal.

A separate run attributes the change from A0 to A1 (simrun job 42).
It fits five analyses to the same simulated trials and changes one
element at a time. E0n refits the formula and the sample of A0 with
\texttt{nlme} and no residual correlation. It changes only the software and
the degrees-of-freedom method. E0nc adds \texttt{corCAR1} to E0n. E1nc drops
the expectancy term and the exclusion rule of A0 and keeps \texttt{nlme}
without residual correlation. A1 adds \texttt{corCAR1} to E1nc. The run
covers the 30 cells of the grid at 1,000
replicates per cell. E0nc failed to converge in at most
9 replicates of a cell. The other four analyses
converged in every replicate. Table S2 gives the
results at \(t_{1/2} = 1\) week.

\textbf{The residual correlation carries the whole size correction.}
Adding \texttt{corCAR1} to E0n lowers Type I error from
0.137 (MCSE 0.008)
to 0.047
(0.005) under CO, from
0.098 (0.007)
to 0.034
(0.004) under OL+BDC, and from
0.061
(0.005) to
0.034
(0.004) under Hybrid. Pooled over
the six null cells the paired change is
-0.061 (paired SE
0.008). Adding \texttt{corCAR1} to the A1
formula (E1nc to A1) has the same effect,
-0.060
(0.008). Without the residual
correlation the standard error is too small. The calibration ratio
\(\kappa\) of E0n is 0.75 to
0.97 at the null cells. With
\texttt{corCAR1} it is 0.99 to
1.08. The overcorrection below
nominal under Hybrid and OL+BDC is the conservatism that rung 2
removes.

\textbf{The software and the degrees-of-freedom method have no effect.} A0 and
E0n give \(p\)-values that differ by less than
0.0001 in every
replicate. Their rejection
rates agree in all 30 cells.

\textbf{The expectancy term and the exclusion rule have almost no effect.} The
exclusion rule of A0 drops no patient in any replicate of this grid,
because the drug indicator varies within patients in every design.
Under CO the expectancy term is absent from A0, so E0n and E1nc are
the same model. Under Hybrid and OL+BDC, removing the expectancy term
changes Type I error by -0.005 to
+0.001 at the null cells (paired SE at most
0.003). It changes power by
-0.015 to +0.000 at the
alternative cells (paired SE at most 0.004).
A0 is therefore liberal because it has no residual correlation
structure.

\textbf{The residual correlation also carries the change in power.} Pooled
over the six reference alternatives, adding \texttt{corCAR1} to E0n changes
power by -0.124 (paired SE
0.013), and adding it to E1nc by
-0.129 (0.013). At
\(t_{1/2} = 1\) week it lowers power in all 12 alternative
cells, by 0.040 to 0.193.
Part of this loss is the removal of the excess rejections of a liberal
test. There are 4 exceptions, all at
\(t_{1/2} = 0\) under Hybrid and OL+BDC. There \texttt{corCAR1} raises power by
0.007 to 0.042 (paired SE
0.003 to 0.013). The
gain exceeds two paired SE in
3 of these cells. The residual correlation makes the estimate more precise
in these designs. Under Hybrid at \(N = 35\) and \(t_{1/2} = 0\) the
empirical standard deviation of the estimate falls from
1.273 to
1.086. Under CO
the empirical standard deviation changes by at most
4
percent, and the reported standard error rises by
28
to
29
percent. Under CO the residual correlation therefore corrects only
the standard error.

\textbf{The residual correlation changes the estimate under carryover.} At
\(t_{1/2} = 1\) week and \(c_{bm} > 0\), adding \texttt{corCAR1} moves the mean
estimate toward zero by 12
to 16 percent under Hybrid
and OL+BDC and by 2 to
4 percent under CO. At \(t_{1/2} = 0\) the
change is at most
1
percent. The binary indicator does not represent carryover, so its
coefficient depends on how the fit weights the off-drug occasions. A
plausible mechanism is that generalized least squares under a serial
correlation gives less weight to an occasion that is strongly
correlated with its neighbors. The first off-drug occasions sit next to
on-drug ones, and they carry most of the residual effect. We did not
verify this mechanism.

Rung 1 therefore turns a liberal test into a conservative one. In the
base grid A1 rejects the null at 0.038 against
0.110 for A0. It also costs power. A1 has
0.563 against 0.691 for A0
(Figure \ref{fig:fig-ladder-power}). The comparison of power
is not like for like, because A0 rejects too often under the null.
Rungs 2 to 4 recover power with a correctly sized test.

\subsubsection{Rung 2: CR2 cluster-robust inference}\label{sec-rung2}

Rung 2 replaces the working-covariance standard error of A1 by a CR2
cluster-robust standard error with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom
(A1 to A2). The point estimates do not change. Across the null cells
of the factorial grid the working-covariance test rejects at about 0.037 on
average (Section \ref{sec-rung3} gives the range). We asked whether CR2 corrects it without disturbing
the ranking of the analyses. Table S3 diagnoses
the conservatism at four Hybrid null cells with 2,000 replicates each.
That count is enough to resolve the calibration ratio \(\kappa\) of
Section \ref{sec-cr2-method} (two-MCSE band around 0.05:
\([0.040, 0.060]\)).

The working-covariance test is conservative for both regressors at
every cell. It is most conservative with the exposure-weighted
predictor (\(\kappa\) 1.05 to 1.07, Type I error
0.033 to 0.037). The CR2 standard error brings
\(\kappa\) to within 0.10 of one and Type I error inside the band for
A2, A3 and A4 at all four cells. A4 rejects at
0.048 to 0.053 with \(\kappa\)
0.96 to 0.99, against
0.97 to 0.99 for A3. A0 rejects at
0.062 to 0.064 at all four cells.

Table S1 also reports Type I error at the six null cells of the base
grid. A1 is at 0.03 to 0.05 throughout. A2, A3 and A4 are
at 0.04 to 0.07, inside the
two-MCSE band at every cell except A2 at
OL+BDC, \(N = 35\)
(0.074, at its edge). A separate check
with 5,000 independent null datasets per cell, under this paper's
parameter set, gave A2
0.049 (MCSE
0.003) at \(N = 35\) and
0.056 (MCSE
0.003) at \(N = 70\) in OL+BDC
(\texttt{analysis/scripts/cr2-olbdc-check/}, output in
\texttt{analysis/data/cr2-olbdc-check/}). The 0.074 is therefore a Monte
Carlo excursion of a 500-replicate cell, not a small-sample failure of
CR2. The rate at \(N = 70\) is about two MCSE above 0.05, a small excess.
The median Satterthwaite degrees of freedom of the CR2 test were
12 at \(N = 35\) and
24 at \(N = 70\). So few
degrees of freedom widen the reference distribution. That is part of
the power cost of CR2 at small \(N\). A0, the starting analysis, rejects at 0.06 to 0.15. It is far outside the band under CO and OL+BDC and at its edge
under Hybrid.

-1.0 points (minimum

Rung 2 therefore fixes size. In the base grid CR2 moves the null

\subsubsection{Rung 4: choosing the half-life}\label{sec-rung4}

Rung 4 replaces the true half-life by the half-life that AIC selects
from the data, on the model without the interaction (Section
\ref{sec-g4}). We asked two questions. The first is whether the
selection recovers the power that the predictor obtains at the true
half-life. The second is what the selection step costs in size and in
standard-error calibration. A4 was fitted afterward to the stored
trials of families 1 to 5, so every comparison with A3 below is paired
on the same trials.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec-discussion}

\subsection{What each rung bought}\label{sec-bought}

The ladder was not planned in advance. Each rung answered the question
the previous one raised. The recommendation rests on the chain of
rungs and on the stress scenarios rather than on any single
comparison. Everything below is conditional on the correlation encoding
of the companion study, on one parameter set chosen for the
simulation core (Table \ref{tab:tab-params}), and on the Hybrid
design. The analysis model is
close to the conditional mean of that Gaussian DGP. Part of what the
rungs show is therefore that a correctly specified regressor
outperforms a misspecified one. The findings concern which choices cost
power or size under that DGP, not the DGP itself.

The direction of rung 3 depends on the encoding. The factorial grid
also ran the mean-moderation encoding, in which the biomarker shifts
the drug-response mean at on-drug visits only, so no moderation
persists after discontinuation. There the binary indicator is the
correctly specified regressor. At a one-week half-life exposure
weighting changed power by -6.6
points under Hybrid and -5.0
under OL+BDC, against +6.0 and
+5.5 under the correlation
encoding. Rung 3 therefore pays only when the moderation fades with
the drug effect. Which encoding holds for a given drug is an empirical
question that these trials have not yet answered.

Rung 1 showed that the starting analysis is liberal and that A1 is
conservative. A0 is liberal by a factor of two to three under the
crossover and OL+BDC designs and marginally liberal under Hybrid. It
differs from A1 in three respects at once. The isolation analyses of
Section \ref{sec-rung1} separate them. The missing residual
correlation accounts for the whole inflation. The software, the
degrees-of-freedom method, the expectancy term and the exclusion of
patients change size by at most
0.005.
A1 costs power against A0 in the base grid, but the power of A0 is
the power of a test that rejects too often.

Rung 2 showed that the working-covariance test is conservative by about a
quarter of its nominal size. The conservatism comes from a working
covariance that does not match the data. A CR2 cluster-robust standard
error with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom brings every analysis to
within about half a point of nominal size. It changes power by a mean
of +2.4 points under
Hybrid, +3.3 under
OL+BDC and -1.0 under the
crossover design.

Rung 3 showed that the choice of exposure representation is a question
of power, not validity. Only the representation that changes the
regressor carrying the interaction recovers any power. Even at the
true half-life, exposure weighting recovered only
15\% to 44\% of the loss at a one-week half-life under
Hybrid and 19\% to 25\% under OL+BDC. Most of the loss is
not recoverable by the analysis and has to be met by the design. The
exposure-weighted predictor was within Monte Carlo noise of the
binary indicator where it did not gain. The earlier finding that it
failed under a crossover design was a coding defect, and we withdraw
it. Under this DGP the exposure-weighted predictor at the true decay is
the correctly specified regressor. Rung 3 therefore measures the cost
of misspecifying the regressor, not a general superiority.

Rung 4 showed that the true half-life, which rung 3 gave the analyst, is
not needed. Choosing it by AIC on the model without the interaction
(A4) changes power against the oracle A3 by
-0.013 to +0.003 and size by
-0.002 to +0.002 across the six families. This
includes the crossover design, once the selection compares ML fits and
the predictor is coded correctly before first exposure. Rung 4 does not
show that the selection recovers the half-life. The selected value is
spread over the candidates (Table S4). The small power
cost means the exposure weight is insensitive to the half-life over
this range.

Selecting the half-life with the interaction in the selection model
instead raised size above the oracle's in every family (Section
\ref{sec-comparators}). That is why A4 leaves the term under test
out of the selection. The scenario grids confirmed the ordering across
sample size and effect size. Dropout of 10 to 30 percent of patients,
completely at random or dependent on baseline severity, changed
neither the size of the CR2 analyses nor their ordering. Only the heavier per-occasion convention, which removes up to
73 percent of the observations, collapsed power. A patient-specific
linear trend of 0.3 outcome units per week, unrelated to the biomarker,
left size and power unchanged.

Size was evaluated at \(N = 35\) and \(N = 70\). The working-covariance tests were
conservative at both, which points to a working covariance that does
not fit rather than to a small-sample effect. The small-sample
refinements of CR2 would matter less in larger trials, but the need for
a robust standard error would not.

\subsection{The recommended analysis for a Hybrid trial}\label{sec-recommend}

For a Hybrid aggregated N-of-1 trial with a biomarker that is
predictive only, simulated under the correlation encoding at
\(N \le 70\), we recommend A4:

\[\text{Sx}_{it} \sim bm_i + t + D_{bc,it}(\hat t^{a}_{1/2}) +
  bm_i{:}D_{bc,it}(\hat t^{a}_{1/2}),\]

with a patient random intercept and \texttt{corCAR1} residual correlation.
\(D_{bc,it}\) equals one on drug, \(2^{-t_{sd}/\hat t^{a}_{1/2}}\) off
drug after first exposure, and zero before first exposure.
\(\hat t^{a}_{1/2}\) is chosen by AIC over \(\{0.25, 0.5, 1, 2\}\) weeks
from ML fits of the model without the interaction,
\(\text{Sx}_{it} \sim bm_i + t + D_{bc,it}\). The model with the
interaction is then fitted by REML at that half-life. The interaction
is tested with a CR2 cluster-robust standard error and Satterthwaite
degrees of freedom. Two properties recommend it. It requires no
pharmacokinetic input from the analyst. Its size is within one paired
standard error of the oracle at the true half-life in every family,
because the half-life is chosen without reference to the term under
test. Two qualifications apply. Its
standard error under the alternative is still too small, by a few
percent in pooled families and by up to about
13
percent in single cells, and its interval covers about one point below nominal at
\(N = 70\) (Section \ref{sec-calibration}). With a predictive-only biomarker A2 to A4 hold
nominal size without the design-expectancy terms in every family
examined; the terms matter when the biomarker may also be prognostic,
which the companion paper (\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats_prognostic2026}{12}) examines. A
trial whose biomarker may be prognostic should follow that paper,
which pairs the selection rule of A4 with the expectancy terms.

The recommendation transfers to OL+BDC on the evidence of rungs 3
and 4 and the scenario cells, where A4 and A3 lead in the same
near-tie. Under the crossover design the analyses are within a few
points of one another. A4 has the lowest power of A1 to A4 there,
0.615 against 0.631 for
A1 on the base grid, and the binary indicator is as good a
choice. The recommendation is stated for the correlation encoding.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{limitations}

The numbers come from a single parameter set chosen for the
simulation core (Table \ref{tab:tab-params}). It is not calibrated to
data, and it differs from the starting values that the companion paper
uses. Its placebo-response SD is a fifth of the starting value, so the
placebo pathway is weak. Reruns of the main cells under the starting
values exist but are not reported here. That set has one trajectory shape per
component, one response SD vector, \(\rho = 0.7\) except in the \(\rho\)
grid, and \(c_1 = 0.2\). The recommendation comes from one design.
Size was evaluated at \(N = 35\) and \(N = 70\); behavior at larger samples
was not examined for a predictive-only biomarker. Absolute power values are
therefore illustrative. The ranking, conditional on design, is the
more portable result. The correlation encoding is itself an
unvalidated modeling assumption of this project. The analysis model
is close to its conditional mean. The study therefore cannot show
how the ranking would change under a DGP with a different mean
structure. Every biomarker simulated here is predictive only; a
biomarker that is also prognostic is examined in the companion paper
(\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats_prognostic2026}{12}). Selecting the half-life costs a few percent of
standard-error calibration under the alternative.
Coverage of the CR2 interval has been quantified only over the
cells of the supplementary study (Section \ref{sec-calibration}). A
selection-aware interval has not been evaluated. Dropout was examined at 10 to 30
percent of patients, monotone, with the MAR mechanism tied to
baseline severity. Baseline severity is not among the analysis
variables, so the mechanism is MAR given the analyzed data only
insofar as early outcomes proxy for it. Informative dropout related
to the outcome or to treatment response was not simulated. The
dropout block has no alternative cell without dropout under the same
code. Under the heavier per-occasion convention power collapsed for
every specification. The recommendation is therefore for trials that
retain most of their observations. The scenario grids use one
alternative effect size per scenario. The patient-specific slope
variance was simulated at one value, in one Hybrid cell, and with A3
and A4 only; A4 was also fitted with a random slope on time. Cells within a job share random-number streams.
Counts of cells inside a band are therefore not independent, and the
cross-job comparisons are unpaired. The calibration ratio \(\kappa\)
uses the mean rather than the root mean square of the standard
errors, which lowers it by 0.01 to 0.03 (Section
\ref{sec-cr2-method}). The
effect-size curve of
earlier drafts was not rerun under the corrected code. The
sensitivity checks vary one factor at a time, so they can miss
interactions between factors.

\subsection{Related approaches not evaluated}\label{sec-related}

Three N-of-1 analysis approaches sit outside the family examined.
Each would require substantial independent development to enter the
comparison. Tang and Landes (\citeproc{ref-tang2020ttests}{20}) propose formula-based
\(t\)-tests that correct a single-subject paired-difference test for
an estimated serial correlation. They have no biomarker-moderation
term. Schmid and Yang (\citeproc{ref-schmid2022bayesian}{21}) describe a Bayesian
hierarchical alternative with partial pooling of individual-level
treatment, trend and autocorrelation parameters, and discuss
extensions for carryover. Daza
(\citeproc{ref-daza2018causal}{22}) frames N-of-1 inference as a counterfactual
time-varying-treatment problem. In that framing carryover is
confounding to be controlled rather than a predictor to be modeled.
Chen and Chen (\citeproc{ref-chen2014}{3}) found that a paired \(t\)-test matched or
exceeded mixed models in simulated N-of-1 series with and without
carryover. Araujo, Julious and Senn (\citeproc{ref-araujo2016}{23}) describe the
variance components that a set of N-of-1 trials identifies. These
two papers motivated a paired-difference specification in earlier
drafts. On the corrected grid it trails the mixed-model analyses under
every design and is not pursued. The closest prior work
on an exponentially decaying exposure term is the carryover-adjusted
parametric model of Gärtner et al.~for aggregated N-of-1 trials,
with exponential wash-in and wash-out (\citeproc{ref-gartner2023}{6}). Its estimand is
the treatment effect, not the biomarker interaction. The biomarker
interaction is where this study's contribution lies. Blackston et
al. (\citeproc{ref-blackston2019}{5}) show inflated Type I error for aggregated N-of-1
analyses of the treatment effect that ignore moderate or strong
carryover. The interaction behaves differently. A1 ignores carryover
and is conservative, and the inflation of A0 comes from its missing
residual correlation (Section \ref{sec-e0}). For the interaction,
ignoring carryover costs power rather than validity. Percha et al.
(\citeproc{ref-percha2019}{4}) give design recommendations from simulated
single-series N-of-1 trials with carryover and wash-in. Zucker et
al. (\citeproc{ref-zucker2010}{24}) set out the methodology for combining N-of-1
trials into population estimates. The case against a single lagged
term as a carryover adjustment in crossover trials is classical
(\citeproc{ref-senn2002}{25}--\citeproc{ref-jones2014crossover}{27}).

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec-conclusions}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Rung 0 to 1: do not use the starting analysis as is.} It is liberal
  by two to three times nominal under the crossover and OL+BDC designs
  and marginally liberal under Hybrid. It differs from A1 in its
  residual correlation structure, an expectancy term and its software.
  A1 is conservative and costs power against A0 in the base grid.
\item
  \textbf{Rung 2: use a CR2 cluster-robust standard error.} The working-covariance
  test rejects at about 0.037 under the null. CR2 with Satterthwaite
  degrees of freedom brings every analysis to within about half a
  point of nominal size. It changes power by a mean of
  +2.4 points under
  Hybrid and +3.3 under
  OL+BDC, and by -1.0 under
  the crossover design.
\item
  \textbf{Rung 3: represent residual exposure in the regressor.} An
  exposure-weighted predictor at the true half-life recovers part of
  the power that carryover removes under the Hybrid and OL+BDC designs,
  15\% to 44\% and 19\% to 25\% at a one-week
  half-life. The rest has to be met by the design, for example with
  more participants. It is within Monte Carlo noise of the binary
  indicator where it does not gain.
\item
  \textbf{Rung 4: let the data choose the half-life, on the model without
  the interaction.} AIC selection over four candidates, comparing ML
  fits of the model without the interaction and refitting the
  interaction model at the selected half-life by REML, removes the
  need for the analyst to supply a half-life. Paired against the
  oracle at the true half-life, it changes size by
  -0.002 to +0.002 and power by
  -0.013 to +0.003 across the six families. The
  selected value is spread over the candidates, so the small power
  cost reflects the insensitivity of the predictor to the half-life
  rather than recovery of it. Leaving the interaction out of the
  selection keeps the size of the oracle; selecting with it in the
  model raised size in every family.
\item
  \textbf{Recommendation.} For a Hybrid aggregated N-of-1 trial with a
  biomarker known to be predictive only, simulated under the
  correlation encoding at \(N \le 70\), use A4: the exposure-weighted
  predictor at the half-life chosen by AIC on the model without the
  interaction, with CR2 inference. Its size is equivalent to the
  oracle's within \(\pm 0.01\) in the run with independent seeds and in
  4 of the other five families. Against A2, the binary indicator with
  CR2, A4 gains 1.3 points of power
  on average in the Hybrid cells, up to
  7.1 at long half-lives, and loses at
  most 1.0 point in the
  6 cells where it is behind. Among the analyses fitted in every family that do not
  need the true half-life, A4 is the most powerful one whose size is
  within Monte Carlo error of the oracle's. Under
  dropout of 10 to 30 percent of patients, completely at random or
  dependent on baseline severity, A4 keeps its size and, in all but
  one cell, its power advantage over the binary indicator. It should
  be accompanied by an
  effort to retain patients, because heavier missingness costs every
  analysis power.
\item
  \textbf{A biomarker of unknown role.} Whether a candidate biomarker is
  also prognostic is rarely known before a trial and is hard to test
  within it. A4 omits the design-expectancy terms, which protect the
  test when the biomarker is prognostic through a pathway that
  changes over time. The default for a biomarker of unknown role is
  therefore the analysis of the companion paper
  (\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats_prognostic2026}{12}). This paper supports the parts of that
  analysis it shares with A4: the exposure-weighted predictor, the
  choice of half-life on the model without the interaction, and the
  CR2 test.
\item
  \textbf{Limits.} The recommendation is conditional on the correlation
  encoding, on one parameter set (Table \ref{tab:tab-params}), on
  one design and on a biomarker that is predictive only. Size was
  evaluated at \(N = 35\) and \(N = 70\). The analysis model is close to
  the conditional mean of the DGP, so part of what the rungs show is
  that a correctly specified regressor outperforms a misspecified
  one.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Glossary}\label{glossary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Glossary}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Analysis codes (A0 to A4).} The five analyses of Table
  \ref{tab:tab-spec-summary}, numbered by rung. The companion papers
  and earlier drafts call them E0, G1, G6, G8 and G10. Stored data
  files label them \texttt{E0}, \texttt{E1}, \texttt{E1cr2}, \texttt{E2cr2} and \texttt{E7null\_cr2}.
\item
  \textbf{A4.} The recommended analysis: the exposure-weighted predictor at
  the analysis half-life chosen by AIC on the model without the
  interaction, refitted by REML with the interaction and tested with
  CR2 (G10 in the companion papers).
\item
  \textbf{Oracle benchmark (A3).} An analysis that uses the true
  data-generating half-life. It is not available to an analyst and is
  not a candidate analysis. It shows what the selection step costs.
\item
  \textbf{Slope variance.} A data-generating option added for the
  referee-variant runs (\texttt{sd\_slope}). It adds a patient-specific linear
  trend, unrelated to the biomarker, with the stated standard
  deviation per week.
\item
  \textbf{Isolation analyses (E0n, E0nc, E1nc).} Three analyses that change
  one difference between A0 and A1 at a time. E0n refits the formula of
  A0 with \texttt{nlme} and no residual correlation. E0nc adds \texttt{corCAR1} to
  E0n. E1nc uses the formula of A1 with \texttt{nlme} and no residual
  correlation. A1 is E1nc with \texttt{corCAR1}.
\item
  \textbf{Ladder.} The ordered set of analyses of Table
  \ref{tab:tab-spec-summary}, from the starting analysis (rung 0) to the
  analysis with an AIC-selected half-life and CR2 inference (rung 4).
\item
  \textbf{Rung.} One analysis of the ladder. Each rung changes one thing
  from the rung before, except the step from rung 0 to rung 1.
\item
  \textbf{Scenario family.} A set of simulation cells that share one
  departure from the base grid. The families are numbered 1 to 5,
  with family S for the Hybrid run with independent seeds (Table
  \ref{tab:tab-families}).
\item
  \textbf{Biomarker-response correlation (\(c_{bm}\)).} The on-drug
  correlation between the biomarker and the drug-response component.
  It is the interaction estimand of this study. Stored data and code
  refer to it as \texttt{c\_bm} or \texttt{coupling}.
\item
  \textbf{Carryover half-life (\(t_{1/2}\)).} The time after
  discontinuation at which the residual drug effect, and under the
  correlation encoding the biomarker-response correlation, has
  fallen to half its on-drug value. The analysis half-life
  \(t^{a}_{1/2}\) is the value the analyst assumes or selects.
\item
  \textbf{Common random numbers.} Fitting every specification to the same
  simulated dataset within a cell, so that differences between
  specifications are paired and have reduced Monte Carlo variance.
\item
  \textbf{CO (crossover).} The two-period blinded crossover design of
  Section \ref{sec-designs}.
\item
  \textbf{Cluster-robust standard error (CR2).} The bias-reduced
  cluster-robust sandwich standard error of Bell and McCaffrey, which
  estimates the variance of the interaction coefficient from
  between-patient variation. It remains valid when the working
  covariance is misspecified. It is paired with Satterthwaite degrees
  of freedom.
\item
  \textbf{Working-covariance standard error.} The standard error that
  \texttt{nlme::lme} reports, computed under the working covariance: a
  patient random intercept with \texttt{corCAR1} residual correlation. It is
  correct only if that covariance is the true one. A test that uses
  it is called a working-covariance test. Each cluster-robust analysis
  fits the same model and changes only the standard error, the
  reference distribution and the \(p\)-value.
\item
  \textbf{Correlation encoding of the interaction.} The data-generating
  convention in which the biomarker-treatment interaction is a
  correlation between the biomarker and the drug response that is
  present on drug and absent or decayed off drug. Code and data
  files label it \texttt{mvn} or covariance moderation.
\item
  \textbf{\(D_{bc}\).} The exposure-weighted predictor: one on drug,
  \(2^{-t_{sd}/t^{a}_{1/2}}\) off drug after first exposure, zero
  before first exposure.
\item
  \textbf{Expectancy (\(D_e\), \(\eta_t\)).} The participant's probability of
  being on drug by design: one during open-label periods and one
  half during blinded periods. It scales the placebo-response
  component.
\item
  \textbf{Hybrid design.} Open-label titration, blinded discontinuation,
  then a brief crossover (Section \ref{sec-designs}).
\item
  \textbf{\(\kappa\) (calibration ratio).} The mean reported standard error
  (working-covariance or cluster-robust, whichever the analysis uses)
  divided by the empirical standard deviation of the estimates across
  replicates. A value of one is correctly calibrated.
\item
  \textbf{MCSE.} Monte Carlo standard error, the standard error of a
  simulation estimate that arises from the finite number of
  replicates.
\item
  \textbf{Natural-history component (TV).} The latent component of the
  outcome that follows the untreated time course.
\item
  \textbf{OL+BDC.} Open-label titration followed by blinded discontinuation
  (Section \ref{sec-designs}).
\item
  \textbf{Placebo-response component (PB).} The latent component of the
  outcome that responds to expectancy rather than to the drug.
\item
  \textbf{Predictive-only biomarker.} A biomarker that changes the drug
  response and nothing else: it is uncorrelated with baseline
  severity, the natural-history component and the placebo-response
  component. Every cell of this study has such a biomarker.
\item
  \textbf{Recommended DGP} (\texttt{refined} in code and data labels). The
  data-generating process of the companion study, in which the
  biomarker-response correlation decays after discontinuation, the
  within-component correlation is separable AR(1), carryover decays
  from the last on-drug mean, and a non-positive-definite covariance
  matrix stops the simulation.
\item
  \textbf{Satterthwaite degrees of freedom.} An approximate degrees of
  freedom for the \(t\) reference distribution of a cluster-robust
  test, computed from the variance of the variance estimate.
\item
  \textbf{Separable AR(1) structure.} The same first-order autoregressive
  correlation for every component across occasions, with a constant
  between-component correlation at every lag.
\item
  \textbf{Starting analysis (A0).} The analysis published with the
  starting DGP: \texttt{lme4::lmer} with a random intercept, no residual
  correlation structure, a binary on-drug indicator, and Satterthwaite
  \(p\)-values. Paper 01 calls it the binary-indicator analysis.
\item
  \textbf{Starting DGP.} The data-generating process of Hendrickson et al.
  (2020), the starting point of the companion study.
\item
  \textbf{Supplementary study.} A later simulation study that added the
  shape, half-life, serial-correlation and dropout cells of families 2
  to 4. Code and data files label it \texttt{gap-fill}.
\item
  \textbf{Two-MCSE band.} The interval of plus or minus two Monte Carlo
  standard errors around the nominal 0.05 rejection rate, which is
  \([0.031, 0.069]\) at 500 replicates, \([0.036, 0.064]\) at 1,000 and
  \([0.040, 0.060]\) at 2,000.
\end{itemize}

\texttt{simulation-core.R}. Specifications are stored under \texttt{E}-prefixed
codes (\texttt{E1}, \texttt{E2}, \texttt{E3}, \texttt{E7}, \texttt{E9} and their \texttt{cr2} variants, and
\texttt{E7null\_cr2}) in every data file. This manuscript displays them as
A0 to A4 at render time through \texttt{to\_a()} in \texttt{spec-labels.R}; the
companion papers display them as G codes. Output archived before
2026-07-31 stores the specifications as \texttt{A1}, \texttt{A2}, \texttt{A3}. Those legacy
stored codes are unrelated to the A0 to A4 labels of this manuscript.
The migration is documented in \texttt{analysis/report/NOTATION.md}. The
exploratory sensitivity blocks of earlier drafts and their figures are
documented in the \texttt{archive/} directory of this manuscript, including
the earlier supplement (\texttt{archive/supplement-2026-10-21.Rmd}). The scripts that
produced them are retained under the same directory as above.
