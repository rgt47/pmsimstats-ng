\section*{Abstract}\label{abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

\textbf{Background.} Simulation studies of a predictive biomarker in
aggregated N-of-1 trials must state how the biomarker-treatment
interaction enters the data-generating process (DGP). Two encodings
are in use. The mean-shift encoding adds a biomarker-proportional
shift to the drug-response component on drug. The correlation
encoding makes the biomarker-response correlation present on drug and
absent off drug. We study a DGP with independent weights on both.

\textbf{Methods.} We derived the joint law of the dual-channel DGP, the
conditional slope of the outcome on the biomarker at every visit, and
the population value of the fitted interaction coefficient. We then
simulated a \(3 \times 3\) grid of channel weights in three designs and
four carryover half-lives, at a total of 70 participants, with 1000
replicates per cell. Common random numbers paired every weight cell
within a replicate. A validation gate required the boundary cells to
reproduce the single-encoding DGPs draw for draw.

\textbf{Results.} The boundary cells reproduced the single-encoding DGPs exactly in 1,800 paired comparisons. At zero half-life the fitted coefficient was additive in the two weights. Combined power exceeded the probability-scale additive benchmark by 0.22 to 0.34. Doubling one weight produced the same excess, 0.21 to 0.35. A benchmark that adds the two contributions on the probit scale, with the critical value calibrated to the observed size, matched every combined cell within two MCSE. At a half-life of 1 week and weight 0.45, Hybrid power was 0.818 (MCSE 0.012) under the mean-shift encoding. It was 0.723 (MCSE 0.014) under the correlation encoding. The mean-shift estimate exceeded its nominal value by up to 20 percent.

\textbf{Conclusions.} Under a normally distributed biomarker, the two
channels act on the same quantity, the conditional mean slope. They
are two parameterizations of one Gaussian model, not two mechanisms.
They differ only in how much interaction persists off drug and in a
small reduction of on-drug residual variance. The dual-channel DGP is
useful as a two-parameter family of off-drug interaction profiles.
A genuinely distinct second channel would require a variance that
depends on the biomarker or a responder mixture.

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Hendrickson et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendrickson2020}{1}} supplied the design machinery
on which this series builds. That work introduced the Hybrid design,
the decomposition of the outcome into three response components, and
the correlation encoding of the biomarker-treatment interaction. A
companion paper extends that DGP and describes the extensions\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper01}{2}}. We call the result the extended DGP and the
DGP of Hendrickson et al.~the original DGP.

A simulation study of power for a predictive biomarker has to say
how the interaction is generated. The usual convention in trial
simulation is the mean-shift encoding. The treatment effect is
scaled by the biomarker in the conditional mean. The original DGP
uses the correlation encoding instead. The interaction is a
correlation between the biomarker and the drug-response component
that is present on drug and absent off drug. The moderator framework
of Kraemer et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-kraemer2002}{3}} and the PATH statement\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-kent2020path}{4}} describe the estimand. Neither fixes the generating
mechanism.

This paper studies a DGP with both encodings active at once, each
with its own weight. We call it the dual-channel architecture. Either
encoding is recovered by setting the other weight to zero. An earlier
draft of this paper claimed two results. The first was that the two
channels combine super-additively in power. The second was that the
two channels are distinct mechanisms that act on different moments of
the response. Neither claim holds. This version replaces both.

The paper makes four contributions. First, we show that under a
normally distributed biomarker the dual-channel DGP is one
multivariate normal law. Both channels act on the conditional mean
slope of the drug-response component on the biomarker. Neither makes
the conditional variance depend on the biomarker (Section 2). Second,
we derive the slope profile at every visit, off-drug visits
included, and its projection onto the fitted interaction coefficient
(Section 3). The two weights enter that coefficient additively, with
coefficients that are equal only when there is no carryover. Third,
we show that the excess of combined power over the
probability-scale additive benchmark is a property of the power
curve. A single channel whose weight is doubled shows the same excess
(Section 4). Fourth, we report a corrected simulation under the
extended DGP. It passes a validation gate that the earlier run lacked.

\section{The dual-channel architecture}\label{sec-def}

\subsection{Definition}\label{definition}

Let \(B_i\) be the biomarker of participant \(i\), with population mean
\(\mu_{bm}\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_{bm}\). Write
\(b_i = (B_i - \mu_{bm})/\sigma_{bm}\) for the biomarker standardized
at its population values. The outcome at visit \(t\) is
\(Y_{it} = \mathrm{BL}_i - TV_{it} - PB_{it} - BR_{it}\). Here
\(\mathrm{BL}_i\) is the baseline level, \(TV_{it}\) the natural-history
component, \(PB_{it}\) the placebo-response component and \(BR_{it}\)
the drug-response component. The three components are reductions in
symptom severity, so an increase in any of them lowers \(Y_{it}\).
Interaction coefficients are therefore negative, and the channel
weights are positive (NOTATION.md, rule 5).

The extended DGP draws \((B_i, \mathrm{BL}_i, TV_{i\cdot},
PB_{i\cdot}, BR_{i\cdot})\) from one multivariate normal law. Each
component has the same first-order autoregressive correlation,
\(\rho = 0.7\) per week. Two components correlate at
\(c_1 \rho^{|w_t - w_s|}\) with \(c_1 = 0.2\), where \(w_t\) is the
calendar week of visit \(t\). The mean of \(BR_{it}\) follows a modified
Gompertz curve in the time on drug. Off drug, it decays from the last
on-drug mean at the carryover half-life \(t_{1/2}\). A covariance matrix
that is not positive definite stops the simulation.

The dual-channel architecture adds two weights to this law.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The \textbf{correlation channel}, weight \(c_{bm,B}\), sets
  \(\mathrm{Cor}(B_i, BR_{it}) = c_{bm,B}\, g_t\). The profile \(g_t\)
  is 1 on drug, \(\phi_t = 2^{-t_{sd}/t_{1/2}}\) off drug after first
  exposure, and 0 before first exposure. Here \(t_{sd}\) is the time
  since discontinuation.
\item
  The \textbf{mean-shift channel}, weight \(c_{bm,A}\), adds
  \(c_{bm,A}\, b_i\, \sigma_{BR}\, D_{it}\) to \(BR_{it}\) after the
  draw. Here \(D_{it}\) is 1 on drug and 0 off drug, and \(\sigma_{BR}\)
  is the standard deviation of the drug-response component.
\end{itemize}

The two weights are calibrated to a common scale (NOTATION.md,
rule 2). One unit of either weight moves the on-drug conditional mean
of \(BR_{it}\) by \(\sigma_{BR}\) per standard deviation of the
biomarker. The code labels are \texttt{combined} for the dual-channel
architecture, \texttt{mean\_moderation} for the mean-shift encoding alone and
\texttt{mvn} for the correlation encoding alone. The weights are \texttt{c.bm\_a}
and \texttt{c.bm\_b}.

\subsection{One Gaussian law, one conditional-mean slope}\label{sec-gauss}

The mean shift is linear in \(b_i\), and \(b_i\) is normal. The
dual-channel DGP is therefore a single multivariate normal law. Its
conditional moments given the biomarker follow from standard
regression algebra. At visit \(t\),
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[BR_{it} \mid b_i] = \mu_{BR,t}
  + \bigl(c_{bm,A}\, D_{it} + c_{bm,B}\, g_t\bigr)\,\sigma_{BR}\, b_i,
\label{eq:condmean}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Var}[BR_{it} \mid b_i] = \sigma_{BR}^2
  \bigl(1 - c_{bm,B}^2\, g_t^2\bigr).
\label{eq:condvar}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:condmean} shows that both channels act on the
conditional mean. Equation \eqref{eq:condvar} shows that only the
correlation channel changes the conditional variance, and that it
lowers it. The mean shift is a fixed function of \(b_i\), so it adds no
variance once the biomarker is given. Its contribution
\(c_{bm,A}^2\sigma_{BR}^2\) to the marginal variance of \(BR_{it}\)
disappears on conditioning. Neither conditional variance depends on
\(b_i\). The package source states the on-drug equivalence of the two
channels in a comment (\texttt{R/generateData.R}, lines 189 to 195).

At the parameters used here, \(\sigma_{BR} = 8\) points. The
on-drug conditional variance of \(BR_{it}\) falls from
\(64\) to \(51.0\) at
\(c_{bm,B} = 0.45\). The outcome sums four components, so the effect
on \(\mathrm{Var}(Y_{it} \mid b_i)\) is smaller. At the on-drug Hybrid
visits the ratio is 0.978
to 0.982.

Three consequences follow.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  On drug, the two channels are observationally the same. A pair
  \((c_{bm,A}, c_{bm,B})\) gives the on-drug slope
  \((c_{bm,A} + c_{bm,B})\,\sigma_{BR}\) and differs from another
  pair with the same sum only through the small variance reduction
  above.
\item
  Off drug, they differ. The mean shift is applied at on-drug
  visits only, so the mean-shift channel's interaction has no
  carryover. The correlation channel's interaction decays with
  \(\phi_t\). The drug-response mean itself carries over under both.
\item
  Setting \(c_{bm,B} = 0\) gives the mean-shift encoding exactly.
  Setting \(c_{bm,A} = 0\) gives the correlation encoding exactly. The
  simulation uses this as a validation gate (Section
  \ref{sec-gate}).
\end{enumerate}

Only \(c_{bm,B}\) is bounded by positive definiteness. The weight
\(c_{bm,A}\) has no feasibility bound. At the parameters used here,
every matrix with \(c_{bm,B} \le 0.45\) was positive definite in every
design and half-life. The smallest eigenvalue over all
108 design, half-life and weight combinations
was 2.87.

\subsection{Relation to mixture and location-scale models}\label{relation-to-mixture-and-location-scale-models}

An earlier draft described the two channels as distinct biological
mechanisms. It called the mean shift ``pharmacodynamic scaling'' and the
correlation ``co-regulatory'' activity. It also said the correlation
channel carries information through the class-conditional dispersion
\(\mathrm{Var}[BR \mid B]\). Section \ref{sec-gauss} shows that, as
specified, both channels move the conditional mean and neither makes
the dispersion depend on the biomarker. The two descriptions are two
readings of one model. They are not separable mechanisms within it.

The earlier draft also called the combined form the mean- and
covariance-structure model of Arminger et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-arminger1999}{5}}. That
paper concerns finite mixtures of conditional mean- and
covariance-structure models. Verbeke and Lesaffre\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-verbekelesaffre1996}{6}} study a mixture distribution for the random
effects of a linear mixed model. The dual-channel DGP has no mixture
component, so neither paper describes it. Both describe the kind of
extension that would create a second channel. A responder mixture
with a biomarker-dependent class probability is one such extension.
The latent-class companion paper develops it\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper03}{7}}.
A mixed-effects location-scale model, in which a covariate enters the
within-participant variance, is another\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hedeker2008}{8}}. Under either,
a drug could change the spread of response as well as its mean.

The variance-ratio literature tests that second moment directly.
Under a constant treatment effect, treated and control arms have the
same outcome variance\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-senn2016mastering}{9}}. Reviews of parallel-group
trials\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-cortes2018}{10}}, antipsychotic trials\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-winkelbeiner2019}{11}} and
antidepressant trials\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ploderl2019}{12}} found treated-arm variances
equal to or smaller than control-arm variances. The correlation
encoding predicts exactly that pattern. It lowers the on-drug
conditional variance and leaves the marginal variance unchanged. The
mean-shift encoding raises the marginal on-drug variance by
\(c_{bm,A}^2\sigma_{BR}^2\). Neither encoding produces a
biomarker-dependent variance.

\section{Analytical results}\label{sec-analytic}

\subsection{The slope profile over all visits}\label{the-slope-profile-over-all-visits}

Write the outcome vector of a participant on path \(p\) as
\(\mathbf{Y}_i = (\mathrm{BL}_i, Y_{i1}, \ldots, Y_{iT})^\top\). The
baseline visit is included, because the analysis model includes it.
Conditional on the biomarker,
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Y}_i \mid B_i] = \mathbf{m}_p
  + \mathbf{s}_p\,(B_i - \mu_{bm}),
\qquad
s_{pt} = -\bigl(c_{bm,A}\, D_{pt} + c_{bm,B}\, g_{pt}\bigr)
  \frac{\sigma_{BR}}{\sigma_{bm}},
\label{eq:slope}
\end{equation}
with \(s_{p0} = 0\) at baseline. The conditional covariance
\(\mathbf{V}_p = \mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{Y}_i \mid B_i)\) does not depend
on \(B_i\) or on \(c_{bm,A}\). Both follow from the covariance matrix the
DGP builds. Script \texttt{analysis/scripts/dgp-combined/03-analytic-projection.R}
computes them.

\subsection{The fitted interaction coefficient}\label{the-fitted-interaction-coefficient}

The analysis fits \(Y_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_B B_i + \beta_T w_t +
\beta_D D_{bc,it} + \beta_{bm:D} B_i D_{bc,it} + u_i +
\varepsilon_{it}\), where \(D_{bc,it}\) is the exposure-decayed drug
indicator at the true half-life (Section \ref{sec-methods}). The
biomarker enters through \(\beta_B\) and \(\beta_{bm:D}\). Their
population values are the projection of the slope profile
\(\mathbf{s}_p\) onto the columns \((1, D_{bc,pt})\), pooled over paths.
With generalized least squares under the true conditional covariance,
\begin{equation}
\begin{pmatrix} \beta_B \\ \beta_{bm:D} \end{pmatrix}
= \Bigl(\sum_p n_p\, \mathbf{X}_p^\top \mathbf{V}_p^{-1}
  \mathbf{X}_p\Bigr)^{-1}
  \sum_p n_p\, \mathbf{X}_p^\top \mathbf{V}_p^{-1}\, \mathbf{s}_p,
\qquad \mathbf{X}_p = (\mathbf{1}, \mathbf{D}_{bc,p}),
\label{eq:proj}
\end{equation}
where \(n_p\) is the number of participants on path \(p\).

Two cases follow from Equation \eqref{eq:slope}.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{The correlation channel.} At the true half-life, \(g_{pt}\) equals
  \(D_{bc,pt}\) at every visit. Its slope profile lies in the span of
  \(\mathbf{X}_p\), so the projection is exact for any weighting. Its
  contribution to \(\beta_{bm:D}\) is \(-c_{bm,B}\,\sigma_{BR}/\sigma_{bm}\)
  in every design and at every half-life. At the parameters used
  here that is \(-0.2344\) at \(c_{bm,B} = 0.45\).
\item
  \emph{The mean-shift channel.} Its profile is \(D_{pt}\), which is 0 at
  off-drug visits where \(D_{bc,pt}\) is positive. At \(t_{1/2} = 0\) the
  two coincide and the projection is again exact. At \(t_{1/2} > 0\) the
  profile is not in the span of \(\mathbf{X}_p\). The projection then
  depends on the weighting and exceeds the nominal value in absolute
  size. The analysis model reads a step-shaped interaction through an
  exposure-decayed regressor.
\end{itemize}

The coefficient is therefore linear in the two weights,
\begin{equation}
\beta_{bm:D} = c_{bm,A}\,\kappa_A + c_{bm,B}\,\kappa_B,
\qquad \kappa_B = -\sigma_{BR}/\sigma_{bm},
\label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
with \(\kappa_A = \kappa_B\) only at \(t_{1/2} = 0\). Under the true
conditional covariance, the mean-shift channel's coefficient exceeds
the nominal value by
1 percent in the crossover design,
8 percent in the Hybrid design and
7 percent in the OL+BDC design at
\(t_{1/2} = 1\) week. The departure from exact linearity caused by the
dependence of \(\mathbf{V}_p\) on \(c_{bm,B}\) was at most
9.2e-06 over the grid.
The fitted model uses an estimated random-intercept and continuous
AR(1) working covariance, not \(\mathbf{V}_p\), so its mean-shift
overshoot can differ. Section \ref{sec-est} reports it.

\subsection{Identifiability}\label{identifiability}

At \(t_{1/2} = 0\), any two weight pairs with the same sum give the same
fitted coefficient. The two weights are not separately identifiable
from it. At \(t_{1/2} > 0\) they enter with different coefficients, but
one coefficient still cannot identify two weights. The data carry the
difference in the off-drug visits. A model that estimates the off-drug
slope separately could identify the off-drug profile, and with it the
split between the channels. An example is a specification with a
biomarker-by-lagged-exposure term. We did not fit such a model.

\subsection{Noncentrality and the scale of additivity}\label{noncentrality-and-the-scale-of-additivity}

The GLS standard error of \(\beta_{bm:D}\) under the true conditional
covariance is
\(\{[(\sigma_{bm}^2 \sum_p n_p \mathbf{X}_p^\top \mathbf{V}_p^{-1}
\mathbf{X}_p)^{-1}]_{22}\}^{1/2}\). The noncentrality of the test is
\(\Lambda = |\beta_{bm:D}| / \mathrm{SE}\). By Equation
\eqref{eq:kappa}, \(\Lambda\) is nearly linear in the two weights. It is
not exactly linear, because \(\mathbf{V}_p\) depends on \(c_{bm,B}\).
Power \(\pi(\Lambda) \approx \Phi(\Lambda - z_{0.975})\) is not linear
in \(\Lambda\). It is convex below \(\pi = 0.5\) and concave above. A
probability-scale benchmark \(p(a, 0) + p(0, b) - p(0, 0)\) therefore
understates the combined power whenever the combined noncentrality
lies in the convex region. It overstates it in the concave region,
and it can exceed 1. This holds for any two additive contributions to
\(\Lambda\). It holds equally when one channel's weight is doubled. The
appropriate benchmark adds the two contributions on the
noncentrality scale. Section \ref{sec-additivity} applies it.

\section{Simulation study}\label{sec-sim}

We report the study under the ADEMP framework\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morris2019simulation}{13}}. The study is exploratory.

\subsection{Aims}\label{aims}

The aims were (1) to check that the boundary cells reproduce the
single-encoding DGPs, (2) to describe power over the weight grid under
the extended DGP, (3) to test additivity on the scale of the fitted
coefficient and of the noncentrality, and (4) to compare the
equal-total weight pairs, which differ only in their off-drug
profile.

\subsection{Data-generating mechanisms}\label{data-generating-mechanisms}

We used the extended DGP of Section \ref{sec-def} with the
prazosin-PTSD calibration of the companion paper. The drug-response
standard deviation was \(\sigma_{BR} = 8\) points and the
biomarker standard deviation \(\sigma_{bm} = 15.36\)
units. The factors were the following.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Channel weights \(c_{bm,A}, c_{bm,B} \in \{0, 0.225, 0.45\}\), nine
  cells. The anti-diagonal holds the three equal-total pairs with
  \(c_{bm,A} + c_{bm,B} = 0.45\). They are the points
  \(\omega \in \{0, 0.5, 1\}\) of the mixing parameterization
  \(c_{bm,A} = (1 - \omega)\,c\), \(c_{bm,B} = \omega\, c\) at a total
  weight \(c = 0.45\).
\item
  Three designs. The crossover design (CO) has two periods and two
  randomization paths. Open-label titration followed by blinded
  discontinuation (OL+BDC) has two paths. The Hybrid design
  (open-label titration, blinded discontinuation, then a brief
  crossover) has four paths. Each design has eight post-baseline
  visits.
\item
  Carryover half-life \(t_{1/2} \in \{0, 0.5, 1\}\) week. The two
  positive values are behavioral scenarios. The pharmacological range
  of prazosin is 0.1 to 0.2 week, so a separate pass of the same driver
  added \(t_{1/2} = 0.2\) week (Section \ref{sec-g9}).
\item
  A total of \(N = 70\) participants, allocated across the paths of each
  design (35 per path in CO and OL+BDC, 18, 18, 17 and 17 in Hybrid).
\end{itemize}

Each replicate set one seed, determined by the design, half-life and
replicate index, before generating the data of every weight cell.
Since the Cholesky factor changes with \(c_{bm,B}\) but the normal
deviates do not, the nine cells of a replicate share their random
numbers. Contrasts between weight cells are paired. Designs and
half-lives use independent streams. Every simulated covariance matrix
was positive definite, so no replicate was refused.

\subsection{Estimand}\label{estimand}

The estimand is the biomarker-by-exposure coefficient
\(\beta_{bm:D}\) of the analysis model, in outcome points per biomarker
unit. Its population value is given by Equation \eqref{eq:kappa}.
Power is the probability of rejecting \(\beta_{bm:D} = 0\) at two-sided
\(\alpha = 0.05\).

\subsection{Methods}\label{sec-methods}

Each simulated trial was analyzed with the following model and two
standard errors.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{E2.} \texttt{Sx\ \textasciitilde{}\ bm\ +\ t\ +\ Dbc\ +\ bm:Dbc} fitted by \texttt{nlme::lme} with REML,
  a random intercept per participant and a continuous AR(1) residual
  correlation, \texttt{corCAR1(form\ =\ \textasciitilde{}\ t\ \textbar{}\ ptID)}. \(D_{bc,it}\) is built at
  the true half-life. It is 1 on drug, \(2^{-t_{sd}/t_{1/2}}\) off drug
  after exposure and 0 before first exposure. The test uses the
  model-based standard error and the \texttt{lme} \(t\) distribution.
\item
  \textbf{E2cr2.} The same fit, with the CR2 cluster-robust standard error\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-bell2002bias}{14}} and a Satterthwaite \(t\) test\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-pustejovsky2018}{15}},
  from \texttt{clubSandwich}. Paper 02 labels it G8\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper02}{16}}.
\end{itemize}

The analysis data include the baseline visit, with \(t = 0\),
\(D_{bc} = 0\) and outcome \(\mathrm{BL}_i\), followed by the eight
post-baseline visits. A fit that failed was recorded as not converged.
No fallback model was fitted.

The program's current recommendation for the interaction is G9, the
E2 form with the half-life chosen by AIC and a CR2 test, or F1 at a
fixed analysis half-life\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper02}{16}}. G9 costs about eight
E2 fits per trial. We fitted it to a subset of the trials, the first
200 replicates of four cells per design and half-life, and compared it
with E2cr2 on the same trials (Section \ref{sec-g9}). We did not fit
F1. Its expectancy term is not identifiable in the CO design, whose
design expectancy is constant.

\subsection{Performance measures}\label{performance-measures}

For every cell we report the rejection rate \(\hat\pi\) with
\(\mathrm{MCSE} = \{\hat\pi(1-\hat\pi)/n_{\text{reps}}\}^{1/2}\), the mean
of the estimate, its empirical standard deviation, and the ratio
\(\kappa\) of the mean model-based standard error to that standard
deviation. Contrasts between weight cells of the same design and
half-life are paired. Their MCSE is the standard deviation of the
contrast over 2000 bootstrap resamples of the replicates. Contrasts
across designs or half-lives combine the two cell MCSEs in quadrature.

\subsection{Validation gate}\label{sec-gate}

For the first 50 replicates of every design and
half-life, the driver regenerated the four single-channel boundary
cells under the single-encoding DGPs from the same seeds. That gave
1,800 comparisons. The mean-shift
encoding with weight \(a\) was compared with the cell \((a, 0)\), and the
correlation encoding with weight \(b\) with the cell \((0, b)\). The
generated data were identical in every comparison, with a largest
absolute difference of 0.
The fitted E2 estimates were identical too, with a largest difference
of 0. The
gate passed. The boundary cells of the grid are the two single-encoding
DGPs of this driver exactly. Of the 216,000
E2 and E2cr2 fits, 215,968 converged.

\subsection{The fitted coefficient}\label{sec-est}

At \(t_{1/2} = 0\) the fitted coefficient was additive in the two
weights, replicate by replicate. The paired interaction contrast
\(\hat\beta(0.45, 0.45) - \hat\beta(0.45, 0) - \hat\beta(0, 0.45) +
\hat\beta(0, 0)\) averaged
1.4e-11
or less in absolute value. The reason is exact. At \(t_{1/2} = 0\) the
mean shift is a linear combination of the fixed-effect columns
\(B_i D_{bc,it}\) and \(D_{bc,it}\). Adding it changes neither the REML
variance estimates nor anything but the interaction estimate. At
\(t_{1/2} > 0\) the contrast was at most
1.3e-04, against
estimates near 0.23 in size.

The two weights did not have equal coefficients at \(t_{1/2} > 0\). The
correlation channel's mean estimate stayed within
4 percent of its nominal value in every design and
half-life. The
mean-shift channel's estimate at weight 0.45 and \(t_{1/2} = 1\) week
exceeded the nominal \(-0.234\) by
2 percent in CO,
15 percent in Hybrid and
20 percent in OL+BDC. The MCSE of each mean
estimate was about 0.003 or less.
The GLS projection under the true conditional covariance gave
1,
8 and
7 percent (Section
\ref{sec-analytic}). The fitted model weights the off-drug visits
differently from the true covariance. Its overshoot was roughly twice
the projection's in Hybrid and three times in OL+BDC. The 2026-10-28
internal review reported 21 to 39 percent for the earlier run, which
lacked the baseline visit and used the original carryover rule. The
direction agrees, and the size depends on the analysis data.

The empirical standard deviation of the E2 estimate exceeded the GLS
standard error under the true covariance in every cell. The ratio of
the two ranged from 1.08 to 1.26.
The working covariance of
the analysis model is a random intercept with continuous AR(1)
errors. The DGP has four correlated components, so the fitted
estimator is less efficient than GLS. The GLS noncentrality therefore
overstates the attainable power and serves as an upper reference only.

\subsection{Additivity of power}\label{sec-additivity}

Table \ref{tab:additivity} compares the combined cell
\((0.225, 0.225)\) with three benchmarks. The first is the
probability-scale benchmark \(p(0.225, 0) + p(0, 0.225) - p(0, 0)\).
The second is the probit benchmark of the 2026-10-28 internal review.
It maps each single-channel power to a noncentrality,
\(\hat\Lambda = \Phi^{-1}(\hat\pi) + 1.96\), adds the two and maps back,
\(\Phi(\hat\Lambda_A + \hat\Lambda_B - 1.96)\). The third is the same
benchmark with the critical value calibrated to the observed null
rejection rate, \(z = \Phi^{-1}(1 - \hat\pi_0/2)\). It corrects for a
test whose size is not 0.05. The table also gives the within-channel
control. It compares \(p(0.45, 0)\) with \(2p(0.225, 0) - p(0, 0)\) for
the mean-shift channel and the analogous quantity for the correlation
channel.

The combined cell exceeded the probability-scale benchmark by
0.221 to 0.339 under E2. The single channels
doubled showed the same excess, 0.210 to 0.337 for the mean-shift
channel and 0.243 to 0.349 for the correlation channel. The
excess is therefore a property of the power curve, not of combining
two channels. At \((0.45, 0.45)\) the probability-scale benchmark
exceeded 1 in every design and half-life. The combined power fell
short of it by 0.284 to 0.769.

On the probit scale the combined cell agreed with the benchmark. Under
E2 the departure was -0.047 to 0.028
in CO and Hybrid, within about two MCSE in every cell. In OL+BDC it was
0.031 to 0.092. These are the cells
where E2 was conservative. With the critical value calibrated to the
observed size, the OL+BDC departures were
-0.062 to 0.015, and all nine
departures were within two MCSE. Under the CR2 test, whose size was
near nominal, the uncalibrated probit departures were
-0.055 to 0.026.
The probit approximation ignores the lower rejection tail and the \(t\)
reference distribution, so agreement within a few hundredths is what
it can deliver.

\subsection{The equal-total line and carryover}\label{sec-eqsum}

The anti-diagonal of the grid holds three pairs with total weight
0.45: \((0.45, 0)\), \((0.225, 0.225)\) and \((0, 0.45)\). On drug they give
the same conditional slope. They differ in the off-drug profile only.
Table \ref{tab:eqsum} gives their power and the paired difference
between the two single-channel ends.

At \(t_{1/2} = 0\) the correlation end had slightly higher power than
the mean-shift end. The paired E2 differences, mean-shift minus
correlation, were -0.025 to -0.016. This is the
effect of the smaller conditional variance under the correlation
channel. At \(t_{1/2} = 1\) week the order reversed in Hybrid and
OL+BDC. The differences were 0.095 (0.009) and
0.181 (0.012). In CO the difference stayed near
zero, -0.002 (0.006). The mixed pair
\((0.225, 0.225)\) lay between the two ends, or within two paired MCSE
of the nearer end, in every design and half-life. Under E2cr2 the reversal at \(t_{1/2} = 1\) week was
0.060 (0.008) in Hybrid and
0.120 (0.010) in OL+BDC. Pairs with the
same total weight therefore do not give similar power once carryover
is present. The earlier draft stated that they did.

The loss of power from \(t_{1/2} = 0\) to 1 week shows the same
difference. Under E2, the mean-shift encoding at weight 0.45 lost
0.076 in Hybrid and
0.061 in OL+BDC. The correlation encoding
lost 0.188 and 0.267.
The MCSE of each loss is about 0.020. Under
E2cr2 the losses were 0.080 and
0.016 for the mean-shift encoding and
0.175 and 0.189
for the correlation encoding. Part of the E2 loss in OL+BDC reflects
the test's growing conservatism there. In CO all losses were small,
0.031 to
0.045 under E2. The correlation
channel's interaction decays with the drug effect. The mean-shift
channel's interaction stops at discontinuation, so the off-drug
visits keep their contrast with the on-drug visits. The earlier draft
stated that the two channels contribute comparably to carryover
sensitivity. In the Hybrid and OL+BDC designs the correlation channel
lost more than twice as much power under either test.

\rule{\textwidth}{0.4pt}\bigskip

\section{When to use the dual-channel architecture}\label{when-to-use-the-dual-channel-architecture}

The dual-channel DGP does not represent two biological mechanisms.
Within it, the two weights describe one conditional-mean slope and
how much of that slope persists after discontinuation. The mean-shift
channel's interaction stops at discontinuation. The correlation
channel's interaction decays at the carryover half-life. The ratio of
the weights fixes the off-drug profile of the interaction.

This gives the architecture a specific use. A simulation study
usually does not know whether the biomarker's influence on response
outlasts the drug. A sweep along the equal-total line,
\(\omega \in [0, 1]\) at a fixed total weight, shows how power depends
on that unknown profile. It holds the on-drug interaction fixed. At
\(t_{1/2} = 0\) the sweep is nearly flat, so it is informative only when
carryover is plausible.

The architecture is not a model of a drug that changes the spread of
response in high-biomarker participants. That question concerns the
second moment. It needs a variance function\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hedeker2008}{8}} or a
responder mixture\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper03}{7}}. In the prazosin-PTSD example,
the question is whether blood pressure predicts a larger prazosin
effect, and whether that predictive association fades after
discontinuation at the rate the drug effect does. The dual-channel
architecture can represent both answers to the second question. It
cannot represent heterogeneity of response variance.

As a default for a single power calculation, we recommend one of the
two encodings, with its weight stated. The correlation encoding is the
cautious choice when carryover is plausible. Its interaction decays
with the drug effect. In the Hybrid and OL+BDC designs its power loss
under carryover was more than twice that of the mean-shift encoding
(Section \ref{sec-eqsum}). A second free weight adds nothing to a power
calculation unless the off-drug profile is itself in question.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

The dual-channel DGP nests the two encodings of the biomarker-treatment
interaction. Under a normally distributed biomarker it is one Gaussian
model. Both weights set the conditional-mean slope of the
drug-response component on the biomarker. They differ in two respects
only. The correlation weight also lowers the on-drug residual
variance, by about 2 percent at the outcome level in this calibration.
The two weights also give different off-drug profiles. These facts
replace the earlier draft's account of two mechanisms acting on two
moments.

The fitted interaction coefficient is linear in the two weights. Its
coefficients are equal when there is no carryover and differ when
there is. The difference comes from the mean-shift channel. Its
interaction stops at discontinuation, but the analysis regressor
\(D_{bc,it}\) decays. The projection of its step-shaped profile onto the
regressor overshoots the nominal value. The correlation channel's
profile matches the regressor at the true half-life and gives the
nominal value exactly.

Power is not additive in the weights, but nothing about the channels
causes that. Additivity holds for the coefficient and, to a close
approximation, for the noncentrality. Excess over the
probability-scale benchmark is the curvature of the power function.
A single channel with its weight doubled shows the same excess. The
title of the earlier draft named a ``channel super-additivity''. We
withdraw that term.

The earlier run failed its own validation. Its boundary cells fell
10 to 19 points below the single-encoding power reported in an
earlier version of paper 01. The earlier draft attributed the gap to
mismatched calibration inputs. Those inputs were in fact identical in
the two drivers. The 2026-10-28 internal review traced most of the gap
to the analysis data. The package analysis that produced paper 01's
numbers adds the baseline visit. The combined driver did not. Adding
the baseline visit closed the gap in CO and OL+BDC to within one MCSE
in a 400-replicate check. In the Hybrid design a gap of 0.03 to 0.06
remained, 1.3 to 2.5 MCSE. We have not located its source. One
candidate is a difference between the package data generator and the
\texttt{implementations/tidyverse} generator the combined driver uses. We do
not compare with paper 01's numbers here. They come from a version of
paper 01 that has since been restated, under the earlier DGP. The
validation gate of Section \ref{sec-gate} instead compares each
boundary cell with the single-encoding DGP of the same driver, draw
for draw.

The study has limitations. The half-lives 0.5 and 1 week are
behavioral scenarios. At 0.2 week, inside the pharmacological range of
prazosin, the two channels were close to interchangeable, so the
differences reported here matter mainly for behavioral carryover. The
G9 arm covered 200 replicates of four cells per design and half-life,
so its comparisons are coarser than the main grid. The biomarker is
normal, so the two channels share the
conditional mean by construction. A skewed or discrete biomarker
would break the equivalence. We did not fit a specification that
models the off-drug slope separately, so we have not shown that the
off-drug profile is estimable in practice. The study is exploratory.

\section*{Glossary}\label{glossary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Glossary}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Dual-channel architecture.} The DGP of this paper, with both
  channel weights free. Data value \texttt{combined}. Formerly
  ``Architecture C''.
\item
  \textbf{Mean-shift encoding, mean-shift channel.} The interaction as a
  shift \(c_{bm,A}\, b_i\, \sigma_{BR}\) of the drug-response component
  at on-drug visits. Data value \texttt{mean\_moderation}. NOTATION.md calls
  it the mean-moderation architecture.
\item
  \textbf{Correlation encoding, correlation channel.} The interaction as a
  correlation \(c_{bm,B}\, g_t\) between the biomarker and the
  drug-response component, present on drug and decaying off drug.
  Data value \texttt{mvn}. NOTATION.md calls it the covariance-moderation
  architecture.
\item
  \textbf{\(c_{bm,A}\), \(c_{bm,B}\).} The mean-shift and correlation weights
  (code \texttt{c.bm\_a}, \texttt{c.bm\_b}). The subscripts are symbol labels only.
\item
  \textbf{\(\omega\).} Share of a fixed total weight assigned to the
  correlation channel.
\item
  \textbf{\(g_t\).} Correlation profile: the fraction of \(c_{bm,B}\) present
  at visit \(t\).
\item
  \textbf{\(D_{bc,it}\).} Exposure-decayed drug indicator of the analysis
  model (code \texttt{Dbc}).
\item
  \textbf{\(\kappa_A\), \(\kappa_B\).} Coefficients of the two weights in the
  population value of \(\beta_{bm:D}\) (Equation \eqref{eq:kappa}).
\item
  \textbf{\(\kappa\).} Calibration ratio: mean model-based standard error
  divided by the empirical standard deviation of the estimates.
  Values above 1 mean a conservative test.
\item
  \textbf{\(\Lambda\).} Noncentrality of the interaction test.
\item
  \textbf{Extended DGP.} The DGP of the companion paper\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-pmsimstats-paper01}{2}}: decaying biomarker-response correlation (0
  before first exposure), the same AR(1) correlation for every
  component with a constant between-component correlation at every
  lag, carryover that decays from the last on-drug mean, and a stop on
  a matrix that is not positive definite. Code label \texttt{refined}.
\item
  \textbf{Original carryover rule.} The drug-response mean at an off-drug
  visit is the previous visit's mean times the decay factor. The
  earlier run of this paper used it.
\item
  \textbf{Largest feasible correlation.} The largest \(c_{bm,B}\) for which
  every path's covariance matrix is positive definite.
\item
  \textbf{E2, E2cr2, G8, G9.} Analysis codes. E2 is the exposure-weighted
  interaction model with a model-based test. E2cr2 (G8) is the same
  fit with a CR2 test. G9 chooses the half-life by AIC and uses a CR2
  test.
\item
  \textbf{CO.} Two-period crossover.
\item
  \textbf{OL+BDC.} Open-label titration followed by blinded
  discontinuation.
\item
  \textbf{Hybrid.} Open-label titration, blinded discontinuation, then a
  brief crossover.
\item
  \textbf{Common random numbers.} The same random deviates used for every
  weight cell of a replicate.
\item
  \textbf{Probability-scale benchmark.} \(p(a, 0) + p(0, b) - p(0, 0)\).
\item
  \textbf{Probit benchmark.} \(\Phi(\hat\Lambda_a + \hat\Lambda_b - z)\)
  with \(\hat\Lambda = \Phi^{-1}(\hat\pi) + z\), where \(z\) is the
  critical value.
\end{itemize}

pass carry the suffixes \texttt{-hl02} and \texttt{-g9} and have their own \texttt{meta}
