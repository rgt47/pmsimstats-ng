\section{Abstract}\label{abstract}

\textbf{Background.} Aggregated N-of-1 designs can detect a treatment effect with fewer participants than a parallel-group trial. A fair comparison needs designs that estimate the same quantity and analyses whose behavior under the null has been checked.

\textbf{Methods.} We simulated the Hybrid design of Hendrickson et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendricksonOptimizing2020}{1}}, an alternating design, a two-period crossover and a parallel-group trial. All designs share a calendar of eight visits over 20 weeks. The drug-response asymptote was set for each design so that all designs estimate the same mean drug response on drug. We ran twelve one-axis sweeps. They cover effect size, carryover half-life, decay form, treatment heterogeneity, within-participant variability, number of periods, sample size, serial correlation, period length, biomarker correlation and misspecification. Each within-subject dataset was analyzed with a mixed model that built the exposure indicator at two or three assumed half-lives. The parallel group was analyzed by baseline-adjusted ANCOVA. Covariance matrices that were not positive definite were dropped, not repaired. We report bias, empirical and model standard errors, coverage and Monte Carlo standard errors for every cell.

\textbf{Results.} The results of the corrected sweeps are pending. They are generated from the stored artifacts when the sweeps have been rerun.

\textbf{Conclusions.} The study separates the cost of unmodeled carryover from the cost of carryover itself, and it compares designs on one estimand. The study is exploratory. Its conclusions about design ordering apply to the parameter values simulated and need confirmation in an independent study.

\textbf{Keywords.} N-of-1 trials; statistical power; carryover; serial correlation; sensitivity analysis; Monte Carlo simulation; crossover designs.

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Aggregated N-of-1 trials observe each participant on and off treatment and pool the participants in one model\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-lillie2011}{2},\citeproc{ref-duanSinglepatientNof1Trials2013}{3}}. Several simulation studies compare them with parallel-group and crossover trials. Blackston et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-blackstonComparisonAggregatedNof12019}{4}} simulated three cycles, four variance structures and constant additive carryover. Their parallel-group comparator used one observation per participant. Wang and Schork\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-wang2019}{5}} derived the power of a single participant under AR(1) correlation, period stacking, heteroscedasticity and washout. They had no parallel group. Percha et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-perchaDesigningRobustNof12019}{6}} varied the number of blocks, their order, the treatment duration and the sampling frequency under carryover and wash-in. Chen and Chen\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-chenComparisonFourMethods2014}{7}} compared compound-symmetry and AR(1) error structures and paired and mixed-model analyses. Araujo et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-araujoUnderstandingVariation2016}{8}} noted that such models omit the participant-by-treatment interaction. Senn\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-sennSampleSizeConsiderations2019}{9}} set out the variance components that determine the sample size of an N-of-1 series. Gärtner et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-gaertnerCarryover2023}{10}} compared analysis methods under carryover. Carrozzo et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-carrozzoTwoArm2025}{11}} compared a two-arm crossover trial with a meta-analysis of N-of-1 trials. Lin et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-lin2016}{12}} studied bootstrap tests for AB and ABAB series under autocorrelation. Tang and Landes\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-tangTtestsNof1Trials2020}{13}} studied t-tests under serial correlation. Shi and Ye\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-shiBehavioralCarryoverEffect2024}{14}} studied the power trade-off between crossover and parallel designs under behavioral carryover.

Hendrickson et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hendricksonOptimizing2020}{1}} proposed the Hybrid design for validating a predictive biomarker. It combines open-label titration, blinded discontinuation and a brief crossover. We did not find a study of its power for the treatment main effect in a data-generating process (DGP) that includes a placebo-response component, carryover and serial correlation, with the factors varied in turn. This paper reports one.

The paper makes four methodological choices that the 2026-05 version of this study lacked. First, the Hybrid is the published schedule, not a shorter two-path schedule. Second, all designs are put on one estimand, the mean drug response on drug, so that a difference in power is a difference between designs. Third, the analysis half-life is crossed with the data-generating half-life, so that the cost of unmodeled carryover is separate from the cost of carryover. Fourth, the parallel group is analyzed with a standard arm contrast whose null behavior we check before any comparison.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{Aims}\label{aims}

We follow the ADEMP structure of Morris et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morris2019simulation}{15}}. The study has three aims. The first is to describe the power of the Hybrid design for the treatment main effect as one factor at a time departs from a baseline. The second is to find the regimes in which the Hybrid has more or less power than the comparators on the same estimand. The third is to report Type I error, bias and standard-error calibration for every design. The study is exploratory. No analysis plan was fixed in advance, and the protocol (\texttt{00-ademp-protocol.md}) is a working document.

\subsection{Designs}\label{designs}

All designs have eight follow-up visits on the calendar of the Hybrid, at weeks 4, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 16 and 20, except where a sweep changes it. A baseline score is observed before treatment. Table \ref{tab:design-summary} lists the designs. Table \ref{tab:hybrid-paths} gives the drug state of the Hybrid at each visit.

The Hybrid has an open-label phase (weeks 4 and 8, expectancy \(\eta_t = 1\)), a blinded discontinuation phase (weeks 9 to 12) and a blinded crossover (weeks 16 and 20), with \(\eta_t = 0.5\) after week 8. Every path is on drug at week 9. The drug stops after week 9 in two paths and after week 10 in the other two. The crossover is drug first in two paths and placebo first in the other two. The schedule and paths are those of \texttt{docs/40}, Section 2, which were checked against the original code at commit 58b32a9.

The comparators are blinded throughout (\(\eta_t = 0.5\)). The alternating design switches the drug state at every visit and has two paths. The two-period crossover has one assessment at the end of each of two periods, at weeks 10 and 20. The parallel group has two arms, drug at every visit or never. The design \texttt{RCT-OL} is the parallel group with \(\eta_t = 1\) at every visit. It is the assumption of the 2026-05 version of this study, and it quantifies the effect of the comparator's blinding assumption. The design \texttt{Compact2} is the two-path schedule of eight weekly visits used in that version. It is not the published Hybrid and is kept only for S1.

\subsection{Data-generating process}\label{dgp}

The outcome is \(Y_{it} = \mathrm{BL}_i - (TV_{it} + PB_{it} + BR_{it})\). The baseline score \(\mathrm{BL}_i\) has mean 8 and SD 1.5. The three components are the natural-history component \(TV\), the placebo-response component \(PB\) and the drug-response component \(BR\). They are reductions in symptoms, so a drug effect is negative. Each component has a modified Gompertz mean
\[G(t; m, d, r) = m\,\frac{\exp(-d\,e^{-r t}) - e^{-d}}{1 - e^{-d}},\]
with asymptote \(m\), displacement \(d = 1\) and rate \(r = 0.3\) per week. The asymptotes are \(m = 0.5\) for \(TV\), \(1\) for \(PB\) and a design-specific value for \(BR\) (next section). The component SDs are 0.5, 0.5 and 1. The \(TV\) mean runs on calendar weeks, the \(PB\) mean on the time under positive expectancy scaled by \(\eta_t\), and the \(BR\) mean on the time on drug in the current run. The \(PB\) SD is also scaled by \(\eta_t\). A participant who is re-exposed starts the \(BR\) curve again from zero. Within each component, the correlation between visits \(s\) and \(t\) is \(\rho^{|w_s - w_t|}\) with \(\rho = 0.3\) per week. The correlation between components is 0.1 at the same visit and \(0.05\,\rho^{|w_s - w_t|}\) at different visits. The biomarker \(B_i\) has mean 120 and SD 15. Its correlation with \(BR\) at on-drug visits is \(c_{bm} = 0.3\) (the correlation encoding). The mean-shift encoding shifts \(BR\) at on-drug visits by \(c_{bm}\,b_i\,\sigma_{BR}\), with \(b_i\) the standardized biomarker. S10 compares the two encodings.

Carryover acts on the \(BR\) mean. At an off-drug visit after exposure, the mean is the previous visit's mean multiplied by \(\phi(t_{sd})\). The decay family \(\phi\) is exponential, \(e^{-\lambda t}\) with \(\lambda = \ln 2 / t_{1/2}\), linear, Weibull or power (Section \ref{sweeps}). The baseline half-life is 3 days. This rule adds the decayed mean of the previous visit, which already contains earlier carryover. Across consecutive off-drug visits it compounds, so the realized decay is faster than the nominal half-life implies (\texttt{docs/53}). The half-life axes are therefore nominal.

Treatment heterogeneity enters through \(\tau\). Each participant draws \(v_i \sim \mathcal N(0, \tau^2)\) once and the drug response at visit \(t\) is shifted by \(v_i \Psi_{it}\), with \(\Psi_{it}\) the mean drug response divided by its asymptote. The mean effect is unchanged. The baseline has \(\tau = 0\).

\textbf{Units.} We quote the carryover half-life in days, because the 3-day baseline matches the pharmacokinetic scale of the prazosin application. The companion papers on the biomarker interaction quote it in weeks on the grid \(\{0, 0.5, 1\}\). One week is seven days, so that grid is \(\{0, 3.5, 7\}\) days here. The 3-day baseline is \(3/7 \approx 0.43\) week. Visits are weekly in both conventions. Whether paper 05 should move to weeks is an open decision (Section \ref{openitems}).

\subsection{Estimand}\label{estimand}

The designs expose participants to the drug for different times, and the Gompertz response restarts at each re-exposure. A fixed asymptote \(m\) therefore gives a different mean effect in each design. The 2026-05 version compared designs at one asymptote. We define the realized estimand
\[\Delta_{\mathrm{on}} = -\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{on}}}\sum_{q=1}^{P}\sum_{t:\,D_{qt}=1}\mu_{BR,qt},\]
the mean of the drug-response mean \(\mu_{BR,qt}\) over the \(n_{\mathrm{on}}\) on-drug follow-up visits of the \(P\) paths. It depends on the design and on \(m\) only, because the carryover rule acts only at off-drug visits. It is linear in \(m\). In every sweep except S1 the asymptote \(m\) is set for each design so that \(\Delta_{\mathrm{on}}\) equals -0.5 outcome units. In S1, \(\Delta_{\mathrm{on}}\) is the swept axis. The stored value of \(\Delta_{\mathrm{on}}\) is in column \texttt{delta\_on} of every artifact.

The coefficient \(\beta_D\) that an analysis estimates need not equal \(\Delta_{\mathrm{on}}\). For the parallel group with the ANCOVA contrast it does. For the within-subject designs the model includes linear time and, in the Hybrid, expectancy, so \(\beta_D\) differs from \(\Delta_{\mathrm{on}}\) by an amount that depends on the design. We take as the target of bias and coverage the large-sample value of \(\beta_D\) for each analysis, the pooled fit to all simulated participants of a cell (column \texttt{ETbeta}). We report both the bias against this value and the difference from \(\Delta_{\mathrm{on}}\).

\subsection{Analysis models}\label{analysis-models}

The within-subject designs are fitted with \texttt{nlme::lme}\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-pinheirobates2000}{16},\citeproc{ref-verbekemolenberghs2000}{17}}. The model has a random intercept for the participant and a continuous-time AR(1) residual correlation, \texttt{corCAR1(form\ =\ \textasciitilde{}t\ \textbar{}\ ptID)}. The fixed part is
\[Y_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_B B_i + \beta_T w_t + \beta_D D_{bc,it} + \beta_\eta \eta_t + u_i + \varepsilon_{it}.\]
The term in \(\eta_t\) enters only when the design has more than one value of expectancy, which is the Hybrid. The baseline row (\(w = 0\), \(D = 0\), \(\eta = 0\)) is included. The exposure indicator is \(D_{bc,it} = 1\) on drug, \(2^{-t_{sd}/t^a_{1/2}}\) off drug after exposure and 0 before it. The assumed half-life \(t^a_{1/2}\) is a property of the analysis. It is 0 (binary indicator), 0.2 week (the project policy value when the half-life is fixed), or 3/7 week (the data-generating half-life). Which values a sweep uses is listed with the sweep. The target is \(\beta_D\). The stored column \texttt{fit\_formula} records the model actually fitted in every replicate. Table \ref{tab:formulas} lists the formulas.

The engine keeps it as \texttt{parallel\_analysis\ =\ \textquotesingle{}legacy\textquotesingle{}} only to reproduce stored runs. Section \ref{rctcheck} reports the check of both analyses.

\subsection{Performance measures}\label{performance-measures}

For each cell we report, with Monte Carlo standard errors (MCSE) after Morris et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morris2019simulation}{15}}, the rejection rate at \(\alpha = 0.05\) (power, or Type I error when the effect is zero), the bias \(\overline{\hat\beta_D} - \beta_D^{\infty}\) with \(\beta_D^{\infty}\) the large-sample value, the empirical SD of \(\hat\beta_D\), the mean model SE, the ratio \(\kappa\) of the two, the coverage of the 95\% interval around \(\beta_D^{\infty}\) and the share of fits that failed. A Type I error is called nominal when it lies within two MCSE of 0.05, with the MCSE computed at 0.05. The band is \(0.05 \pm 0.019\) at 500 replicates, \(\pm 0.014\) at 1,000 and \(\pm 0.010\) at 2,000. The MCSE of a power near 0.5 is 0.022 at 500 replicates and 0.011 at 2,000. Bias, SEs, \(\kappa\) and coverage for every cell are in \texttt{analysis/data/derived\_data/sensitivity/cell-summaries.csv}, written by \texttt{16-summarize-all.R}.

\subsection{Feasibility of the covariance matrix}\label{feasibility-of-the-covariance-matrix}

A cell is feasible when its covariance matrix is positive definite. The engine checks this for every path of every cell before the cell runs. It does not repair a matrix that fails the check. It drops the cell, and the artifact records the cell, the smallest eigenvalue of the correlation matrix and the reason. The column \texttt{pd\_repaired} is stored in every replicate row and is \texttt{FALSE} throughout. The 2026-05 version repaired such matrices silently. In S8 and S10, the cells that fail are therefore absent. Script \texttt{17-feasibility.R} computes the smallest eigenvalue for these two grids without simulation.

In S8, 2 of 15 design and correlation cells are infeasible: Hybrid at \(\rho\) = 0.9 (smallest eigenvalue -0.002) and Alt at \(\rho\) = 0.9 (smallest eigenvalue -0.068). In S10 under the correlation encoding, 6 of 24 cells are infeasible: \(c_{bm}\) = 0.6 for Hybrid, Alt, and RCT. The smallest eigenvalue of the feasible cells is 0.004. The same check runs inside every sweep, and the artifacts list the dropped cells.

S3 extends the carryover forms of the engine, and Gärtner et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-gaertnerCarryover2023}{10}} compared analysis methods under carryover but not decay forms. S4 and S5 follow the variance components of Senn\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-sennSampleSizeConsiderations2019}{9}} and Zucker et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-zucker2010}{18}}. S6 follows Percha et al.~S8 follows Wang and Schork and Lin et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-lin2016}{12}}. S12 follows Morris et al.

S3 uses the following decay families. Linear is \(\max(0, 1 - t/(2t_{1/2}))\). Weibull is \(\exp(-(\lambda_w t)^k)\) with \(\lambda_w = (\ln 2)^{1/k}/t_{1/2}\). Power is \(\max(0, 1 - t/(3t_{1/2}))^p\) with \(p = 1.5\). The multiplier equals 0.5 at \(t = t_{1/2}\) for the exponential, linear and Weibull forms. For the power form it equals \((2/3)^{1.5} = 0.544\).

S4 has two blocks. The first varies \(\tau\) at the baseline SD of the baseline score. The second varies that SD at \(\tau = 0\). The baseline SD is a random intercept that enters every visit equally, so it is not treatment heterogeneity. S5 multiplies the SDs of all three components, so the within-participant SD of the outcome scales with the multiplier. S6 fixes the trial at 16 weekly visits and varies the number of equal periods. The number of switches is the number of periods minus one. The \(BR\) response restarts in each period, and the asymptote is set so that \(\Delta_{\mathrm{on}}\) is the same in every design.

The sample-size sweep S7 keeps the allocation rule of the engine. The null calibration S12 sets the drug-response asymptote to zero and keeps \(c_{bm} = 0.3\). S12b refits the null data of the crossover and the alternating design without the serial-correlation structure. S14 is the check of the parallel-group analyses (Section \ref{rctcheck}).

\subsection{Relation to earlier work}\label{relation-to-earlier-work}

The sample-size comparison of Blackston et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-blackstonComparisonAggregatedNof12019}{4}} used a parallel group with one observation per participant. That comparator is unaffected by carryover by construction, and our parallel group has eight observations per participant, so the two are not the same comparison. Wang and Schork\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-wang2019}{5}} studied one participant with 400 observations and no parallel group. They found that serial correlation lowers the power of the within-subject design and that stacking periods mitigates the loss. Our S8 and S9 vary the correlation and the period length in a series of participants, with the effect held fixed across designs. Percha et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-perchaDesigningRobustNof12019}{6}} studied the number of blocks and the sampling frequency under carryover. S6 and S9 are closest to their work, and our designs differ in that the effect is matched across designs. Chen and Chen\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-chenComparisonFourMethods2014}{7}} and Araujo et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-araujoUnderstandingVariation2016}{8}} concern the error structure and the participant-by-treatment interaction. S4 adds that interaction to the DGP as \(\tau\). Gärtner et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-gaertnerCarryover2023}{10}} compared analysis methods under carryover. S2, S3 and S11 compare analysis half-lives under carryover and under a wrong decay form, which is a different question. Carrozzo et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-carrozzoTwoArm2025}{11}} compared a two-arm crossover trial, not a parallel-group trial, with a meta-analysis of N-of-1 trials.

The original DGP of Hendrickson et al.~holds the biomarker-response correlation constant after first exposure. The interaction estimand is then zero at a positive half-life (\texttt{docs/36}, \texttt{docs/40}). Interaction power at short half-lives in that setting is therefore a property of the original rule. It is not a result about the main effect, and this paper does not compare with it. The comparison of the two encodings of the interaction belongs to paper 01 of this series.

The standard texts on the analysis of two-period crossover trials are Jones and Kenward\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-jonesKenward2014}{19}} and Senn\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-senn2002}{20}}. A crossover with one assessment per period gives two follow-up observations per participant, and the baseline row makes three. S12b tests whether fitting \texttt{corCAR1} to these data changes the Type I error, by refitting without it.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{limitations}

The study is a simulation at one baseline. Its conclusions apply to the parameter values simulated.

The effect is the mean drug response on drug, matched across designs. A trial planner may instead hold the drug and dose fixed, in which case the designs differ in exposure and the comparison here does not apply.

The \texttt{lme} Wald tests at \(N = 35\) use containment degrees of freedom. A small-sample correction such as Kenward and Roger\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-kenwardRoger1997}{21}} or cluster-robust standard errors with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom may be needed. The cells report \(\kappa\) and coverage, which show where the model-based SE is miscalibrated. The corrections are not applied.

The parallel group has 17 or 18 participants per arm. Its ANCOVA uses the baseline score and the arm only.

The Hybrid is the only design with open-label visits. The comparators are blinded throughout, and the contrast of \texttt{RCT} with \texttt{RCT-OL} in S1 measures the effect of that choice for the parallel group only. The effect of an open-label phase on the alternating design is not measured.

S6 and S9 use the alternating design, because the Hybrid has a fixed schedule. Their implications for the Hybrid are inferences.

The carryover rule compounds across consecutive off-drug visits, so the half-life axes are nominal. A DGP in which the response does not restart from zero at re-exposure (\texttt{docs/38}) is not simulated. The study has no dropout and no missing data. The designs not evaluated are the single-participant N-of-1, the open-label-only design, multi-cycle designs with more than two periods per sequence, Latin-square designs and sequential designs\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-jiangDesignAnalysis2025}{22},\citeproc{ref-selukarFrameworkSequential2025}{23}}. The project whitepapers \texttt{docs/39} and \texttt{docs/40} report that more switches can lower the power of the interaction test under compound-symmetry correlation with carryover. This paper does not extend that result to the main effect, and it makes no claim of a bound for the unevaluated designs.

\subsection{Open items}\label{openitems}

Three decisions are open. The first is whether paper 05 should quote half-lives in weeks, to match papers 01 to 04. The second is the baseline effect of -0.5 outcome units, chosen because a local test with 200 replicates gave the Hybrid a power below 1.000 at this value, which the earlier baseline did not. The third is the author list on the title page.

\section{Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

The conclusions that do not depend on the rerun are methodological. A comparison of N-of-1 and parallel designs needs a common estimand, because a fixed response asymptote gives each design a different mean effect. The cost of unmodeled carryover needs the analysis half-life crossed with the data-generating half-life. A parallel-group comparator needs an analysis whose null behavior has been checked. Cells with a covariance matrix that is not positive definite need to be dropped or reported, not repaired. The conclusions about the ordering of designs are given in the Results once the corrected sweeps have been run.

\section{Glossary}\label{glossary}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Alt.} The alternating design: two paths that switch drug state at every visit.
\item
  \textbf{Analysis half-life.} The half-life \(t^a_{1/2}\) used to build the exposure indicator \(D_{bc}\) in the analysis. It need not equal the data-generating half-life.
\item
  \textbf{ANCOVA arm contrast.} The analysis of the parallel group: follow-up mean regressed on arm and baseline score.
\item
  \textbf{\texttt{br\_max}, \texttt{br\_sd}, \texttt{bl\_sd}, \texttt{pb\_max}, \texttt{tv\_max}.} Stored values of the drug-response asymptote and SD, the baseline-score SD, and the placebo-response and natural-history asymptotes.
\item
  \textbf{\texttt{c.bm}, \(c_{bm}\).} The correlation between the biomarker and the drug response at on-drug visits in the correlation encoding.
\item
  \textbf{Compact2.} The two-path schedule of eight weekly visits used in the 2026-05 version. It is not the published Hybrid.
\item
  \textbf{\texttt{delta\_on}, \(\Delta_{\mathrm{on}}\).} The realized estimand: the mean drug response over the on-drug follow-up visits of a design.
\item
  \textbf{\texttt{Dbc}, \(D_{bc}\).} The exposure indicator of the analysis: 1 on drug, decaying off drug after exposure, 0 before exposure.
\item
  \textbf{\texttt{ETbeta}.} The large-sample value of the estimated coefficient: the coefficient of a fit to all simulated participants of a cell.
\item
  \textbf{Feasibility.} A cell is feasible when its covariance matrix is positive definite. The engine drops a cell that is not.
\item
  \textbf{Hybrid.} The design of Hendrickson et al.: open-label titration, blinded discontinuation, then a brief blinded crossover. Four paths, eight visits at weeks 4, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 16 and 20.
\item
  \textbf{\(\kappa\).} The mean model-based standard error divided by the empirical SD of the estimates. A value of 1 means calibrated.
\item
  \textbf{\texttt{legacy}.} The mixed-model analysis of the parallel group used in the 2026-05 version.
\item
  \textbf{\texttt{mean\_moderation}.} The mean-shift encoding of the biomarker-by-drug interaction.
\item
  \textbf{\texttt{mvn}.} The correlation encoding of the interaction: the interaction is a correlation between the biomarker and the drug response that is present on drug and absent off drug.
\item
  \textbf{MCSE.} Monte Carlo standard error.
\item
  \textbf{\texttt{pd\_repaired}.} Stored flag, \texttt{TRUE} if a covariance matrix was repaired. It is \texttt{FALSE} for every stored replicate.
\item
  \textbf{RCT, RCT-OL.} The parallel group with blinded expectancy 0.5, and with open-label expectancy 1.
\item
  \textbf{\(\tau\).} The SD of participant-level heterogeneity of the drug effect.
\item
  \textbf{XO.} The two-period crossover with one assessment per period.
\end{itemize}
